#### 4

A plump red squirrel, giving its tail a triumphant flick, scurried briskly up the trunk of a gnarled stone pine and then, startled by something, darted sideways and vanished behind a shaggy branch, dropping a nut from its paws.

The nut struck the crown of Iolaus's head with a sharp crack as he stood beneath the tree, and he tipped his head back, listening to the squirrel's indignant chattering.

"Quiet, silly thing," Iolaus tossed at the irritated little creature. "Or someone will gobble you up, and there'll be no one left to chatter..."

Back at the pier, no sooner had he landed on Euboea, Iolaus decided he would present himself at Eurytus's palace a little later, closer to evening, once the day's commotion had died down. The Oechalian damates --- courtiers, the basileus's trusted men --- greeted the arriving suitors with entirely too much ceremony; everyone was anticipating, entirely too noisily, a grand feast, well seasoned with copious libations and no less copious boasting. The twins and Lichas, catching the general excitement, set off toward the palace along with the rest --- and never even noticed that halfway up the steeply climbing road Iolaus had dropped behind, looked around, and turned off to the right into the forest, forcing his way through the tangled web of low scrub.

Iolaus had prudently unloaded his baggage onto the brothers, sparing not even Lichas, who'd started to grumble about it --- so that now he could roam the forest unencumbered.

Silence pressed on his ears with soft palms. The occasional chittering of squirrels and the whistling of birds only underscored this tranquil stillness --- just as understanding the blueness of the sky requires the black speck of a hawk --- and Iolaus wandered aimlessly between the tree trunks, twirling a downy stalk between his fingers, drifting without noticing it first eastward, then northeastward.

Could it really be over?

Could this really be the end of twelve years of bloody drudgery, could it be that the dull brains of the Achaeans had finally sprouted the thought: "One must not kill each other upon altars, for how else could one tell a god's sanctuary from a beast's den?"; could it be that Heracles had completed --- no, not completed, but at least laid the foundation for something that no one, ever, would call a feat; could it be that one could go courting, could live without Dromoi and the Possessed, walk wherever one pleased, breathe without a rattle, listen without dread, hurry nowhere, suspect no one and nothing... write off the Mycenaean throne (though I, for one, gave that up long ago), stop fortifying Tiryns; pray without any second thoughts...

Probably not, he answered himself. One mustn't, and needn't, live two lives to understand that, but... he wanted it so very much.

He wanted, so very much, to live exactly like that.

"Rejoice! You wouldn't happen to be Iolaus, Heracles's driver and kinsman?"

Rubbing the crown of his head where the nut had struck him, Iolaus turned and saw who had spoken.

This man had no business standing here in the forest right now.

He was supposed to be receiving guests in the outer courtyard of Eurytus's palace.

"Well, youth? It's simple enough --- just answer 'yes' or 'no'!"

"It's simple," Iolaus murmured. "Yes or no --- it's simple... Rejoice as well, Iphitus the archer, son and heir of the basileus of Oechalia! O Moirai, how you've aged... and how much you look like your father now!"

Iphitus the archer looked at the strange young man with some surprise. Then he tugged at his narrow sleeveless vest --- for the occasion he'd had to dress up in the Mycenaean fashion, donning a ridiculous skirt that stood stiff as a board and this cursed embroidered vest --- and gave Iolaus an encouraging pat on the shoulder.

"Why now, youth? I looked like him before, too... though you couldn't know that, of course. We've never met before, have we?"

The sun splashed a handful of glowing dust through a gap in the branches, whitening Iolaus's curly beard for an instant with a ghostly gray --- and Iphitus the archer caught his breath, not even thinking to take his hand off the shoulder of the man standing before him.

"Are you Iolaus?" the Oechalian asked again, his voice gone hoarse... and broke off. "Grandson of Amphitryon the lawagetas?"

"I am Iolaus. Grandson."

"Strange, the way he talks," flashed through Iphitus's mind. "As if not to me, but to himself."

"I asked Alci... Heracles about you, but he hadn't noticed where you'd gone. But some boy hanging about said you'd turned off into the forest halfway up the road."

"And you went looking for me?"

A little beast of strange, unfounded suspicion stirred inside Iolaus, and its claws were sharper than the squirrel's had been.

"I'll box his ears when I get back," Iolaus silently promised Lichas.

"No," Iphitus the archer smirked, looking down at Iolaus from the height of his colossal stature. The prickly, distrustful guest was growing on the Oechalian more and more --- unlike the fussy, idle crowd of suitors, where every face looked the same.

Besides, the young man by the pine trunk reminded Iphitus of Thebes, of Cithaeron, of two restless twin boys, of their stern father, over whose head Iphitus had once shot an arrow, knowing the arrow's path in advance, because it could not have been otherwise...

That memory had already stirred once in the Oechalian today --- when Iphitus drew level with two suitors who'd fallen behind (he had thought, at the time, that they were suitors) and was about to walk past, but a quiet, doubled question struck him in the back like a hammer blow: "Teacher?..."

"No," Iphitus repeated, shaking off his stupor, "I did not go looking for you. Though I, Iphitus of Oechalia, who has lived almost half a century on this earth without noticing it pass, very much wanted to have a look at Heracles's driver, who has accompanied the great hero since childhood. I simply happened to go down to the harbor, have a word with my father's damates, and take the short way back. Would you care to walk together?"

The forest stirred around them, lazily arching its back, and the squirrel watched the departing men go, then shot down like lightning --- the nut wasn't going to waste, after all. And the men walked on and on, talking first of nothing in particular, then of everything at once, then Iolaus alone did the talking, while Iphitus the archer only kept turning his head and lighting secret little sparks in his eyes; Euboea's limestone mountain massifs, thickly overgrown with forest like the chest and shoulders of a seated giant, broke off steeply toward the sea --- and already visible on the cliff was the balustraded terrace of Eurytus's palace, quite close, within arm's reach if one had wings, though on foot it would mean a roundabout way along the little path...

"There's my father, standing there," Iphitus said, waving a hand toward the terrace.

Iolaus narrowed his eyes and did not immediately make out the people standing at the balustrade's railing. One of them, the tallest, with a snow-white shock of hair glittering in the sun, could only be Eurytus, basileus of Oechalia; recognizing the others --- if, of course, they were people he knew --- was out of the question.

For Iolaus, that is; but not for the Oechalian.

"To my father's left --- Hippocoon, a Spartan," Iphitus began listing them off, "nine or ten sons came along with him, I forget which..."

"Heard, haven't seen," Iolaus nodded, meaning either "I've heard of Hippocoon but never met him in person," or "I heard what Iphitus said, but I still can't see anything from here."

"Further left --- Neleus, the wanax of Pylos, who's brought a dozen sons..."

"A fox," Iolaus said curtly; then thought a moment and added: "An old fox."

"To the right --- Augeas, basileus of Eleia..."

Iolaus's face hardened, turned much older, harsher --- and Iphitus added hastily:

"My father has dedicated the contest for Iole's hand to Apollo... so any old scores --- not here."

Iolaus did not answer.

"People say," Iphitus the archer said cautiously, "that when Heracles was cleaning out Augeas's stables... well, he didn't mention he'd been sent by Eurystheus, and demanded payment. Is that true?"

"True," Iolaus snapped, jaw muscles working.

"And when the deception came to light --- Augeas drove Heracles out, and Eurystheus refused to count the deed."

"That's true too."

"Then what's there to be offended about? Wasn't it decreed --- labors without the help of gods and without payment from men..."

"Indeed," Iolaus grunted, calming down. "Shoveling out the privies of Hellas with your bare hands --- without the help of gods and without payment from men... labors, no doubt about it. Fine, forget it. Old scores --- later. And not here. Well, Iphitus, go on --- who's that standing next to honest Augeas the Eleian?"

"Laomedon, ruler of Troy..."

Iphitus faltered. The whole Achaean world knew that three or four years ago Heracles had saved Laomedon's daughter Hesione from a scaly sea monster, and that the high-born Trojan --- who, incidentally, had willingly offered up his own child as a sacrifice --- had shut himself up behind the city walls and, like Augeas, driven his benefactor away.

"A life without the help of gods and payment from men," the Oechalian thought. "A feat? Or penal servitude?..."

Lost in thought, Iphitus missed the moment when the terrace emptied.

"Where did they go?" he murmured, forgetting his companion for a moment. "And so quickly, too..."

"I don't know," Iolaus reminded him of his presence. "I only know that Alcides once promised that scoundrel Laomedon: 'Next time, I'll kill you.' And he --- I mean my uncle Heracles --- is a man of his word. If he promised, he'll deliver."

Without a word between them, the two men exchanged a glance and started up the path.

They walked fast.

Almost ran.

Far below them, sheltering from the salt winds behind the bulk of a rocky headland, foaming tongues of sea eagerly licked at a shimmering strip of sand and shingle; slanting sunbeams slashed open the blue-green flesh of the water, seething with breakers around the stone flanks jutting out here and there, wet and glossily gleaming --- but the men, two tiny figures on the path, had no time to spare for the doings of sea and sun and earth.

People were hurrying toward people.
