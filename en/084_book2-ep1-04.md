#### 4

A plump red squirrel, giving its tail a triumphant flick, scurried briskly up the trunk of a gnarled stone pine and then, startled by something, darted sideways and vanished behind a shaggy branch, dropping a nut from its paws.

The nut struck the crown of Iolaos's head with a sharp crack as he stood beneath the tree, and he tipped his head back, listening to the squirrel's indignant chattering.

"Quiet, silly thing," Iolaos chided the irritated little creature. "Or someone will gobble you up, and there'll be no one left to chatter..."

Back at the pier, the moment he had landed on Euboia, Iolaos had decided he would present himself at Eurytos's palace a little later, closer to evening, once the day's commotion had died down. The Oichalian damates---courtiers, the basileus's trusted men---greeted the arriving suitors with entirely too much ceremony; everyone was anticipating, entirely too noisily, a grand feast, well seasoned with copious libations and no less copious boasting. The twins and Lichas, catching the general excitement, set off toward the palace along with the rest---and never even noticed that halfway up the steeply climbing road Iolaos had dropped behind, looked around, and turned off to the right into the forest, forcing his way through the tangled web of low scrub.

Iolaos had prudently unloaded his baggage onto the brothers---sparing not even Lichas, who'd started to grumble---and could now roam the forest unencumbered.

Silence pressed on his ears with soft palms. The occasional chittering of squirrels and the whistling of birds only underscored this tranquil stillness---just as understanding the blueness of the sky requires the black speck of a hawk---and Iolaos wandered aimlessly between the tree trunks, twirling a downy stalk between his fingers, drifting, without noticing, first eastward and then northeastward.

Is it really over?

Is this really the end of twelve years of bloody drudgery, is it possible that the dull brains of the Achaians have finally sprouted the thought: "Men must not kill one another upon altars, for how then could one tell a god's altar from a beast's den?"; is it possible that Herakles has completed---no, not completed, but at least laid the foundation for something that no one, ever, will call a labor; is it possible that one can go courting, can live without Dromoi and the Possessed, walk wherever one pleases, breathe without a rattle, listen without dread, hurry nowhere, stop suspecting everyone and everything... write off the Mycenaean throne (though I, for one, gave that up long ago), stop fortifying Tiryns; pray without ulterior motives...

Probably not, he answered himself. One can't, and needn't, live two lives to understand that, but... how I want it.

How I want to live exactly like that.

"Rejoice! You wouldn't happen to be Iolaos, Herakles's driver and kinsman?"

Rubbing the crown of his head where the nut had struck him, Iolaos turned and saw who had spoken.

This man was not supposed to be standing here in the forest right now.

He was supposed to be receiving guests in the outer courtyard of Eurytos's palace.

"Well, young man? It's simple enough---just answer 'yes' or 'no'!"

"It's simple," Iolaos murmured. "Yes or no---it's simple... Rejoice as well, Iphitos the archer, son and heir of the basileus of Oichalia! O Moirai, how you've aged... and how much you look like your father now!"

Iphitos the archer looked at the strange young man with some surprise. Then he tugged at his narrow sleeveless vest---for the occasion he'd had to dress up in the Mycenaean fashion, donning a ridiculous skirt that stood stiff as a board and this cursed embroidered vest---and gave Iolaos an encouraging pat on the shoulder.

"Why now, young man? I looked like him before, too... though you couldn't know that, of course. We've never met, have we?"

The sun splashed a handful of glowing dust through a gap in the branches, whitening Iolaos's curly beard for an instant with a ghostly gray---and Iphitos the archer caught his breath, not even thinking to take his hand off the shoulder of the man standing before him.

"Are you Iolaos?" the Oichalian asked again, his voice gone hoarse... and broke off. "Grandson of Amphitryon the lawagetas?"

"I am Iolaos. Grandson."

[Strange, the way he talks,]{.thoughts} flashed through Iphitos's mind. [As if not to me, but to himself.]{.thoughts}

"I asked Alkei---Herakles about you, but he hadn't noticed where you'd gone. Still, some boy hanging around said you'd turned off into the forest halfway up the road."

"And you went looking for me?"

A little beast of strange, unfounded suspicion stirred inside Iolaos, and its claws were sharper than the squirrel's had been.

[I'll box his ears when I get back,]{.thoughts} Iolaos silently promised Lichas.

"No." Iphitos the archer grinned, looking down at Iolaos from the height of his colossal stature. The prickly, distrustful guest was growing on the Oichalian more and more---unlike the fussy, idle crowd of suitors, where every face looked the same.

Besides, the young man by the pine trunk reminded Iphitos of Thebes, of Kithairon, of two restless twin boys, of their stern father, over whose head Iphitos had once shot an arrow, knowing the arrow's path in advance, because it could not have been otherwise...

That memory had already stirred once in the Oichalian today---when Iphitos drew level with two suitors who'd fallen behind (he had thought, at the time, that they were suitors) and was about to walk past, but a quiet question in two voices struck him in the back like a hammer blow: "Teacher?..."

"No," Iphitos repeated, shaking off his stupor, "I did not go looking for you. Though I, Iphitos of Oichalia, who have lived quietly on this earth for almost half a century, very much wanted to have a look at Herakles's driver, who has accompanied the great hero since childhood. I simply happened to go down to the harbor, have a word with my father's damates, and take the short way back. Would you care to walk together?"

The forest stirred around them, lazily arching its back, and the squirrel watched the men walk away, then shot down like lightning---no sense letting a good nut go to waste. And the men walked on and on, talking first of nothing in particular, then of everything at once, then Iolaos alone did the talking, while Iphitos the archer only shook his head in wonder, secret little sparks kindling in his eyes; Euboia's limestone massifs, thickly wooded, fell away steeply to the sea like the chest and shoulders of a seated giant---and there on the cliff the balustraded terrace of Eurytos's palace is already in plain view, quite close, within a stone's throw if you have wings, but as it is, it's the long way round by the narrow path...

"There's my father, standing there," Iphitos said, waving a hand toward the terrace.

Iolaos narrowed his eyes and did not immediately make out the people standing at the balustrade's railing. One of them, the tallest, with a snow-white shock of hair glittering in the sun, could only be Eurytos, basileus of Oichalia; recognizing the others---assuming he knew them at all---was impossible.

For Iolaos, that is; but not for the Oichalian.

"To my father's left---Hippokoon, a Spartan," Iphitos began listing them off, "nine or ten sons came along with him, I forget which..."

"Heard, haven't seen." Iolaos nodded, meaning either "I've heard of Hippokoon but never met him in person," or "I heard what Iphitos said, but I still can't see anything from here."

"Farther left---Neleus, the wanax of Pylos, who's brought a dozen sons..."

"A fox," Iolaos said curtly; then thought a moment and added: "An old fox."

"To the right---Augeias, basileus of Elis..."

Iolaos's face hardened, turned much older, harsher---and Iphitos added hastily:

"My father has dedicated the contest for Iole's hand to Apollo... so any old scores---not here."

Iolaos did not answer.

"People say," Iphitos the archer said cautiously, "that when Herakles was cleaning out Augeias's stables... well, he didn't mention he'd been sent by Eurystheus, and demanded payment. Is that true?"

"True," Iolaos snapped, jaw muscles working.

"And when the deception came to light---Augeias drove Herakles out, and Eurystheus refused to count the deed."

"That's true too."

"Then what's there to be offended about? Wasn't it decreed---labors without the help of gods and without payment from men..."

"Indeed," Iolaos grunted, calming down. "Shoveling out the privies of Hellas with your bare hands---without the help of gods and without payment from men... labors, no doubt about it. Fine, forget it. Old scores---later. And not here. Well, Iphitos, go on---who's that standing next to honest Augeias the Eleian?"

"Laomedon, ruler of Troy..."

Iphitos faltered. The whole Achaian world knew that three or four years ago Herakles had saved Laomedon's daughter Hesione from a scaly sea monster, and that the high-born Trojan---who, incidentally, had willingly offered up his own child as a sacrifice---had shut himself up behind the city walls and, like Augeias, driven his benefactor away.

[A life without the help of gods and payment from men,]{.thoughts} the Oichalian thought. [A labor? Or penal servitude?...]{.thoughts}

Lost in thought, Iphitos missed the moment when the terrace emptied.

"Where did they go?" he murmured, forgetting his companion for a moment. "And so quickly, too..."

"I don't know," Iolaos said, reminding Iphitos he was still there. "I only know that Alkeides once promised that scoundrel Laomedon: 'Next time, I'll kill you.' And he---I mean my uncle Herakles---is a man of his word. If he promised, he'll deliver."

Without a word between them, the two men exchanged a glance and started up the path.

They walked fast.

Almost ran.

Far below them, sheltering from the salt winds behind the bulk of a rocky headland, foaming tongues of the sea eagerly licked at a shimmering strip of sand and shingle; slanting sunbeams slashed open the blue-green flesh of the water, seething with breakers around the stone flanks jutting out here and there, wet and glossily gleaming---but the men, two tiny figures on the path, had no time to spare for the doings of sea and sun and earth.

People were hurrying toward people.
