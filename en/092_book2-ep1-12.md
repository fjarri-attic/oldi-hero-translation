#### 12

The wind struck his chest like a wounded bird, lashed his cheeks with its wings, and slid down, spent, trembling at his feet---while Alkeides went on standing on the wall of Tiryns, staring blankly down at the dust swirling along the causeway, trailing off in a long tail almost to the very horizon.

Less than half a year had passed since the work was finished---the labors, he thought with a bitter smirk---since the hated service to Eurystheus had ended; the wounds had scarred over, making themselves felt only now and then---wounds of the body, and those the keen-eyed healers cannot see. And now, again: Lichas on the altar, the priest's spine snapping with a dry crunch, the frightened, hate-filled stare of basileus Eurytos...

Alkeides suddenly remembered, with startling clarity, the centaur Cheiron: how he'd looked on Pholoe, after the slaughter, staring in bewilderment at his left foreleg, grazed by a poisoned arrow. No---it wasn't Alkeides who had done that, but Pholos the reveler, the Possessed centaur... already dying, Pholos had contrived to pull the arrow steeped in Lernaian venom out of his own body and nick Cheiron with its point as he came running at the noise.

And ever since, on Pelion, the immortal centaur Cheiron, son of Kronos the Fallen, has been dying, and still cannot die.

It's very nearly ten years now.

And all of Hellas, as ever, blames it on mad Herakles.

"...Alkeides?" someone asked uncertainly behind him.

Alkeides had no doubt whose voice that was. Even from above he had recognized, in the unusually tall charioteer, his former teacher Iphitos the Oichalian, whose chariot, only a short while ago, had rolled slowly up the sloping ramp along the wall and vanished behind the heavy outer gates.

But turning, Alkeides saw before him a haggard old man with the eyes of a hunted animal.

Seeing Iphitos the archer like this was agonizing.

Now, if ever, was the time to be glad that neither Iolaos nor Iphikles was in Tiryns: Iolaos, having told the twins along the way about the Brotherhood of Salmoneus and the part the conspirators had assigned to Alkeides and Iphikles in the coming Gigantomachy, had left three days after their return. Said he was going to look for Autolykos. Why? As if it mattered... Iphikles, meanwhile, had taken to visiting the local physicians and folk healers, though he was in perfect health, and had since been seen more than once keeping company with the town's holy fools; and finally he had set off down the Dromoi for Athens---where, it was said, Asklepios himself, son of Apollo and an earthly god of healing, was staying at the time.

"You wouldn't happen to be here about those idiotic herds, would you?" Alkeides asked, just to say something, aware even as he spoke that it was nonsense. "Because Eurytos is trumpeting it to the whole world: Herakles the thief, Herakles the brigand---couldn't get Iole, so he drove off the horses instead..."

Iphitos the archer didn't answer.

The wind crept up to his sandals like a beaten dog and whined, spinning in circles.

"My father meant to kill you." The words tore loose from the Oichalian's cracked lips as though against his will. "I saw the bow... I saw it... I know. He was provoking you on purpose, Alkeides."

"Yes." Alkeides turned away, unable to look at his former teacher. "You're right. But what your father was plotting is far worse than simply shooting an angry Herakles out of jealousy."

"What could be worse?"

"The death of many innocents."

"Surely my father wouldn't---"

"No. I would have killed them. In a fit of madness."

"Madness?! But Hera---"

"Hera has nothing to do with it."

Alkeides felt himself being carried along, felt that he would not be able to stop until he had shared the heavy burden lying on his soul with someone...

And he fell silent only once he had told the man standing before him everything.

"So that's how it is," the Oichalian muttered, and Alkeides, wrung out like a rag, flinched at the cracked sound of his voice. "Yes---now I understand what my father meant when he remarked to Augeias in passing: 'Herakles is a failed attempt. Now we know what the Gigantes must be: invulnerable to the gods, and powerless against us.' Forgive me, Alkeides, but to the Possessed and the Fallen you really are a 'failed attempt'; perhaps to the Olympians too. That's why I came to you---to beg you to stop them."

"Stop whom, teacher? The Brotherhood of Salmoneus? The Fallen? The Olympians? Whom?!"

"No. Stop the Gigantes. Our... our children."

"Your children?!" Alkeides decided he must have misheard.

"Our children," the Oichalian repeated quietly, raising to Alkeides eyes wet with tears and full of inhuman suffering. "Mine, and those of Augeias's sister Molione, of Nestor, son of Neleus of Pylos, of Podarkes, son of Laomedon, of Phyleus Augeiad, and of others... And also"---Iphitos swallowed, his sharp Adam's apple jerking convulsively, like a piece of fruit caught in his throat---"also of the Gorgon sisters, Stheno and Euryale[\*](#fn-53){.noteref}, of the Three-Bodied Geryon, whom you killed, of his father Chrysaor the Golden Bow, and of the other descendants of the Fallen and the ancient Titans whom men have chosen to forget."

"You"---astonishment made it hard for Alkeides to speak---"you... you actually went through with it?!"

"And who asked us?!" Iphitos shouted hoarsely. "No one... asked... no one! Our fathers simply shoved us into the Dromoi" ("You know what that is?"---"I do." Alkeides nodded.) "and slammed the door shut behind us! And we didn't know how to open it ourselves... and still don't, not really. That's how it was, Herakles: the foolish children of cunning fathers ended up on the Phlegraian Fields..."

"And what about... the others? The Gorgons, Geryon... the rest? Were they shoved in too?!"

"No. They were persuaded. Persuaded of the need to continue their line, the need for an infusion of fresh blood---the way the Olympians do it. Because their children, born of each other, were born monsters, with the mind and habits of the Beast: to terrorize some Lerna or Nemea to death, extort human sacrifice, and settle into a stinking lair, until there came..."

"Some Cleaner," Alkeides finished harshly, without pain or irony---simply putting a period to it.

"Some Herakles," Iphitos snapped, and something in his voice, in his hardened face, in the cold narrowing of his eyes, recalled the man he had once been. "Because the gods wait it out, Herakles comes on his own, the Beast hunts or sleeps, and we---the sons and younger kinsmen of the Possessed---oh, heavens, we were simply used! Like breeding stock, dumb, soulless cattle, fit for one thing only---multiplying!"

The wind, rallying, crumpled Iphitos's words in its fist, tore them to shreds, and flung the scraps at Alkeides.

"It was frightening... at first it was frightening. Many couldn't bear it; four women died in childbirth. Molione lost her mind after giving birth to two conjoined idiots by the Three-Bodied one---they nearly tore her womb apart---but she wouldn't give up the children, she screamed, she bit... now they're in Elis, with Augeias. Sweet little Stheno simply tore Polybos the Lakonian apart, unable to hold herself back in the moment of her climax! I was lucky: the Leaper, her sister, turned out to be more restrained... Later they sent us more people, my sister Iole among them. I tried---"

"Iole?!"

"Yes. But my father took her back almost at once, realizing she wouldn't be able to bear it... though what Iole had already seen was enough---since then she's stopped speaking, and stopped feeling pain."

Alkeides remembered Iole the bride: leopards, blood, a scream---and a clot of icy, inhuman indifference on a litter.

"Four years," Iphitos mumbled, almost inaudibly, "four years, and then some... man is a strange creature! He's capable of getting used to anything, of adapting, of enduring... we even started to be drawn to one another, morbid attachments of a sort took hold... we even grew jealous! I thought I was beginning to understand them---the descendants of Titans who had not become monsters but had ceased to be marvels as well!... just an echo now, a reverberation of past greatness. They are lonely, Alkeides, immeasurably, unbelievably lonely upon a Gaia that has forgotten them. 'I'd sooner have Tartaros,' Stheno once told me. And she was right. Having left the world of the living behind, and yet not turned into mindless shades, they live as if half-asleep, walled off from everything in their own closed pocket worlds, sunk in illusions and memories... Meeting us was no less a shock for them than meeting them was for us; that's why, later, Geryon chose to die rather than let you through to one of the Phlegraian Dromoi."

"Then why didn't he just tell me?!" Alkeides shrugged, at a loss.

"Tell you? You?! To them, Herakles is a bloody specter, the terror of sleepless nights, the killer hero, Zeus's living thunderbolt! And by then, on Phlegra, children weren't just being born---they were surviving! Unusual, in some ways flawed---but children! Our children!---though the Possessed took them away almost at once, to raise them apart..."

The Oichalian seemed to be crying.

Suddenly he lurched toward Alkeides and seized the other's pharos with both hands, snapping the silver fibula shaped like an enamel butterfly.

"They killed them!" Iphitos shrieked hysterically, spraying spittle. "Killed them! They sacrificed them! They---"

"Sacrificed? Who's 'they'?! Who's 'them'?!" Alkeides whispered, going cold, and unobtrusively tried to steer the agitated Oichalian away from the edge of the wall.

He never heard an answer.

Only strange words rose unbidden in his mind: "I, Apollo Thyraios..."---and the smell of mold settled over Tiryns.
