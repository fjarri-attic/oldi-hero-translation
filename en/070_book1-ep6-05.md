#### 5

That evening Amphitryon woke, as always, half an hour before midnight, at the gentle touch of Alcmene, who was trying very hard not to wake her grandson.

He waited for his wife... his former wife, and now a grandmother, a thing he could not get used to --- in short, for Alcmene to withdraw and her footsteps to fade away in the gynaeceum, and then he sprang from the bed.

Slipping out of the house unnoticed was easy enough --- everyone, including the fumbling doorkeeper, was fast asleep. Climbing the wall around the courtyard was somewhat harder for a short six-year-old boy, but Amphitryon had done it many times before, and today, besides, he felt some particular surge of strength.

"Maybe it's because yesterday was my birthday --- the birthday of Amphitryon from Mycenae, and Alcmene made offerings for her husband who had gone to Hades?" Amphitryon thought. And he was astonished at how natural the idea seemed to him.

His feet carried him swiftly toward the palaestra, empty at this hour, while the thoughts kept grinding like millstones in the head of a boy who was nearly sixty years old.

Anax Hades had said that Alcides had had no fits since the Orchomenian War. Five years, then... and now Alcides, judging by his behavior, was on the edge of breaking. Something had to happen. Either Tartarus was about to start toying with him again, like a cat with a mouse --- and the long-awaited sacrifice would for a time dissolve Alcides's tormented mind in its blood --- or...

Or he would break on his own!

"There's no waiting any longer!" Amphitryon was thinking, while his body ran circuit after circuit on the track, jumped, threw stones (the discuses were locked away for the night), gradually filling his muscles with a healthy fatigue that would, in time, turn into suppleness and strength.

He was preparing the new body carefully and painstakingly.

He, Amphitryon the Exile, was at war again. Only this was not the familiar war of men, where everything was clear: here was life, and there was death, and a sword's reach separated the former from the latter. The immortals waged war differently --- here was life, and there was life, and no armies drew up on the plain, gleaming with breastplates and helmets...

But blood was shed in this war too.

The blood of mortals.

A trifle to both warring sides.

And he, Amphitryon the Exile, by the will of Ananke the Inevitability, found himself in one of the warring camps.

Gods, Titans, the Fallen... and I.

Funny?

No, not like that: gods, Titans, the Fallen... and we.

I and my sons --- that already makes "we."

And that's not funny anymore.

* * *

Palely shining Selene the Moon and star-eyed Argus gazed indifferently down at the small figure moving within the empty palaestra. Amphitryon did not know that this very night, Iphicles's fair-haired, quiet wife Asteropeia could not sleep; having risen from her bed, she wandered aimlessly about the house for a while, and then, already quite ready to lie down again, she looked in on the children's quarters, following her mother-in-law's example --- and found Iolaus's bed empty.

Her cries woke first the slave nurse, then the rest of the household, and soon the whole house was up and about, ablaze with the light of lamps and torches.

Iolaus was nowhere to be found.

When the thoroughly tired Amphitryon climbed back over the wall and made for his chambers, he found, to his own surprise, the entire family gathered in the megaron: a scowling Alcides, an agitated Iphicles, a tear-streaked Alcmene, an irritated Megara (though that was her usual state), a bewildered, blinking Asteropeia --- and even Licymnius, who lived apart from the household, and his wife Perimede, sighing silently in a corner.

"Should've sent for Creon, too!" Amphitryon grimaced, looking at the crowd of relatives. "How could they forget the basileus?!"

"Little Iolaus! Thank Zeus, you're alive!" Alcmene threw up her hands, rushing to her grandson, and her enormous shadow lurched frantically across the wall of the megaron.

"So where were you gallivanting, you little vagabond?!" Iphicles demanded sullenly, carefully hiding his relief that the night's alarms had ended well.

"At the palaestra," Amphitryon answered laconically.

"At the palaestra?" the still half-asleep Licymnius couldn't help blurting, turning sideways to the boy for no clear reason and hunching his head into his shoulders. "At night? What is there to do at night at the palaestra?!"

"To train."

"And you weren't afraid?"

"I wasn't," Amphitryon cut off, beginning to grow weary of this interrogation.

Iphicles narrowed his eyes thoughtfully, and the household fell silent without quite noticing it, looking questioningly at Iolaus's father.

"You've gotten strange somehow, son... no, the palaestra is a good thing, and you've grown stronger, taller, you eat enough for three! Only why not play with boys your own age, sleep at night like everyone else, and train at the palaestra by day?! You're such a quiet one, can't drag a word out of you... and we worry!"

Amphitryon kept a patient silence, living up to Iphicles's last words, and looked past his relatives.

"Perhaps you saw something?" Asteropeia asked with concern. "Or one of the gods appeared to you in a dream? Don't be shy, Iolaus, tell us!"

And it was then that Amphitryon made up his mind.

He saw no other way to make the Tartarus-Possessed take an interest in him --- in harmless little Iolaus, son of Iphicles and nephew of Alcides.

In him.

Bait.

A sacrifice.

"Someone appeared," he announced for everyone to hear. "Only I don't think it was a god."

"Then who?" --- Amphitryon didn't even notice whose voice had asked.

"I don't know. Sort of like a shade... it looked like you, Dad. And like Uncle Alcides. It said --- he said: 'I am your grandfather Amphitryon, son of Alcaeus the Mycenaean.'"

"You're lying!" Alcides breathed out, dropping the bronze pendant he had been absently turning in his fingers.

"That's just what he said --- that you wouldn't believe it. So he told me: tell them how our house burned, back when your father and his brother weren't even born yet, and grandma Alcmene was with child, and twisted her ankle, and nearly suffocated --- they barely pulled her out..."

"It happened," Alcmene whispered, turning pale.

"Or how she was attacked one night in an alley, coming back from Nausicaa's --- and I... that is, he fought off six men to save grandma. One of them, he said, he cut the throat of with the man's own knife..."

"It happened!" Alcmene stared at the boy with utterly wild eyes, and Amphitryon decided: enough.

"Did that really happen, mom?" Iphicles's voice sounded dull in the silence that had fallen.

"It happened. I never told you..."

"So it's true?" Alcides picked up the ill-fated pendant and hunched over, trying not to look at Iolaus.

"How is he... your grandfather?" Alcmene's eyes pleaded: "Say something, anything!" --- and Amphitryon couldn't bear it.

He looked away.

"He said --- it's not bad for him there. In that Hades. He lives on the Isles of the Blessed. Alone. No one bothers him. He's waiting for all of you to join him. But he warned that you shouldn't hurry --- he'll wait, he has plenty of time now. Too much, even."

"What else did he say?" Iphicles leaned forward entirely. "Anything else at all?!"

"He did," Amphitryon agreed readily. "Said the gods had sent him to me. To let me know that from now on I'll be going everywhere with dad and uncle Alcides. That they're about to start performing great feats" (hearing this, Alcides flinched and dropped the pendant again) "and I'll go with them... And for that I need to eat a lot and train well. So I'm trying."

"Is that all?" Alcides asked, after a pause.

"No, not all. He told me to eat more meat and flatbread, and to give great-uncle Licymnius goat's milk to drink. Three times a day. No, even four. Instead of wine. And also..."

Amphitryon glanced back quickly and made sure the door to the megaron stood slightly ajar. The servants were plainly eavesdropping, just as he had hoped, and Amphitryon was counting heavily on the maidservants' long tongues.

"And also grandfather said not to forget to make offerings to him. All kinds. And if I don't obey him, the same thing will happen to me as happened to Uncle Alcides. But he didn't say what that would be. Uncle Alcides, did you disobey grandfather when you were little? What happened to you?"

"I was sick," Alcides said sullenly.

"And now are you well?"

Alcides did not answer.
