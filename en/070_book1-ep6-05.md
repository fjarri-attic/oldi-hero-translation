#### 5

That evening Amphitryon woke, as always, half an hour before midnight, at the gentle touch of Alkmene, who was trying very hard not to wake her grandson.

He waited for his wife... his former wife, and now a grandmother, a thing he could not get used to---in short, for Alkmene to withdraw and her footsteps to fade away in the gynaikeion, and then he sprang from the bed.

Slipping out of the house unnoticed was easy enough---everyone, including the fumbling doorkeeper, was fast asleep. Climbing the wall around the courtyard was somewhat harder for a short six-year-old boy, but Amphitryon had done it many times before, and tonight, besides, he felt an unusual surge of strength.

[Maybe it's because yesterday was my birthday---the birthday of Amphitryon from Mycenae, and Alkmene made offerings to her husband, who has gone to Hades?]{.thoughts} Amphitryon thought. And he was astonished at how natural the idea seemed to him.

His feet carried him swiftly toward the palaestra, empty at this hour, while his thoughts kept grinding like millstones in the head of a boy who was nearly sixty years old.

Lord Hades said that Alkeides had had no fits since the Orchomenian War. Five years, then... and now Alkeides, judging by his behavior, is on the verge of breaking down. Something has to happen. Either Tartaros is about to take hold of him again, like a cat with a mouse---and the long-awaited sacrifice will for a time dissolve Alkeides's tormented mind in its blood---or...

Or he will break down on his own!

[There's no waiting any longer!]{.thoughts} Amphitryon was thinking, while his body ran circuit after circuit on the track, jumped, threw stones (the discuses were locked away for the night), gradually filling his muscles with a healthy fatigue that would, in time, turn into suppleness and strength.

He was preparing the new body carefully and painstakingly.

He, Amphitryon the Exile, was at war again. Only this was not the familiar war of men, where everything was clear: here was life, and there was death, and a sword's reach separated the former from the latter. The immortals waged war differently---here was life, and there was life, and no armies drew up on the plain, gleaming with breastplates and helmets...

But blood was shed in this war too.

The blood of mortals.

A trifle to both opposing sides.

And he, Amphitryon the Exile, by the will of Ananke the Inevitability, found himself in one of the warring camps.

Gods, Titans, the Fallen... and I.

Laughable?

No---rather: gods, Titans, the Fallen... and we.

My sons and I---that already makes "we."

And that's no laughing matter anymore.

Palely shining Selene the Moon and star-eyed Argos gazed indifferently down at the small figure moving within the empty palaestra. Amphitryon did not know that this very night, Iphikles's fair-haired, quiet wife Asteropeia could not sleep; having risen from her bed, she wandered aimlessly about the house for a while, and then, just as she was about to go back to bed, she looked in on the children's quarters, following her mother-in-law's example---and found Iolaos's bed empty.

Her cries woke first the slave nurse, then the rest of the household, and soon the whole house was on its feet, ablaze with the light of lamps and torches.

Iolaos was nowhere to be found.

When the thoroughly tired Amphitryon climbed back over the wall and made for his chambers, he found, to his surprise, the entire family gathered in the megaron: a scowling Alkeides, an agitated Iphikles, a tear-streaked Alkmene, an irritated Megara (though that was her usual state), a bewildered, blinking Asteropeia---and even Likymnios and his wife Perimede, who lived apart from the household, with Perimede sighing silently in a corner.

[Why not send for Kreon, too!]{.thoughts} Amphitryon grimaced, looking at the crowd of relatives. [How could they forget the basileus?!]{.thoughts}

"Iolie! Thank Zeus, you're alive!" Alkmene threw up her hands, rushing to her grandson, and her enormous shadow lurched frantically across the wall of the megaron.

"So where were you gallivanting, you little vagabond?!" Iphikles demanded sullenly, carefully hiding his relief that the night's alarms had ended well.

"At the palaestra," Amphitryon answered laconically.

"At the palaestra?" the still half-asleep Likymnios couldn't help blurting, turning sideways to the boy for no clear reason and drawing his head down into his shoulders. "At night? What is there to do at night at the palaestra?!"

"To train."

"And you weren't afraid?"

"I wasn't," Amphitryon said curtly, beginning to grow weary of this interrogation.

Iphikles narrowed his eyes thoughtfully, and the household fell silent without quite noticing it, looking questioningly at Iolaos's father.

"You've been acting strange lately, son... no, the palaestra is a good thing, and you've grown stronger, taller, you eat enough for three! But why not play with boys your own age, sleep at night like everyone else, and train at the palaestra by day?! You're such a quiet one, can't drag a word out of you... and we worry!"

Amphitryon kept a patient silence, bearing out Iphikles's last words, and looked past his relatives.

"Maybe you had a vision?" Asteropeia asked with concern. "Or one of the gods appeared to you in a dream? Don't be shy, Iolaos, tell us!"

And it was then that Amphitryon made up his mind.

He saw no other way to make the Tartaros-Possessed take an interest in him---in harmless little Iolaos, son of Iphikles and nephew of Alkeides.

In him.

Bait.

A sacrifice.

"Someone appeared," he announced for everyone to hear. "But I don't think it was a god."

"Then who?" Amphitryon didn't even notice whose voice had asked.

"I don't know. Sort of like a shade... it looked like you, dad. And like uncle Alkeides. It said---he said: 'I am your grandfather Amphitryon, son of Alkaios the Mycenaean.'"

"You're lying!" Alkeides breathed out, dropping the bronze pendant he had been absently turning in his fingers.

"That's just what he said---that you wouldn't believe it. So he told me: tell them how our house burned, back when your father and his brother weren't even born yet, and grandma Alkmene was with child, and twisted her ankle, and nearly suffocated---they barely pulled her out..."

"It happened," Alkmene whispered, turning pale.

"Or how she was attacked one night in an alley, coming back from Nausikaa's---and I... that is, he fought off six men to save grandma. He said he slit one man's throat with that man's own knife..."

"It happened!" Alkmene stared at the boy with utterly wild eyes, and Amphitryon decided: enough.

"Did that really happen, mom?" Iphikles's voice sounded dull in the silence that had fallen.

"It happened. I never told either of you..."

"So it's true?" Alkeides picked up the ill-fated pendant and hunched over, trying not to look at Iolaos.

"How is he... your grandfather?" Alkmene's eyes pleaded: "Say something, anything!"---and Amphitryon couldn't bear it.

He looked away.

"He said he's doing all right there. In that Hades. He lives on the Isles of the Blessed. Alone. No one bothers him. He's waiting for all of you to join him. But he warned you not to hurry---he'll wait, he has plenty of time now. Too much, even."

"What else did he say?" Iphikles leaned forward eagerly. "Anything else at all?!"

"He did," Amphitryon agreed readily. "Said the gods had sent him to me. To let me know that from now on I'll be going everywhere with dad and uncle Alkeides. That they're about to start performing great feats"---hearing this, Alkeides flinched and dropped the pendant again---"and I'll go with them... And for that I need to eat a lot and train well. So I'm trying."

"Is that all?" Alkeides asked, after a pause.

"No, not all. He told me to eat more meat and flatbread, and to give great-uncle Likymnios goat's milk to drink. Three times a day. No, make that four. Instead of wine. And also..."

Amphitryon glanced back quickly and made sure the door to the megaron stood slightly ajar. The servants were plainly eavesdropping, just as he had hoped, and Amphitryon was counting heavily on the maidservants' loose tongues.

"And also grandfather said not to forget to make offerings to him. All kinds. And if I don't obey him, the same thing will happen to me as happened to uncle Alkeides. But he didn't say what that would be. Uncle Alkeides, did you disobey grandfather when you were little? What happened to you?"

"I was sick," Alkeides said sullenly.

"And are you better now?"

Alkeides did not answer.
