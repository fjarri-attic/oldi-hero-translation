#### 9

Oblivion refused to let Alkeides go, rocking him with an insistent, monotonous croon, swaddling him in darkness and silence, bouncing him in soft arms like a plump, good-natured mother trying to settle a fractious child; but the child stubbornly refused to sleep, flailing its little arms and legs, thrashing about---and oblivion, sighing, went on its way.

Warm.

Dry.

Either there are no suspicious rustlings, or they are hiding themselves very well.

And it takes no sage to guess, even with eyes still closed, that since the last thing Alkeides remembers---thunder, lightning, a downpour, stones and javelins---a good deal has happened.

So it is time to sit up and look around.

He sat up and looked around, feeling in his head a painful surf breaking against the shores of accursed Kos.

"Cheiron?" Alkeides called softly, already understanding he was mistaken, that this was not the cave on Pelion where he had so often been a guest of the wise centaur, that it was just as quiet and snug here, but did not smell of dried herbs and roots; and besides, Cheiron would have found it rather cramped.

"Come to, have you?" a solicitous old woman's voice inquired from behind him. "You just lie there, dearie, lying's better than leaping about... and Cheiron's not here, Cheiron's far away, in Thessaly. What would he be doing hanging around here, that four-legged know-it-all?"

Turning, Alkeides found in the corner of the cave a hunched, hook-nosed old crone with startlingly clear blue eyes---a combination strange enough in itself to give one pause.

"Was it"---the words came with difficulty, barely breaking through the booming surf inside his skull---"was it you who saved me?"

The old woman batted her thick, girlish lashes.

"As if I've nothing better to do than pull strapping lads out of the crap." She giggled, fishing Alkeides's clothes out from somewhere and deftly tossing them into his lap. "Oh sure, I lie awake dreaming of it, hauling some big lug like you on my back! No, it was the little one who dragged you here---soon as there's trouble, he remembers Crete, and in good times he doesn't so much as show his face, the scamp..."

"The little one?"

"To me, to old Dikte[\*](#fn-72){.noteref}, he's the little one; to everyone else---Dias the Father, Zeus the High-Thundering! Got it?"

Alkeides nodded, getting dressed. His chiton turned out to be freshly washed and still slightly damp, his battle sandals with their bronze studs on leather straps had been scrubbed clean of Kos's mud, and the belt wasn't even his.

"My thanks for shelter and kindness, goddess Dikte," he said, rising, and bowed low to the old woman.

"Goddess?" she said, surprised. "No, I'm one of the first, one of the Ouranids---of the Titans' own stock... Never mind that I look old---old is just for your benefit, to keep us clear of sin!"

"What sin would that be?" Alkeides smirked.

"The main one." The old woman scorched him with the blue flame of a youthful gaze. "When Zeus brought you here, I took one look: a fine-looking man, young, and I thought, soon as he comes round he'll start pestering me---you need your rest, and I'd wear you out to death! So I changed my shape, for safety's sake... And then Zeus started carrying on over you: however, he says, can we go to Phlegra without Herakles?! The Family's locked in battle with the Gigantes, and here's our chief ally lying out cold!"

Alkeides started.

The cave suddenly seemed a trap, a cunning snare with no way out.

"There you lay," Dikte went on meanwhile, "the little one all but in tears, when in flies this slip of a girl, spear and all, near put my eye out with it! Athena Promachos, if you please... And straight off she starts telling her father: Herakles took Kos, killed Eurypylos the basileus, so you were saving the wrong one, pops! Well, the little one smacks his forehead, grabs his daughter by the scruff, and off the two of them go, like mad... and you they left with me. Patch him up, they said, and then he can clear off wherever he likes!"

Dikte fell abruptly silent.

And almost at once a tall warrior stepped into the cave, pulling off his full-face helmet with its horsehair crest.

The silver lions on the visitor's greaves bared their teeth at the old woman; a single gray lock fell across the warrior's eyes, and, brushing it aside, he looked straight at Alkeides, who had gone still.

"Do you remember me?" the question rang out, clear and resonant.

"Yes." Alkeides stretched with deliberate slowness, as though after a long sleep. "I remember you, Ares Enyalios. Once, I refused to be your driver."

"But today I've come to hire on as your guide." Ares drew a sword with an ivory hilt from its sheath and tossed it to Alkeides, who caught it in midair.

"To Phlegra?" Alkeides asked quietly.

"To Phlegra."

"And are you certain, Enyalios, that I'll go fight for the Family?"

"I am. I suspect you have weighty reasons of your own for taking part in the Gigantomachy, apart from the Family; but one of them I know for certain."

"Which one?"

"Your brother is already at Phlegra."

And Ares walked out of the cave, without even glancing back to see whether Herakles was following him.
