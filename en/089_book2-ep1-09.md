#### 9

Steam curled in hot little streams above the glazed clay, and Iolaos sank into the water with relish, leaning his back against the smooth ceramic tiles that lined the tub.

Eurytos's bathhouse pleasantly surprised him---unlike the rest of the palace's buildings and chambers, which bore the mark, if not of poverty, then of neglect and decay, here everything was clean and cozy; and in the adjoining alcove two seventeen-year-old girls were already bustling about, having thrown linen towels around their hips at the sight of Iolaos.

One of them---a blue-eyed slip of a girl with luxuriant fair hair gathered into a knot at the back---was diligently hauling water with a lekythos[^50] from an enormous bronze cauldron standing on three hoof-tipped legs, filling the second tub and glancing sidelong at the basileus standing nearby; the other, a black-haired, full-bosomed native of Thessaly (only the fertile Vale of Tempe bred women like her, seemingly destined almost from birth for love and motherhood), was keeping an even fire going in the hearth-hollows beneath the cauldron.

[^50]: A ladle.

[She looks like Alkmene,]{.thoughts} very nearly burst from Iolaos, and he turned away, trying not to look at the Thessalian---but the memory rose unbidden: Tiryns, and along the ramp, into the narrow passage between the towers leading to the fortress gates, a chariot turning in, bearing an old man and an old woman.

The old man was the great Cretan Rhadamanthys, whose law had once, like a shield raised just in time, sheltered the thirteen-year-old twins; the old woman was Alkmene, Herakles's mother, the woman whom the late lawagetas Amphitryon Perseid had, long ago, shared with a god.

The late lawagetas Amphitryon Perseid.

"Like what you see?" Eurytos asked cheerfully, catching Iolaos's glance. "An enviable bride! Laodameia, daughter of Akastos..."

"Akastos?" Iolaos half rose, gripping the sides of the tub. "Akastos, the Argonaut, the present basileus of Iolkos?! Aren't... aren't these girls slaves?!"

And he looked at Laodameia differently now, flushed as she was---whether from Eurytos's words or the heat of the hearth.

He realized the rudeness of his own question only belatedly.

"Slaves?" the basileus of Oichalia burst out laughing, slapping his sinewy, hairless thighs with a resounding smack. "Do you know, young man, that suitors are loosing arrows out on the field over this very 'slave'?"---Eurytos fondly tugged the skinny blue-eyed girl by the hair. "Well then, Iole, my daughter---are you happy?"

Iole nodded silently and stepped away from the now-filled tub.

Watching Eurytos settle himself noisily into his bath---the very picture of Poseidon in some bay---Iolaos reflected that hundreds of times he had come across the custom by which the women of a household serve a guest during his ablutions, but never once had he seen a daughter help her own father bathe, and in front of strangers, at that.

Well, it seemed the years had not tamed Eurytos's habit of scandalizing those around him.

"You don't look like a stripling, Iolaos," the basileus said, snorting the water from his face. "Not in body, that is. And I don't mean the number of scars, either... You put me more in mind of your kinsmen---as if you were not a son and nephew, but a third brother. And not that much younger, at that."

"You don't look like a decrepit old man either, Eurytos the marksman," Iolaos tried to put something more than mere politeness into the words. "Only... your daughter and her friend, are they deaf-mute, by any chance?"

"No," Eurytos said. His wet hair was plastered to his face, and the "no" came out muffled and toneless.

"Then, in your place, I'd send them away."

"Why?"

"You see, back there in the megaron, you were talking with a shrewd, ambitious young man of good family, who was supposed to be thrilled at the chance to converse with such distinguished rulers---well, of course, calling him one of their own!---and to jump for joy at the rosy hints about a wanax position in Mycenae. Isn't that so, basileus?"

"Well... more or less," Eurytos smirked, adding Cyprian perfumes to the water.

Iolaos followed his example.

"And---naturally---you didn't tell such a young man everything. But I want to know everything."

"You want to know more?" The basileus stretched out a leg, and Iole, hurrying over, set to trimming his flat, pallid toenails with a curved bronze knife.

"No, basileus. I want to know everything."

"Aren't you rather bold, even for Herakles's driver?" Eurytos inquired.

"For Iolaos, Herakles's driver---yes... Possessed one."

Having said this, Iolaos ducked his head under the water and lay there for a while, holding his breath and relaxing, like an athlete after his first race, gathering himself for the second.

When he surfaced, the girls were no longer there.

The fire still burned beneath the cauldron, steam still curled above the ceramic tiles, and Eurytos the marksman was staring straight at him with the look an archer gives his target.

[And those fellows out there on the field think they're the ones competing with Eurytos!]{.thoughts} thought Iolaos, casually rubbing scented oil into his shoulders.

"For Iolaos," he repeated once more, "for Herakles's driver---yes. But not for Amphitryon the Exile, son of Alcaeus, grandson of Perseus the Gorgon-Slayer. Not for one returned from the depths of Erebos. You acted wisely, sending the girls away, Eurytos."

"Proof?" The first arrow flew from the basileus's thin, bloodless lips.

[A miss,]{.thoughts} Iolaos smirked to himself.

"As much as you like. Take this, for instance: if I threw an apple at the door right now, could you, Eurytos, split it by throwing a second apple after it? That's exactly what once happened in Thebes, twenty-eight years ago---except it was you who threw the first apple, and your son Iphitos the second."

"More."

And the second arrow, too, went wide of its mark.

When more proof is demanded, it means one is already half-persuaded. Or at least allowing for the possibility.

"The contest between Iphitos and Myrtilos the Theban. It ended in a tie, and the next morning proud Myrtilos, fulfilling the condition you yourself had set, offered himself up in sacrifice to Alkeides, the future Herakles---my son."

"Enough. Even if what you're saying were true, Amphitryon the lawagetas could not have known about the condition I supposedly set for Myrtilos."

"Alive---he couldn't have," Iolaos said, and ducked his head under the water again, giving Eurytos time to turn the last words over in his mind. "Alive, he couldn't have. But a fugitive from the crimson gloom of Hades, who killed the soul of his own grandson Iolaos to seize his body?" (the words cost him dear, for there was less lie in them than truth) "What if Amphitryon the lawagetas, in the body of a six-year-old boy, had managed to interest Galinthias, daughter of Proitos, in himself? A pity the old woman died at such an inopportune moment..."

"How... how did she die?!" Eurytos lurched forward, water splashing everywhere.

Iolaos, straining with all his might to look unruffled, did not answer for a long while.

"The old woman's much-vaunted instinct failed her," he said at last, a touch carelessly. "She rashly decided that Thebes was too small for two such as us... and she was right. Galinthias simply left me no choice, Eurytos."

Eurytos stared up at the ceiling in silence.

Iolaos raised his eyes---no, no cracks snaked across this ceiling; it was clean and smooth, like a shard of the Moirai on which no lot had yet been marked.

"But you're not one of the Possessed, Amphitryon," the basileus's words rang out.

"No." The old name suddenly felt alien to Iolaos. "But you too, Eurytos, are possessed by Tartaros in a different way than the late Galinthias."

"True. The Fallen are allies to me, not masters. And you, Amphitryon---you too suit me far better as a seasoned ally than as a will-less puppet named Iolaos. You understand, don't you, that in the event of betrayal... even my influence wouldn't be enough to shield you, or those close to you, from the reckoning."

"You've believed me, Eurytos," Iolaos smirked venomously. "Otherwise you'd never have started threatening. I wonder, what exactly do you hope to frighten me with?"

"I'm not trying to frighten you at all, lawagetas. I simply need to get used to the idea that you're not the man whose body... you're wearing; and still less the man who'd rush off to some temple to inform on us to the gods, crying blasphemy, or go flapping his tongue in the marketplaces. Though I'm growing talkative, and that ill becomes the head of the Brotherhood of Salmoneus."

Iolaos had expected anything but these words. Yes, of course, he had heard the story of the Elean ruler Salmoneus the Madman, who had proclaimed himself Zeus, taken to banging bronze basins together and hurling lit torches into the air, assuring everyone that this was thunder and lightning; and who had finally taken to claiming for himself the offerings due the Thunderer, after which an enraged Zeus---the real one---had burned the madman to ashes, along with his entire city.

The Brotherhood of Salmoneus?

An echo of madness from Perseus's day?! But if it was truly madness---why hadn't Zeus simply let people laugh their fill at Salmoneus, instead of destroying innocent townsfolk along with the heretic basileus and their city?

"Most of the brothers are here right now, in Oichalia," Eurytos went on. "I trust I won't have to formally break my oath by naming them to you?"

"You won't have to," Iolaos nodded.

His thoughts, meanwhile, were taking a very different turn. Bald Augeias---why, he rules Elis, the rebuilt seat of the rebellious Salmoneus! The Spartan Hippokoon---that one is still unclear... but Neleus of Pylos is Salmoneus the Madman's grandson, through his mother, the Thessalian Tyro!

And Laomedon the Trojan---isn't he the one who spreads the rumors about himself: that Phoibos and Poseidon raised his city's unbreakable walls, and that he afterward drove the gods off, even threatening (when they demanded payment) to cut off their ears and sell them as slaves!

A rebellion against the Olympians?!

Lost in thought, Iolaos let Eurytos's last words slip past his ears unheard.

Catching himself, he shook his head, pretending water had gotten into his ear.

The basileus of Oichalia watched these antics with mockery in his eyes.

"That's exactly how the gods are," Eurytos scooped up a handful of fragrant foam and abruptly clenched his fist, "---like water in the ear: dulls your hearing, and you can't shake it out... But you should. It's high time we took the reins of true power into our own hands; time we ruled over men."

Then he thought a moment and corrected himself:

"We---men ourselves."

"That is, Salmoneus's brethren?" Iolaos took a bone awl from a ledge on the wall and toyed with it; the slender point obediently pricked out between his fingers and vanished again.

"That is."

"I see. All that's left is the small matter of tearing down Olympos. You're not Gigantes, by any chance? Son of a bitch Augeias, especially? How do rhapsodes sing it? 'Wild is the tribe of the Gigantes, begotten of Tartaros on Mother Gaia; nurslings of secretest depths, that fostered the ruin of Olympian godheads---O bronze-necked, O serpent-armed, O manifold-shapen...' Bronze-browed too, if I recall. Well, how's that?"

"Witty," Eurytos allowed, winding a long lock of hair around his finger, wet now and gone from snow-white to something murky. "No, Amphitryon, we are not the Gigantes. And that bit about the depths of Gaia---nonsense. The Gigantes..."

He suddenly turned very serious, and a strange spasm crumpled and twisted the basileus's face.

"The Gigantes are our children. Ours, and the Fallen's. No---not children, grandchildren. But the rest of it---about the ruin of the Olympians---that's true."

For an instant the water turned unbearably cold.

"But surely... the Fallen are in Tartaros!" Iolaos barely managed to force out. "And the ones on earth are monsters! They don't even look human!..."

"Not all of them. The Olympians never got around to some of them---or rather, some of their descendants, more or less human-like. Your Herakles, and Perseus, and Bellerophon, and other... Cleaners never did. There were three Gorgons, for instance; two of them---Stheno and Euryale---are still alive to this day. As for Medusa, she had two sons; the Olympians bridled Pegasos almost at once, which is more than can be said for Chrysaor the Golden Bow and his son by the Oceanid Kallirrhoe, Three-Bodied Geryon. Poor boy! His death was a great blow to us..."

Iolaos remembered the twins' account, back from Erytheia[^51] with Geryon's famous cattle in tow---the accursed beasts had scattered the whole way, and at best half of them had made it to Mycenae---of the battle with the three-bodied lord of Erytheia and his henchmen.

[^51]: Erytheia---the purple island of the Sunset, past the Ocean-stream; home to the three-bodied giant Geryon, grandson of Medusa the Gorgon, whose herds were tended by the Gigas Eurytion (Eurytion---Greek for "kinsman of Eurytos") and the two-headed hound Orthros.

Poor boy?!

"You think, Amphitryon," Eurytos went on, "that Geryon fought your Herakles over cattle? No---he shielded a Dromos leading to Phlegrae, the cradle of the Gigantes, with himself. Rest in peace... In short, if the Olympians go on breeding their bastard heroes wholesale---forgive me, dear Amphitryon, but it's the truth---then why shouldn't our children pair off with the descendants of the Fallen? Believe me, it wasn't always pleasant, and not all the offspring survived; nor did all the parents. But... now, at least, the Gigantes exist somewhere besides the songs of florid rhapsodes. They're on Phlegrae---and in the Olympians' worst nightmares."

"Let's say," Iolaos muttered hoarsely, "let's say the gods do perish in the Gigantomachy. What good does that do the Fallen, if they remain in Tartaros?"

"None. In any case, that's exactly what I'd very much like to happen. Let them enjoy their revenge... without ever leaving the brazen bounds of the underworld."

"And the Gigantes themselves?"

"You disappoint me, Amphitryon the lawagetas. The Olympians are counting on Herakles's help in the coming battle; and I needn't explain to you what a man of one's own means in the enemy's camp! To miss the mark once or twice, hesitate, lose his nerve... twist an ankle, even!---and the Gigantes will fulfill their purpose. But afterward, once he's himself again, Herakles will take his due... and there will be no more Gigantes."

[There they are, Hermeias's troubles,]{.thoughts} Iolaos realized. [And the Trickster doesn't even know about some of them.]{.thoughts}

"Zeus's son will never go along with that," he cut in firmly, remembering just in time what was known about Herakles by everyone, Eurytos included.

"No need for him to. He has a brother---and such a brother that the Olympians will scarcely notice the substitution."

"And just how do you mean to replace Alkeides with Iphikles, cunning Eurytos?"

"We mean to, most worthy Amphitryon. You and I together. A harmless wound, a pinch of sleeping powder in the wine---and Herakles sleeps, while Iphikles wins the Mycenaean throne, 'helping' the gods in the Gigantomachy. After all, once Zeus is gone, so is his patronage of Eurystheus, the very thing standing in the way of the Amphitryads restoring their legitimate right. The Brotherhood of Salmoneus will support you..."

"And men will rule over men."

"And we will rule over men. We, who will in time take the Olympians' place in the minds of the Achaeans..."

[Banging bronze basins and hurling torches at the sky,]{.thoughts} very nearly burst from Iolaos.

"Will they bring us offerings?" he asked instead.

"Naturally. Salmoneus was a genius, not a madman: there's no such thing as a god without offerings."

"Human sacrifices included?"

"Well... not necessarily."

"Necessarily. I know men."

"And why does that trouble you, worthy Amphitryon? You, after all, sacrificed your own grandson without a second thought, to return to the world of the living! And I don't condemn you for it. More than that---in your place, I would have done the very same... and perhaps still will."

Iolaos stood, climbed out of the bath, and set to scrubbing himself down with a towel in earnest; a certain revulsion showed through his movements, as though he had spent his time in a tub of liquid manure.

Or of blood.

"But I do condemn it," he said quietly. "I---condemn it. Feeling no love for sacrifices, I have no love for priests either; still less for priests with their eye on godhood. A slave who's clawed his way to power---give me Eurystheus in Mycenae and Zeus on Olympos any day! You are possessed by pride, Eurytos the archer, a rabid, untamable pride, whose altar you're ready to heap with the corpses of gods, men, the Fallen, the Gigantes, heroes; you've been in Tartaros a long time already, basileus. Don't call me to your side---I won't come. I'll say just this: don't fear the Olympians, brethren of Salmoneus the Madman. Fear us."

"Us?" Eurytos's face went purple with rage. "You and your brats, one of whom isn't even yours?!"

"No. Us---men, the ones for whom you're so lightly preparing the fate of a sacrificial herd."

"I fear no one, Amphitryon."

"Now that's the right attitude to have," Iolaos agreed, and walked off, making it clear the conversation was over.

The gaze of the Possessed one burned into his back.
