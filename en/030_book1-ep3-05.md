#### 5

The quarrel between Creon and Nausicaa had blown itself out; the strange retinue of the strange basileus Eurytus had settled down to sleep; Creon's household had dozed off; into the sky, in all her golden-faced beauty, rose Selene the Moon, and across the black shroud of Nyx that wrapped Thebes, the countless shining eyes of the starry titan Argus Panoptes blinked awake. Only they, those starry eyes, and sleepless Selene, silvering all the Peloponnese with her light, watched as yet another figure emerged among the strange and mysterious shadows of the night --- a tall, very tall figure, closely resembling the wandering soul of some dead man, who had either by some miracle escaped the underworld realm of Anax Hades, or had strayed from Hermes the Guide and simply failed to reach Erebus.

As it happened, neither Selene nor Argus took much interest in this shadow --- one among many, even if it had slipped out of basileus Creon's house. But the nighttime wanderer, making his way through the sleeping streets of Thebes toward the city's northern outskirts, paid little attention to the beauties of the starry sky either. The moon lit his road, which suited him well enough; the rest was a matter of complete indifference to him.

At last the outlying huts fell behind, and --- somewhat to the left --- so did the mighty walls of Cadmea, the city's seed, the offspring of Cadmus the Serpent; now his long legs strode briskly along a narrow path, barely visible in the ghostly moonlight, leaving the city behind and climbing ever higher into hills overgrown with flowering tamarisk.

The path wagged its tail slyly, like a dog inclined to play; the man turned once again --- and saw a fire. A small fire, burning in a special hollow atop one of the hills, so that it could be seen only once one had drawn right up close.

Now and then the reddish flicker of the flame tore out of the darkness a half-collapsed, grass-grown entrance: three chipped stone steps, resembling the jaws of some unthinkable monster, and a low arch of porous sandstone.

Someone was sitting by the fire. A shapeless heap of rags, bent forward, in which it was hard to recognize a living creature.

"Greetings, Kinswoman," the newcomer said, quietly and a little hoarsely, stopping right at the fire.

"Greetings, Kinsman," the rags stirred, and the firelight fell upon a wrinkled, old-womanish little face, on which sharp, youthful eyes flashed with predatory attention.

"How many years has it been since we last saw each other, Galinthias, daughter of Proetus?"

"Twelve, my brother Eurytus. You looked considerably younger then," the dwarf-woman tittered.

"And even then you looked just like a Graea[^26] the Crone, same as now. Don't you ever age?"

[^26]: Old hags, sisters of the Gorgons, who shared a single eye and a single tooth between them.

"How much more could I possibly age? I've long since lost count of my years as it is. I don't age, and I don't grow young, and I have no plans to die. Even without that, I've lived, mark you, two ordinary human lifetimes already --- and I'll live on yet. The Fallen are wise, they understand: old fool Galinthias mustn't die, for they're waiting for her in Hades, oh, how they're waiting... The moment I step off Charon's boat they'll pounce on me, asking what and how! But when the appointed day comes, when the Fallen return to the world, when those who have fancied themselves gods come tumbling down from Olympus --- then out of joy I'll grow young again! Wise are the Fallen, and just, and they do not forget their faithful servants..."

"I know, Kinswoman," Eurytus answered dully, lacing his long, strong fingers together and flinching slightly when Galinthias mentioned "faithful servants." It seemed the basileus of Oechalia did not care for the word "servant."

"I know the Fallen won't forget either you or me... and that is why I'm here. And also because the Fallen have need of the Nameless Hero, son of Alcmene and the usurper."

"I knew that long before he was born," Galinthias spat contemptuously into the fire; it seemed to Eurytus that the spittle fell short of the nearest ember, landing in the grass instead --- but even so, it evaporated with a hiss, which appeared to satisfy the old dwarf-woman.

"That is precisely why I came to Thebes," the basileus of Oechalia said curtly, still standing and towering over Galinthias like a mountain. "I, and my son Iphitus. We came to teach the boy --- the what, and the how."

"Is your son one of the Kin?" the old woman asked quickly.

"Well... he will be," Eurytus stumbled slightly.

"Then he won't be able to teach the how."

"I'll be the one teaching the how. In visits. And only once the boy's grown some. I can't very well sit around in Thebes all the time!"

"The Fallen have no objection?"

"They have none."

"Then so be it. A pity it won't be you..."

"Never mind. But you're here, Galinthias. Are the offerings still being made?"

"A strange question. As before --- twice a year."

"Why not more often?"

"You were always in a hurry, Eurytus..."

"That's because I've no wish to spend two human lifetimes waiting!"

"And still --- you're too hasty. Or do you want Alcides to lose his mind before the appointed hour? Don't hurry an arrow along its flight, pupil of Silver-Bowed Apollo!"

A spasm ran through Eurytus's body.

"A sharp shot, Galinthias... Very well, let it be. Do you know that in a day's time there will be an archery contest, for the right to teach the Nameless Hero?"

"I know. You truly have grown older, Kinsman. You've barely arrived, and already you've managed..."

"Yes, I've managed a great deal. And my son Iphitus --- my son and my pupil, mine alone, not Apollo's! --- will contend against Myrtilus, Alcides's current teacher."

"That too I know," the dwarf-woman smiled, almost imperceptibly.

Eurytus raised an eyebrow slightly.

"And do you know, daughter of Proetus, that the loser will be sacrificed to Alcmene's son?!" he said triumphantly, almost shouting it.

The old woman poked thoughtfully at the coals with her staff, sending up a shower of sparks.

"Oh yes, now I know that too... But does the teacher Myrtilus know it?"

"He will. And he'll agree."

"Oh, he'll agree, all right," the old woman nodded, glancing at Eurytus with a certain respect.

"More than that --- I know who will win! And then this Myrtilus will offer *himself* up as the sacrifice! Do you understand, Galinthias?! Not just a victim, not some slave gone mad with fear --- but a teacher, knowingly mounting the altar for his own pupil! That is truly worthy of Tartarus!"

"Oh, yes!" the dwarf-woman's eyes flared in the glow of the dying fire. "You truly have grown up, wise Eurytus! Only..."

She fell silent for a moment, huddling into her rags against the chill.

"Tell me, Eurytus the Archer," she said at last, in a strangely trembling voice, "why do you need all this? Why do you need the Fallen back on Olympus? You don't believe in the Golden Age, do you?"

"I don't," the words came to Eurytus with some difficulty. "But otherwise Apollo will never accept my challenge."

"Apollo? The one who taught you?"

Eurytus did not answer.
