#### 5

The quarrel between Kreon and Nausikaa had blown itself out; the strange retinue of the strange basileus Eurytos had settled down to sleep; Kreon's household had dozed off; into the sky, in all her golden-faced beauty, rose Selene the Moon, and across the black shroud of Nyx that wrapped Thebes, the countless shining eyes of the starry titan Argos Panoptes blinked awake. Only they, those starry eyes, and sleepless Selene, silvering all the Peloponnese with her light, watched as yet another figure emerged among the eerie and mysterious shadows of the night---a tall, very tall figure, looking very much like the lost soul of someone dead---one that had either by some miracle escaped the underworld realm of Lord Hades, or had strayed from Hermes the Guide and simply never reached Erebos.

Not that Selene or Argos took much interest in this shadow---one among many, even if it had slipped out of basileus Kreon's house. But the nighttime wanderer, making his way through the sleeping streets of Thebes toward the city's northern outskirts, paid little attention to the beauties of the starry sky either. The moon lit his road, which suited him well enough; the rest was of no concern to him whatsoever.

At last the outlying huts fell behind, and---somewhat to the left---so did the mighty walls of Kadmeia, the city's seed, the offspring of Kadmos the Serpent; now his long legs strode briskly along a narrow path, barely visible in the ghostly moonlight, as he left the city behind and climbed ever higher into hills overgrown with flowering tamarisk.

The path wagged its tail slyly, like a dog in a playful mood; the man rounded yet another bend---and saw a fire. A small fire, burning in a hollow cut into the hilltop, so that it could be seen only from close by.

Now and then reddish glints of flame picked out from the darkness a half-collapsed, grass-grown entrance: three chipped stone steps, resembling the jaws of some unthinkable monster, and a low arch of porous sandstone.

Someone was sitting by the fire. A shapeless heap of rags, bent forward, in which it was hard to recognize a living creature.

"Greetings, Kinswoman," the newcomer said, quietly and a little hoarsely, stopping right at the fire.

"Greetings, Kinsman." The rags stirred, and the firelight fell upon an old woman's wrinkled little face, on which sharp, youthful eyes flashed with predatory attention.

"How many years has it been since we last saw each other, Galinthias, daughter of Proitos?"

"Twelve, my brother Eurytos. You looked considerably younger then." The dwarf-woman tittered.

"And even then you looked just like a Graia[\*](#fn-26){.noteref}, a Crone, same as now. Don't you ever age?"

"How much more could I possibly age? I've long since lost count of my years as it is. I don't age, and I don't grow young, and I have no plans to die. As it is, I've lived near on two ordinary human lifetimes---and I'll live a while yet. The Fallen are wise, they understand: old fool Galinthias mustn't die, for they're waiting for her in Hades, oh, how they're waiting... The moment I step off Charon's boat they'll pounce on me, wanting to know every last detail! But when the appointed day comes, when the Fallen return to the world, when those who have fancied themselves gods come tumbling down from Olympos---then I'll grow young again from sheer joy! Wise are the Fallen, and just, and they do not forget their faithful servants..."

"I know, Kinswoman," Eurytos answered dully, lacing his long, strong fingers together and flinching slightly when Galinthias mentioned "faithful servants." It seemed the basileus of Oichalia did not care for the word "servant."

"I know the Fallen won't forget either you or me... and that is why I'm here. And also because the Fallen have need of the Nameless Hero, son of Alkmene and the usurper."

"I knew that long before he was born." Galinthias spat contemptuously into the fire; it seemed to Eurytos that the spittle fell short of the nearest ember, landing in the grass instead---but even so, it evaporated with a hiss, which appeared to satisfy the old dwarf-woman.

"That is precisely why I came to Thebes," the basileus of Oichalia said curtly, still standing and towering over Galinthias like a mountain. "I and my son Iphitos. We came to teach the boy---what he ought to know, and how it ought to be done."

"Is your son one of a Kinsman?" the old woman asked quickly.

"Well... he will be." Eurytos faltered slightly.

"Then he can't teach how it ought to be done."

"I'll be the one teaching him that. Whenever I come by. And only once the boy's grown a bit. I can't very well sit around in Thebes all the time!"

"The Fallen have no objection?"

"They have none."

"Then so be it. A pity it won't be you..."

"Never mind. But you're here, Galinthias. Are the sacrifices still being made?"

"A strange question. As before---twice a year."

"Why not more often?"

"You were always impatient, Eurytos..."

"That's because I've no wish to spend two human lifetimes waiting!"

"All the same---you're too impatient. Or do you want Alkeides to lose his mind before the appointed hour? Don't try to speed an arrow in flight, pupil of Silver-Bowed Apollo!"

A spasm ran through Eurytos's body.

"You shoot true, Galinthias... Very well, let's leave it. Do you know that in a day's time there will be an archery contest for the right to teach the Nameless Hero?"

"I know. You truly have grown older, Kinsman. You've barely arrived, and already you've managed..."

"Yes, I've managed a great deal. And my son Iphitos---my son and my pupil, mine alone, not Apollo's!---will contend against Myrtilos, Alkeides's current teacher."

"That too I know." The dwarf-woman smiled, almost imperceptibly.

Eurytos raised an eyebrow slightly.

"And do you know, daughter of Proitos, that the loser will be sacrificed to Alkmene's son?!" he said triumphantly, almost shouting it.

The old woman poked thoughtfully at the coals with her staff, sending up a shower of sparks.

"Oh yes, now I know that too... But does the teacher Myrtilos know it?"

"He will. And he'll agree."

"Oh, he'll agree, all right." The old woman nodded, glancing at Eurytos with a certain respect.

"More than that---I know who will win! And then this Myrtilos will offer *himself* up as the sacrifice! Do you understand, Galinthias?! Not just a sacrifice, not some slave gone mad with fear---but a teacher, knowingly mounting the altar for his own pupil! That is truly worthy of Tartaros!"

"Oh, yes!" The dwarf-woman's eyes flared in the glow of the dying fire. "You truly have grown up, wise Eurytos! Only..."

She fell silent for a moment, huddling into her rags against the chill.

"Tell me, Eurytos the archer," she said at last, in a strangely trembling voice, "what do you want with all this? Why do you need the Fallen back on Olympos? You don't believe in the Golden Age, do you?"

"I don't." The words came to Eurytos with some difficulty. "But otherwise Apollo will never accept my challenge."

"Apollo? The one who taught you?"

Eurytos did not answer.
