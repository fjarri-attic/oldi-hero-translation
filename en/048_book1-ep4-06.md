#### 6

After his talk with Creon, Amphitryon was not overly worried about the coming trial --- barring something unforeseen, the judges would decide whatever the basileus prompted them to. Alcides would be found guilty and at once acquitted, under the law of the most just Cretan Rhadamanthys; then the boy would undergo the rite of cleansing, and in a week or two would set off for Cithaeron, into the hills, far from idle gossip and prying eyes.

Of course, that wouldn't bring Linus back to life... but then, the living have the living to attend to, and the talk with the twins was best not put off.

* * *

The brothers were nowhere to be found in the house. Amphitryon shrugged in surprise and felt the rumpled beds --- the blankets still held warmth, which meant the twins had bolted only just now and couldn't have gotten far.

Silently showering curses, he climbed onto the roof of the house and scanned the surroundings.

"I'll catch them and thrash them, like Zeus thrashed the goat Amalthea!" he vowed silently. "Gone completely wild... some heroes! I'll flog them, and lock them up until the very day we leave!"

And almost at once he spotted two figures, utterly indistinguishable from each other at that distance, in matching sand-colored chitons, walking briskly toward the northeastern edge of Thebes.

Shouting was pointless --- either the twins wouldn't hear him, or they'd pretend not to (Amphitryon didn't doubt that for a moment) --- so the former lawagetas jumped down into the courtyard, nearly turning his ankle, and hurried after his sons.

Right at the edge of the city he again caught sight of the two familiar figures ahead, turning off toward the hills, and quickened his pace.

A little longer, and Amphitryon would surely have caught up with the twins. He strode quickly past a heap of rotting branches that looked like the remains of a forgotten lean-to, growled so fiercely at a piebald dog that had started to bark at him that the poor animal tucked its tail and vanished in an instant, turned into a shallow hollow between the hills; and there they were, Alcides and Iphicles, some fifty paces ahead...

Zeus Dodonaios!

For the first moment Amphitryon simply froze where he stood, mouth agape, and then, coming to his senses, rushed forward, and soon found himself in the very spot where his sons had just been (he'd seen it himself!) --- and had vanished.

Vanished, disappeared, gone... in an empty spot where there wasn't even anywhere to hide!

Amphitryon convulsively swallowed the bitter lump stuck in his throat, trying to suppress his confusion and the fast-rising alarm, then glanced back at Thebes (there was no fortress wall on the northern edge, and the nearest buildings stood within a bowshot of him) --- and began darting between the hills, all but sniffing at every bush and boulder, trying not to miss anything that might serve as a hiding place for the damned boys!

"Alcides!" Amphitryon, thoroughly alarmed by now, finally couldn't help himself. "Iphicles!.."

Silence.

Amphitryon understood --- shout or don't shout, there would be no answer.

Circling, for the hundredth time, the small patch of ground where the children had vanished, Amphitryon suddenly, unexpectedly even to himself, felt that all his alarm had boiled away, down to the last drop, and his irritation had boiled away too, while his confusion had gone earliest of all, skimmed off like the first froth, leaving at the bottom only anger --- a faceless, unrestrained anger, swelling into a burning fury beyond reason's control, as though a battle raged all around him, and he, Amphitryon, had already drawn back his spear for the decisive blow, only to find the enemy vanished somewhere, and the spearhead trembling impatiently, longing to bury itself in anything at all, to pierce, to shatter, to tear apart; no hope remained, no thoughts remained, gone were purpose, meaning, use, and the inner fire stood ready to devour itself for want of any other prey...

It was just then that he saw.

Sidelong, out of the corner of his eye, hardly believing himself, he saw the outlines of the hills shift, almost imperceptibly, saw a half-ruined house flicker in a shifting haze to his left, near which...

Amphitryon just had time to mutter through his teeth, "Well, Hermes Pompaios!" --- and, without giving himself time to think, stepped into the haze. The ground swayed beneath his feet, a faint chill rose in his groin, and Amphitryon suddenly saw, with perfect clarity, this house that had appeared from who knew where, at whose entrance, sitting right on the ground, were Alcides and Iphicles, fervently explaining something to a lithe, sun-browned youth in a fine chiton and reddish-brown sandals.

The youth cut the twins off with a gesture; a gust of wind carried his voice to Amphitryon, and the former lawagetas flinched, biting his lip --- the youth's voice, even torn to shreds by the wind, seemed unpleasantly familiar. "The alley," memory prompted at once. "The dark alley, the slain Ethiopian slave, Alcmene at the fence, and the unyielding opponent in the aquamarine cloak... and a voice from behind his back! A voice that had rung out once before, in a tavern where poor Elphistic the Troezenian died so senselessly!"

That had been thirteen years ago.

Amphitryon looked more closely --- and recognized not only the voice but the youth's face as well. It was this very face he had seen eight years ago, in Autolycus's courtyard, when he'd come to persuade the man to take on the twins' training. Yes, this exact face, narrow, hook-nosed, high-cheekboned, resembling Autolycus's own features in some way, but without Autolycus's characteristic bulk... and in all the years since, the strange youth hadn't changed a bit.

"Well, so we meet again," Amphitryon smirked darkly to himself; he slowed his pace and, whistling and pointedly unhurried, made for the house.

The boys, absorbed in their conversation, and the mysterious ageless youth noticed Amphitryon only when he was quite close --- though the former lawagetas had walked openly, making no attempt to hide.

They noticed him, gathered their wits, and stared at the newcomer as though he were a shade come up from Hades.

"And here's dad..." Iphicles blurted, flustered, choking on the words halfway through, but Amphitryon paid no attention to him or to Alcides. He stood and stared intently at the hook-nosed youth, who returned the look in kind.

"I see you still haven't left my family in peace," Amphitryon said at last, in a tone that boded nothing good.

"This is Hermes, dad," Alcides said warily. "Well, the one... who's a god."

Something flickered in Amphitryon's eyes, but even the youth Alcides had called Hermes couldn't quite catch what it was.

"Rejoice, Hermes, son of Zeus," Amphitryon greeted the youth calmly, pronouncing each word with quiet dignity. "I am Amphitryon, son of Alcaeus. Will you receive a guest?"

"Rejoice as well, Amphitryon, son of Alcaeus," Hermeias answered gravely. "Be my guest, in the name of my father, patron of the hospitable!"

"Papa, Papa, we were just talking here with Dum... with Hermes," the twins babbled over one another, having decided that nothing too terrible was going to happen; but there they were mistaken, because Amphitryon cut them both off sharply:

"Quiet! Who taught you to interrupt when your elders are talking?!" --- the brothers fell silent, frightened. --- "I'll settle with you at home!"

Hermeias nodded approvingly.

"That's the right approach," he remarked, smiling. "I could always use more strictness myself. Too soft-hearted, I am..."

"So I've noticed," Amphitryon said, lowering himself to the ground across from the young god. "It was thanks to your lessons, after all, that Alcides broke his teacher Linus's neck."

"I know," Hermeias said, no longer smiling. "They've already told me everything."

"But not me," --- Amphitryon's voice came out unexpectedly sad.

"Then how do you know the killer was Alcides, and not Iphicles?"

"You just confirmed it yourself."

"I did?!"

"You did. I told you that Alcides broke Linus's neck, and you didn't object. What's more, you agreed, and added that the children had told you everything."

Hermeias's eyebrows crept upward.

"Chiron was right," he muttered, apropos of nothing in particular, and immediately added, "And why is it you need the killer to turn out to be Alcides specifically?"

"Because then he'll be acquitted under the law of Rhadamanthys. Heard of it? A Cretan, brother of the lord Minos... the one that says a man who answers an unjust blow with a blow bears no guilt."

"A good idea," Hermeias approved. "Creon thought of it, I'd wager?"

"Him... who else would it be."

"A clever basileus. Well, so be it then; all the more so since it's the truth. And if the judges decide to consult the oracle --- the answer will be the one you need. I'll see to that myself!"

Iphicles, growing bolder, touched his father's shoulder.

"So Alcides will be acquitted, then?" he blurted out. "Right, dad?"

"He'll be acquitted," --- this time Amphitryon didn't rebuke his son.

"And everything will go back to how it was?"

"No," Amphitryon cut him off roughly, trying to hide how deeply his son's question had wounded him --- a question asked in a voice so like Alcmene's, a question he had already answered yesterday. "After the trial I'll send Alcides to Cithaeron. And you, Iphicles, will stay in Thebes."

"I won't stay," Iphicles scowled. "Let them judge me too! So what if they find me guilty... they won't execute me, will they?!"

"They won't execute you. They'll exile you."

"There, you see!" Iphicles brightened, for some reason. "Let them exile me, then!"

"You fool!" Amphitryon exploded, barely holding back the urge to give his stubborn son a good clout across the ear. "You brainless ass! I'll be the one to exile Alcides myself, as his father!.. Or at least as his earthly father. But your brother will be acquitted, and cleansed before the gods" (Hermeias confirmed this with a nod) "and before men! But you'll be exiled without cleansing! And any free man will simply be obligated to kill you, and not only without polluting himself, but even performing an act pleasing to the gods! Understand now?! Am I right, Hermes?"

"You're right," Hermeias confirmed glumly.

"And there'll be no shortage of men eager to please the gods," Amphitryon finished, more calmly now. "Unlike those willing to cleanse you of your miasma. Remember the god-fearing Thebans at our gate... So it was Alcides who killed Linus. That's the end of it. Let's not speak of it again."

"Fine, we won't," Iphicles gave his father a stubborn look. "But I'm going with Alcides all the same. You won't stop me."

"Oh, come on, Iphicles," Alcides objected uncertainly to his brother. "Stay, why not! The house, the palaestra... Mother will be glad to have you, too... and you can come visit me. Dad, you'll let him visit, won't you?"

"I'll let him visit," Amphitryon relented, softening a little.

A mistake.

"I don't want to visit," Iphicles snapped, his voice dry and taut. "We're going together. Or I'll run away from home. Or I'll kill someone --- and then they'll have to try and exile me too!"

Hermeias stepped in just in time --- right then, only something utterly improbable, like the intervention of a god, for instance, could have stopped Amphitryon from giving Iphicles a mind-boggling thrashing.

"Boys," the Trickster ordered hastily, "scram! Quickly now! Let the grown-ups talk."

The twins scrambled to their feet with relief and dashed off at once, bare heels rustling through the tall grass --- both pairs of sandals stayed right where they were, standing by the threshold of the house.

For a while, left alone together, the god and the man said nothing.

"I wanted to ask about Alcides's fits," Hermeias said at last, stumbling slightly over the words. "How have they been, lately?"

"Almost stopped," Amphitryon shrugged. "What, has Hera grown kinder? Though... is it all right, me speaking to you so plainly like this? Should I make some kind of offering?"

"It's fine. And Hera... it's not about the stepmother. The truth is, Alcides's fits of madness haven't stopped, and aren't likely to stop any time soon," the Trickster admitted honestly. "The point is that he... that the children have learned to sense them coming, and I've taught them, meanwhile, to hide from prying eyes. Don't ask me, Amphitryon, why I'm telling you this --- just try to believe it: the brothers mustn't be separated. And don't worry --- I'll keep an eye on them at Cithaeron."

"And here I thought they'd let me grow old and die quietly in Thebes," Amphitryon said suddenly, with a strange smile at the young god, and it seemed to the Trickster, for a moment, that sitting before him in mortal guise was Senior, his uncle, Lord Hades; and the fact that Hermeias was many times this man's elder no longer mattered in the least.

For the first time, the Trickster had nothing to say.

"You know, Hermes," Amphitryon added, quite simply, waving away a persistent horsefly from his neck, "from this day on, I think I'll hold a better opinion of the gods. Of some of them, at least."

"Thank you," Hermeias answered, just as simply. "That's the best offering you could have made."

"Rather too big a one," Amphitryon sighed.
