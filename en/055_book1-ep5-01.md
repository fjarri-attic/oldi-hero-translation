### Fifth epeisodion

#### 1

"It isn't true," Hermeias said thoughtfully, watching the sweat-soaked twins skin the slain beast, "that a live dog is better than a dead lion. Whoever came up with that strange notion is a fool. In some --- no, in many cases, a dead lion is far better than a live dog! Wouldn't you agree, Pan?"

"Uh-huh," the taciturn Pan nodded and scratched under his right knee with his left hoof.

"Let's have a drink instead," curly-haired Dionysus put in, but no one paid him any attention.

The merry god of the vine had, for the occasion, thrown a spotted leopard skin over his usual chiton, and looked somewhat ambiguous next to the half-flayed carcass swarmed by flies gone mad with joy --- all that remained of the former glory of the Man-Eater of Cithaeron.

One of the twins paused for a moment in skinning the kill, tore up a handful of grass, and wiped it across the dully gleaming blade of his knife. Then he ran the back of his hand across his forehead, smearing his face with fresh blood, and went back to what he'd been doing.

"I wonder," Hermeias went on, surveying the brothers' powerful backs --- like heaps of river boulders, wet and slick --- with a connoisseur's eye, "what the local shepherds would say if our fine fellows had brought them a live dog instead of a dead lion? I imagine eighteen-year-old boys would find it hard to hear all that without blushing. Wouldn't you agree, Pan?"

"Uh-huh," the taciturn Pan nodded and scratched under his left knee with his right hoof.

"Well, let's have a drink already!" Dionysus raised his voice, offended, shaking a nearly full wineskin temptingly.

Alas, Bassareus's call went unheeded once again.

The second twin, laboring diligently over the lion's shaggy head, cried out as he pricked his finger on one of the fearsome fangs, and stuck the injured finger in his mouth like a child. At the sight of this Hermeias couldn't help laughing --- the gesture looked altogether too absurd next to his torn shoulder and the impressive red-and-blue bruise across his ribs. The twins raised their heads at the same moment, glancing at the Trickster, and Hermeias waved a reassuring hand: carry on, all's well!

The sharp smell of death rising from the sprawled body of the Man-Eater of Cithaeron was nauseating; sated, heavy flies buzzed lazily, gleaming green and mother-of-pearl; from a tiny spring in the hollow came a damp, musty smell, and the Trickster caught himself feeling an irresistible urge to talk --- nonsense, foolishness, anything at all, so long as it wasn't the silence, broken only by the crunch of flesh being parted and the buzz of insects, a silence that smelled of blood and recalled the recent screams, the roaring, the furious snarling of three throats, the soul-tearing crack of bones, the choking, gurgling wheeze...

Hermeias had never thought of himself as a warlike god.

Which was why he disliked frenzied Ares; and, perhaps, unperturbably icy Artemis the huntress as well.

But sometimes, in rare moments of weakness or solitude --- like now, watching the brothers' focused faces --- the cunning son of the Thunderer and Maia Pleiades admitted to himself a faint envy of mortal recklessness: their gift for deciding without weighing, and acting without measuring the consequences.

"Poor little cub," Hermeias said, measuring with his eyes the heap of meat, muscle, and bone that only recently had been a fierce predator a good five cubits long (not counting the tufted tail) and two and a half cubits at the shoulder. "Two against one... there's something not quite fair about that, when you get down to it! Is the foolish beast to blame for preferring bipeds to quadrupeds (though it wasn't above the latter either)?! There it lies, the innocent victim of a mismatch in taste, reeking up the whole countryside! Wouldn't you agree, Pan?"

"Uh-huh," Pan plopped down on the grass and set to scratching his itching legs with both hands, cursing the stifling, humid month of Metageitnion[^37], when fleas plagued the shaggy god worst of all.

[^37]: Corresponds roughly to the period from August 15th to September 15th.

"Wine, right here!" cried Dionysus, deeply miserable, in the tone of a market huckster, trying not to look at the lion's hind paws, already stripped of hide and curved claws. "Ah, what delicious wine, damn you all! Alcides, my good man, shall we toast the departed?!"

Without looking, Alcides reached out, took the wineskin that Dionysus gladly held out to him, untied the knot with his teeth, and began splashing wine over the sides and belly of the dead lion, folding the hide back and carefully trimming away the yellowish-white strands of fat with his knife.

"It's dried on already," he explained, passing the wineskin to his brother. "Comes off badly... And it smells less now, too."

Dionysus clutched his head and groaned.

"Should I get angry, then?" the merry god inquired listlessly, pursing his plump lips.

"No need," the twins responded in chorus. "Better go fetch a second wineskin. Or send someone."

The brothers' fingers --- one could sense, even from a distance, just how sticky they were --- moved with confidence and skill, their breathing never once faltering, not even when the awakened snakes of muscle wound around their arms, tensing in a short, precisely measured effort; only the slightest bulge showed in their ridged bellies with each breath --- not as pronouncedly sculpted as a city athlete's, but able to withstand a satyr's kick; and the twins' bare thighs, right down to the middle of their stone-hard buttocks, were equally covered in a coarse growth of black hair --- spiteful tongues called all the male Perseids Melampygoi, that is, Black-Arsed (and sometimes considerably cruder still).

Called that, naturally, behind their backs --- and it's hardly worth explaining why.

Hermeias studied the twins, catching himself in a sudden, unaccustomed swell of pride, as though he were a sculptor gazing at the finished statue of his own making, or a father contemplating his grown sons... and a faint cloud of sadness did not darken but rather set off the Trickster's present state, because sadness was not natural to the Family, and also because Hermeias Psychopompos had never looked at Autolycus, nor at Pan, nor at six-year-old Abderus from Opuntian Locris, his youngest son by a mortal woman, the way he looked at the Amphitryad brothers.

Only now was he beginning to understand what he had lost and what he had gained; only now did the meaning of that strange expression become clear to him --- the one that occasionally surfaced in the coal-black depths of the childless Lord Hades's eyes, when Senior glanced sidelong at Hermeias, his nephew, thinking the Trickster didn't notice.

And fragile, pale Persephone, his uncle's wife, who looked like a night-blooming flower, wept quietly at night.

"We --- men, fathers, mortal or immortal," Hermeias thought, "siring children, we never think about them, plunging headlong into the river of momentary pleasure. And afterward we don't really care about them either, but about ourselves, trying to raise an extension of ourselves --- one able to finish what we left undone, to love what we failed to love, to prove what we failed to prove --- and, substituting usefulness for love, we lose them, the way a precious brooch is lost from a bag worn through. We lose our sons --- and others find them. It is always so..."

Alcides frowned and bit his lower lip; Iphicles, gripping the edge of the hide more firmly, unconsciously did the same --- and a low, slightly muffled voice sounded in the Trickster's mind, as if its owner were standing right beside him instead of being in Thebes.

"Not always," said that voice, the voice of Amphitryon. "Far from always."

The three gods stood by the remains of the Man-Eater of Cithaeron, watching the brothers walk away --- one of them dragging the freshly stripped lion's hide, the other carrying a club and a bundle of short javelins.

"And Chiron said," Hermeias murmured, "that in a fight they're like young Ares... the same rapture of battle, the same dissolving into the moment. Though, according to Chiron, Ares loved all sorts of shiny toys almost from childhood, while these two are only hindered by weapons. 'They themselves are weapons,' the centaur said."

"Uh-huh," this time Pan didn't even think to scratch himself.

"Or maybe..." Dionysus began hopelessly, cutting the question off halfway, staring at the ground and so not seeing Hermeias and Pan smile and wink at each other.

"My throat's gone a bit dry," Pan remarked thoughtfully, and gave a gentle butt to the back of Dionysus, who could scarcely believe his luck. "Shall we have a little something, you old drunkard?"

And a sparkling wine-fall, crashing down straight out of the air, washed over what only recently had been the Man-Eater of Cithaeron, and was now simply carrion.
