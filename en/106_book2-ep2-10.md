#### 10

Over the Phlegraean Wastes the sun rose warily.

::: {.small-scene-break}
:::

...Today that other one, the second one, as though sensing something, gave no sign of life, having hidden himself in the farthest corner of consciousness, or even deeper still, in the dark, tangled galleries of his inner Tartarus, where Eurytus dared not look --- and the former basileus of Oechalia, former head of the Brotherhood of Salmoneus, former Possessed Eurytus the archer, and now a hero of Tartarus under that same name still, for he had never once called himself a Gigas, and the Possessed nurses had long since grown used to it...

Barely recalling what he had just been thinking about, Eurytus finally drew a full breath and looked about him with something like a lucid gaze.

This did not happen often --- his consciousness was forever clouded by that other one, the second one (or rather, the first one, who had lived in this body before Eurytus), whom he had never quite managed to kill. Yes, Eurytus had succeeded in suppressing the young Gigas's primitive mind, but the former basileus had not been able to destroy his grandson's soul entirely. He walked, ate, breathed, shot his bow --- every skill remained perfectly intact --- spoke with the Possessed, yet at the same time his mind, the shadow of his immortal soul, always felt the faint but insistent pressure of an alien and terrible presence. Eurytus dreamed while waking; his speech grew disjointed; phantasmagoric visions swarmed within and without --- cliffs weeping tears of blood, an enormous fish's tail, cooing contentedly, swallowing lines of bodiless ghosts trudging obediently toward it, a sky studded with weeping eyes, the half-decayed faces of the Possessed nurses...

Sometimes he remembered his meeting with Amphitryon-Iolaus, and always wondered: why had that stubborn, intractable man managed to kill his grandson's soul completely, seizing sole control over the body for himself?!

He, Eurytus the archer, had not managed it --- but heaven be his witness, not for lack of wanting to!

In the rare minutes of lucidity --- like now, for instance --- Eurytus knew clearly that he was going mad; that he was mad already --- the three-odd years in which he had shared a growing body with a grandson defeated but not destroyed had taken their toll.

"You cannot toy with Tartarus and go unpunished," he thought bitterly. "We tried to make use of the Fallen; they tried to do the same with us... Can the knot that has been tied be undone without loss to both sides? Or can it only be cut?!"

Laomedon the Trojan, who had been to Phlegrae about a year before, had wrestled with the same question, but even the two of them together had found no answer.

Eurytus sighed once more and surveyed the children's half of the Phlegraean Wastes --- the smooth carpet of velvety black ash, the sparse outlying hills, the child-Gigantes lazily chasing one another round the central altar ("the dinner table," he smirked)... Gigantes --- to the gods. But to him, and to the Possessed nurses conferring over something nearby --- children. Grandchildren. Mortal offspring of men and the Fallen. Heroes unaware of their own purpose...

And what, in truth, is that purpose? And is Eurytus himself certain he knows?

Just now, Eurytus was certain of nothing.

::: {.small-scene-break}
:::

At that very moment, on the crest of a nearby hill, the threads of an opening Dromos began to shimmer silver.

The Family had come to Phlegrae.

Eurytus might have failed to recognize hobbling Hephaestus, or Artemis the huntress; in the end, he might not even have recognized Zeus Brotoloigos, since until now he had seen only cult images of him, which bore little resemblance to the original --- and besides, Zeus was not among the Olympians who had appeared on the Phlegraean Fields.

But his own teacher and executioner, golden-bowed Phoebus-Apollo, Eurytus could not fail to recognize!

And the lips of the hideously beautiful adolescent, from whose eye-sockets an old Possessed one looked out at the world, stretched into an ominous smirk.

So meet wolves from different packs.

Eurytus slowly picked up the bow lying beside him, checked the bowstrings, the working one and the spare, slung the strap of his arrow-quiver over his shoulder --- praise the nurses, they had supplied him with everything he needed! --- and, just as unhurriedly, drew the bow and nocked the first arrow to the string.

He was in no hurry at all.

The Olympians had come to the Phlegraean Wastes without Heracles --- well, splendid! If everything went as planned, Eurytus would deal with the Gigantes himself afterward.

True, the approaching Family was missing Zeus and a few other gods; that was unsettling.

The Dromos on the hill flared brighter, and Eurytus, glancing sidelong at the newest arrival, gave a satisfied snort: speak of the Thunderer, and there he is!

And, taking calm aim as though at an ordinary contest, he loosed the first arrow into the chest of Apollo, striding proudly at the head of the Family.

He hit.

The god staggered, glanced in surprise at the oaken shaft with its pale grey-blue fletching jutting from his body, yanked it free in a single motion, flung it aside --- and unerringly turned an angry gaze on Eurytus, who was already drawing a second arrow from his quiver.

The golden bow blazed in Phoebus's hands, but the Olympian had no time to shoot.

The children playing by the altar noticed the newcomers and ran toward them, unhurried, waddling.

Creatures like these were, to the young Gigantes, sometimes sacrifices and food, sometimes amusing toys, but in the end always --- food.

Just now the children were well-fed.

They wanted to play.

They hurried toward their new toys, which were far brighter and more appealing than the old ones.

Eurytus had seen this happen more than once --- the Possessed nurses regularly delivered to Phlegrae petty dryads, young satyrisks, and heedless little nymphs --- and he never ceased to marvel at the sight.

The silhouettes of the Gigantes blurred and swam; around each child a column of roiling darkness began to form --- colossal figures with indistinct outlines, propping up a sky already low enough --- while the fearsome gods visibly began to shrink, turning into toys, fakes... into gods no one believes in.

They do not even know such a word --- gods.

A tiny dim arrow flew from the string of the toy bow of the toy Apollo --- and struck the left eye of Ephialtes, who was running in front; the Gigas whimpered, clutching the wounded spot with his hand, then, stopping, began to rub the injured eye (though it wasn't hurt badly), with a look of concentration and grievance. The new toy had turned out to be a bad one --- it had hurt the little one! Sent a nasty fly that bit him in the eye! Well, never mind, now the little one would show this horrid doll how it felt to hurt him! --- and young Ephialtes advanced with determination on Phoebus, who was backing away in terror.

Zeus, lord of gods and men, was already thundering and flashing lightning for all he was worth --- but the thunder came out somehow unconvincing, and the lightning bolts looked more like sparks flying from a bonfire; they only barely singed the hands of the Gigantes reaching for their new amusement --- and the hands jerked back in surprise, only to reach out again a moment later.

The other gods fought off the children no less furiously with their traditional weapons, but the children only stared in bewilderment, not understanding what was happening, and tried to touch the little biting creatures, to look at them more closely, to stroke them...

Eurytus lowered his bow and burst out laughing.

For the Family this was a battle to the death; for the Gigantes, a game of kittens with baby mice, but soon the children would grow angry, grow hungry --- and the game would end. There they were, the great and mighty gods, scrambling like pitiful puppets underfoot, dodging in terror the fingers of those who did not believe in them!

And it was these nonentities that all Hellas glorified, placated, and feared?!

O Olympus, grey Olympus, you deserve better masters!

::: {.small-scene-break}
:::

Eurytus was still laughing when the Dromos on the hill spat out two more figures.

The adolescent with the eyes of the Possessed and the body of a half-man started, then, cutting off his laughter, quickly took aim --- and the bowstring, creaking, crept back toward the archer's right ear.

Before Eurytus stood the one man who could change the outcome of the Gigantomachy that had begun.

Before him stood Heracles.

::: {.big-scene-break}
:::

Athena watched the great battle.

Her father, high-thundering Zeus, struck with flashing lightning at the clawed paws of the Gigantes reaching for him, but, though they recoiled, the paws came back again, and the Thunderer slowly retreated; father Dias's palms were already smoking, but he did not notice, forgetting the pain, and for an instant it seemed to Athena that the Gigantes were about to falter, to give ground... no, that was only an illusion.

Her brother Apollo Aigletes struck down enemies from a bow blazing unbearably bright, but the black colossi paid no attention whatever to the golden arrows striking them, only occasionally dodging the red-hot boulders that the lame Hephaestus, god of the forge, hurled at them...

The Family fought with the last of its strength.

And Athena's place was there, among them.

The Cleaner would work out for himself what to do --- though her father had erred in this last scheme of his, for what could a mortal do where even the Kronids were powerless?!

And the mortal Iphicles looked down at Phlegrae, and saw the never-before-seen.

Children were playing with dolls.

But the dolls did not want to play. They fussed about, brandished their doll weapons, ran, dodged --- and the children simply could not understand what was happening. Any moment now the children would grow angry and start tearing the arms, legs, and heads off their disobedient dolls --- the way an uncomprehending toddler who has caught a fly tears off its mica wings and its thin little legs, and then puts it in his mouth, not understanding what he is doing.

Through the shimmering haze, like a false vision, there loomed: formless columns propping up a sky oozing with the festering sores of stars, giants circling the plain --- and the Olympian gods, hurling the full might of the Family at their enemy, already understanding and accepting their doom...

A lie.

But here is the truth.

Children are playing with dolls.

Children are breaking dolls.

Iphicles looked at the Gigantes and understood --- before him were Heracles's brothers and sisters, little Alcideses, forcibly conceived at the crossing-point of many great designs; here it was, the second, successful attempt, freaks reared on sacrifice, priests and gods unto themselves, mad through and through, for they had never had an Iphicles of their own to hold them, to keep them from being handed over to the moldering madness of the Fallen --- and now these small beings lived out their lives forever in an eternal Tartarus, feeding on the souls of immortals whom they sacrificed to themselves.

Never tormented by what they had done; not even knowing what it was --- to be tormented.

And a little way off stood the Possessed nurses, exchanging words with lazy smirks.

The true parents of the Gigantes.

Unlike Amphitryon the lawagetas and the twin Amphitryads --- happy children and happy parents.

Happy in their deformity.

::: {.small-scene-break}
:::

...because the maimed, misbegotten children are not to blame for their own deformity, but it frightens me to think what will happen if the mad Possessed priests of the Brotherhood of Salmoneus climb to heaven on the shoulders of the mad Gigantes children. I'm afraid all of Hellas will become one vast altar then. Do you promise me?

::: {.small-scene-break}
:::

"I promise," Heracles whispered, not wiping away his tears, as though the shade of the unfortunate Oechalian in Hades could hear him, and raised his bow.

Such children must not live.

And not just children.

The first arrow was wasted --- Iphicles had aimed at the phantom colossus tearing the peplos from Athena, who was screaming and brandishing her spear --- but already the second, sent at the real child tearing with a drooling idiot's smile at the covering of his plaything, now frightened to death and therefore mortal...

Already the second arrow found its mark unerringly.

Then Iphicles ran down the slope and helped Athena climb back up --- Promachos had lost every trace of divine hauteur just now, looking like nothing more than an ordinary girl on the verge of hysterics, a dime a dozen, in torn clothes besides, smeared with Phlegraean soot.

It was at that very moment that an oaken arrow with pale grey-blue fletching whistled past beside them.

Eurytus the archer swore --- Iphicles's dash had been so unexpected that the former basileus had missed, almost for the first time in either of his two lives --- and went back to his accustomed work.

The next arrow he sent struck ringing against the shield that Athena, having come to her senses in time, threw up to meet it.

The third met the same fate as the one before it.

Now Athena knew exactly what she had to do. She, a warrior goddess, had to shield this mortal Cleaner... hero... brother --- for on him, and him alone, hung the outcome of the Gigantomachy.

Her father had been right about everything.

His living thunderbolt was matchless.

The goddess laughed when the Cleaner struck down another Gigas, and then a third; she shook her fist at the Phlegraean Wastes, forgetting herself in rapture...

And the arrow of Eurytus the archer, who never forgave another's mistake, pierced Iphicles's exposed thigh.

Recovering herself, Athena dropped instantly beside the Cleaner, who had fallen to one knee, grinding his teeth against the pain; the goddess covered the wounded man with her shield, cursing herself for her carelessness.

Everything swam before Iphicles's eyes --- the gods and Gigantes shrinking and swelling again, the festering cataracts of the tormented sky, the whirls of ash rising over the plain --- then the sharpness of his vision returned to him, and he saw.

To the right, some twenty of the Possessed nurses were hurrying briskly toward their hill --- and every one of them was armed.

The calculation of the misshapen archer who had wounded Iphicles was unerring: if the goddess were distracted by the Possessed nurses for even a moment, the Phlegraean marksman would not let his chance slip, and would finish off the now-immobile Heracles.

And the Gigantes would finish the rest.

But the pain in his pierced thigh unexpectedly receded, halving itself; an invisible, cool wave washed through him, bathing the torn shore; and Athena stared in astonishment at the smiling Cleaner, who had just distinctly whispered: "You are me..."

That was why the wise goddess did not at once notice that, halfway between them and the band of the Possessed, the glassy webbing of an opening Dromos had begun to pulse, and from it two figures emerged, swords in hand.

A warrior in a familiar horse-maned helmet, gleaming in full battle armor, and a calm, focused man with grey at his temples.

Ares Enyalius, and Heracles.

And the girl with the shield kept shifting her gaze from one brother to the other, from Alcides to Iphicles, from Heracles to Heracles; and in Promachos's eyes, in the goddess's impassive eyes, shock beat like a bird caught in a snare.

"Alcides, the Possessed are yours!..."

Iphicles did not know whether he had shouted it aloud or only thought it, but Alcides gave a short nod, pointed the god of war toward the misshapen archer --- and hurtled down the slope, dusting up black ash, to meet the armed men.

And Ares the Killer answered his would-be driver with just as short a nod, and lunged toward the strange adolescent, who was nocking yet another arrow to his bowstring.

::: {.big-scene-break}
:::

...Iphicles saw his brother burst like a deadly whirlwind into the disorderly crowd of the Possessed, and for some reason it occurred to him that Alcides now resembled a Hecatoncheir, a Hundred-Handed one risen from the depths of Gaia, indifferent to who stood before him --- men, gods, Titans, Gigantes, the Fallen...

He saw Dionysus the Drunkard leap out of the swirling clouds of ash and knock the bow from the young archer's hands with his thyrsus, while the flashing sword of the war god, closing the distance, ripped open the belly of his disarmed opponent.

And the bronze head of Iphicles's arrow, wetted with venomous Lernaean gall, pointed toward seething Phlegrae.

Heracles had promised.

Such children must not live.

And not just children.

::: {.big-scene-break}
:::

Eurytus of Oechalia knew the battle was lost.

And he knew, too, that he was dying.

He no longer felt any pain; a crimson foam clouded his eyes; a deafening ringing swelled in his ears...

With a disobedient, numbing hand, Eurytus found the short, keenly honed knife tucked in his tunic and, whispering the necessary words with parched lips, cut his own throat with a smile.
