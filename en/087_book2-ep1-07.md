#### 7

Around midnight, alone now in the chambers assigned to him, Iolaos went on turning over that strange conversation in the megaron.

He could not shake the feeling that behind the ordinary conspiracy pursuing its banal goal---if not to replace the ambitious Eurystheus with a grateful, and therefore biddable, wanax, then at least to distract Mycenae from steadily carving up other people's pie---in short, behind a conspiracy of the kind that Achaian ruling houses have twelve to the dozen, lay something more.

Behind what was said lay what wasn't.

"If Zeus is to be believed, Herakles's immortality is assured..."---"Who better than us to set Zeus's mistake right..."---"And what about Zeus?..."

::: small-scene-break
:::

Iolaos tossed and turned on the carved kline heaped with worn wolf-skins, stared up at the grim gray ceiling where cracks wound like serpents, and thought, and thought...

From outside came shouts and the ragged singing of drunken suitors; somewhere among them his brothers were getting their own share of praises and foolish questions; somewhere nearby, no doubt, the happy Lichas was underfoot---they had wanted to lodge him in the servants' pavilion, but Iolaos wouldn't have it, hoping to keep at least half an eye on this natural disaster; he had no wish to sleep, no wish for anything, not even to think...

The door creaked.

Iolaos froze; only his right hand slid snakelike toward the head of the kline, feeling for the bone handle of his knife.

And then, relaxing, it slid back, once Iolaos recognized who had come in.

"Mortals knock first, and only then come in," he said offhandedly, turning onto his side.

Hermeias said nothing for a moment; then, the little wings on his sandal heels fluttering, he went back out, knocked softly, and came in again.

"Better, lawagetas?" the god asked listlessly.

And by that "lawagetas," Iolaos understood things were worse than he had supposed.

"Trouble, Hermeias?"

"Who doesn't have some, lawagetas?" The Trickster's haggard face composed itself into something resembling his old smile.

"What kind?"

"The kind you'd best not know. You're on leave with the twins---so enjoy your leave."

It wasn't said unkindly; Iolaos quite believed the Trickster might have troubles that outsiders were better off not knowing.

"Well, I am." Iolaos shrugged and rolled onto his other side.

He, too, had plenty of news of his own that he had no intention of telling Hermeias.

At least, not now.

"Don't be angry, lawagetas." Hermeias sat down beside him on the edge of the kline, straightening his ever-present chiton. "There's no need for you and me to quarrel. What you and I need is to talk."

"About what? About your troubles?"

"No. Not about mine. Not yet. Let's talk about ours instead. My aunt's been giving me no peace lately."

"What aunt?" Iolaos asked, puzzled.

"Mnemosyne. Memory. Take this, for instance---do you remember Eurytos of Oichalia's first visit to you in Thebes? The very first one."

Hermeias paused, then added reluctantly:

"You were still Amphitryon then."

Iolaos sat up sharply on the kline.

"I remember," he said dully. "Only, you know, Trickster... let's skip the reminiscing. I'm not a god, memory's no aunt to me. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Hermeias nodded, not taking his calm gaze off Iolaos's furious, bloodshot eyes. "Should I leave, or go on talking?"

Cracks ran along the ceiling, weaving their senseless lace; "Glory to great Herakles!" a thin voice shrieked in the courtyard, suspiciously like Lichas's, and was at once drowned out by a hoarse, many-throated roar: "Glo-o-o-ory!..."

"Go on." Iolaos touched the weary god's elbow, as if asking forgiveness for his involuntary outburst. "Sorry, Trickster, I think we've both had a hard day..."

"You can say that again. So then: Eurytos's first visit to Thebes, the contest for the right to teach the twins, between Iphitos Eurytid and the previous teacher... what was his name?"

"Myrtilos."

"Yes, Myrtilos. And then---a draw; and this same Myrtilos vanishes the next morning. By the way, did you ever find out what happened to him?"

"No. Neither hide nor hair of him since. There was talk that his chariot---it had a distinctive carving on the back panel---had been seen somewhere in Argolis, or maybe Messenia..."

"The chariot---perhaps. But not its owner."

"So?"

"Nothing. Only, the day after the contest, in the foothills of Kithairon, before the eyes of a shepherd, the teacher Myrtilos sacrificed himself to young Alkeides. I saw part of it---the sacrifice, and... the fit."

A hollow echo of the words filled the chamber, turning it for a moment into something like a mountain gorge.

"And you---kept silent? Silent all this time?! Why didn't you tell me this back then, earlier... when I was still Amphitryon?!"

"And what would you have understood, then? Besides, I had no time for talk. If you don't believe me, ask Cheiron. Then again: the draw in the contest wasn't so much Myrtilos and Iphitos's doing as it was mine and Apollo's. My half-brother decided Eurytos had gotten above himself---Apollo kept calling him 'little basileus' every other word---time, he said, to take him down a peg. So... we took him down. Four-handed."

"Fine, Hermeias---gotten above himself, taken down, a draw... but why sacrifice himself, of all things?!"

"I don't know. I suppose that---"

"Wait, Trickster. Let me gather my thoughts. So: a draw; suppose Myrtilos guesses at divine help... and he's proud---was proud, rather---and decides... No, he wouldn't decide that on his own. So someone helped him along. Put him up to it. Who?---whoever stood to gain by it. Perhaps Galinthias, daughter of Proitos, may she rest in fire! Though no: if the old Possessed one had come to Myrtilos with such an idea, before the contest or after, he'd have thrown her out by the scruff of the neck! Could it be... Eurytos the Oichalian?!"

"I don't know, lawagetas. But I think much the same. If, of course, such terms really were put to the late Myrtilos---and not after the contest, but before it."

"Then ask him, why don't you! Or has Lord Hades forgotten how to loosen a shade's tongue?!"

"Myrtilos isn't in Erebos, lawagetas. He's in Tartaros. Like any other sacrifice to Alkeides---especially a willing one."

"But if there's even a grain of truth in our guesses, then Eurytos is... one of the Possessed! And one of those who knew the true cause of Herakles's madness, at that!"

"And for the third time I'll answer: I don't know, lawagetas. But I'd like to."

* * *

"...Glory!" bellow brazen throats outside the window. "Glory to Herakles's driver, Iolaos, son of Iphikles, son of Amphitryon, son of Alkaios, son of Perseu-u-us!..."

"They named them all," Iolaos whispers soundlessly, staring at the ceiling, "and forgot one: the Thunderer himself, Perseus's own father... Ran out of breath, did they?"

Iolaos is alone in the chambers.

Cracks run and creep, serpentine; the intricate lines of a cracked human fate...
