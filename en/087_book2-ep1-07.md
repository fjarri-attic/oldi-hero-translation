#### 7

Around midnight, alone now in the chambers assigned to him, Iolaus went on turning over that strange conversation in the megaron.

It stubbornly seemed to him that behind the ordinary conspiracy pursuing its banal goal --- if not to replace the ambitious Eurystheus with a grateful, and therefore biddable, wanax, then at least to distract Mycenae from its steady encroachment on someone else's pie --- in short, behind a conspiracy of the sort that in Achaean ruling houses come a dime a dozen, lay something more.

Behind what was said --- lay what wasn't.

* * *

"If Zeus is to be believed, Heracles's immortality is assured..." --- "Who better than us to set right Zeus's mistake..." --- "And Zeus --- what of him?.."

* * *

Iolaus tossed and turned on the carved kline heaped with worn wolf-skins, stared up at the grim gray ceiling where cracks wound like serpents, and thought, and thought...

Outside came shouts and the ragged singing of drunken suitors; somewhere among them his brothers were getting their own share of praises and foolish questions; somewhere nearby, no doubt, the happy Lichas was underfoot --- they had wanted to lodge him in the servants' pavilion, but Iolaus wouldn't have it, hoping to keep at least half an eye on this natural disaster; he had no wish to sleep, no wish for anything, not even to think...

The door creaked.

Iolaus froze, motionless, only his right hand sliding snakelike toward the head of the kline, feeling for the bone hilt of a knife.

And then, relaxing, it slid back, once Iolaus recognized who had come in.

"Mortals knock first, and only then come in," he tossed off carelessly, turning onto his side.

Hermeias said nothing for a moment, then went back out, the little wings on the backs of his sandals fluttering, knocked softly, and came in again.

"Like that, lawagetas?" the god asked listlessly.

And by that "lawagetas," Iolaus understood that things stood worse than he'd supposed.

"Trouble, Hermeias?"

"Who doesn't have some, lawagetas?" the Trickster's haggard face composed itself into something resembling a smile of old.

"What kind?"

"The kind you'd best not know. You're on leave with the twins --- so enjoy your leave."

It wasn't said unkindly; Iolaus quite believed the Trickster might have troubles that outsiders were better off not knowing.

"So I am enjoying it," Iolaus shrugged and rolled onto his other side.

He, too, had plenty of news of his own that he had no intention of telling Hermeias.

At least, not now.

"Don't be angry, lawagetas," Hermeias sat down beside him on the edge of the kline, straightening his unvarying chiton. "There's no need for you and me to quarrel. What you and I need is to take counsel together."

"About what? About your troubles?"

"No. Not about mine. Not yet. Let's talk about ours instead. My aunt's been giving me no peace lately."

"What aunt?" Iolaus didn't understand.

"Mnemosyne. Memory. Take this, for instance --- do you remember Eurytus of Oechalia first visit to you, in Thebes? The very first one."

Hermeias paused, then added, reluctant:

"You were still Amphitryon then."

Iolaus sat up sharply on the kline.

"I remember," he said dully. "Only, you know, Trickster... let's do without extra memories. I'm not a god, memory's no aunt to me. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Hermeias nodded, not turning his calm gaze from Iolaus's furious, blood-shot eyes. "Should I leave, or go on talking?"

Cracks ran along the ceiling, weaving their senseless lace; "Glory to great Heracles!" a thin voice shrieked in the courtyard, suspiciously like Lichas's, and at once drowned in a hoarse, many-throated roar: "Glo-o-o-ory!.."

"Go on," Iolaus touched the weary god's elbow, as if asking forgiveness for his involuntary outburst. "Forgive me, Trickster, I think we've both had a hard day..."

"You can say that again. So then: Eurytus's first visit to Thebes, the contest for the right to teach the twins between Iphitus Eurytides and the previous tutor... what was his name?"

"Myrtilus."

"Yes, Myrtilus. And then --- a draw; and this same Myrtilus vanishes the next morning. By the way, did you ever find out what happened to him?"

"No. Not a word of him since. There was talk that his chariot --- it had a distinctive carving on the back panel --- had been seen somewhere in Argolis, or maybe Messenia..."

"The chariot --- perhaps. But not its owner."

"So?"

"Nothing. Only, the day after the contest, in the foothills of Cithaeron, before the eyes of a shepherd, tutor Myrtilus sacrificed himself to young Alcides. I saw part of it --- the sacrifice, and... the fit."

A hollow echo of the words filled the chamber, turning it for a moment into something like a mountain gorge.

"And you --- kept silent? Silent all this time?! Why didn't you tell me this back then, earlier... when I was still Amphitryon?!"

"And what would you have understood, then? Besides, I had no time for talk. Don't believe me --- ask Chiron. Then again: the draw in the contest wasn't so much Myrtilus and Iphitus's doing as it was mine and Apollo's. My half-brother decided Eurytus had gotten above himself --- Apollo kept calling him 'little basileus' every other word --- time, he said, to take him down a peg. So... we took him down. Four-handed."

"Fine, Hermeias --- gotten above himself, taken down, a draw... but why sacrifice himself, of all things?!"

"I don't know. I suppose that..."

"Wait, Trickster. Let me gather my thoughts. So: a draw; suppose Myrtilus guesses at divine help... and he's proud --- was proud, rather --- and decides... No, he wouldn't decide that on his own. So someone helped him along. Put him up to it. Who? --- whoever stood to gain by it. Perhaps Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, curse her ashes! Though no: if the old Tartarus-Possessed had come to Myrtilus with such an idea, before the contest or after, he'd have thrown her out by the scruff of the neck! Could it be... Eurytus the Oechalian?!"

"I don't know, lawagetas. But I think much the same. If, of course, such a condition really was set for the late Myrtilus --- and not after the contest, but before it."

"Then ask him, why don't you! Or has Lord Hades forgotten how to loosen a shade's tongue?!"

"Myrtilus isn't in Erebus, lawagetas. He's in Tartarus. Like any other sacrifice to Alcides --- especially a willing one."

"But if there's even a grain of truth in our guesses, then Eurytus is... one of the Possessed! And one of those who knew the true cause of Heracles's madness, at that!"

"And for the third time I'll answer: I don't know, lawagetas. But I'd like to."

* * *

"...Glory! --- iron lungs bellow beyond the window. --- Glory to Heracles's driver, Iolaus, son of Iphicles, son of Amphitryon, son of Alcaeus, son of Perseu-u-us!.."

"They named them all," Iolaus whispers soundlessly, staring at the ceiling, "and forgot one: the Thunderer himself, Perseus's own father... Ran out of breath, did they?"

Iolaus is alone in the chambers.

Cracks run and creep, serpentine; the whimsical lines of a cracked human fate...
