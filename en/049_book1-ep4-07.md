#### 7

Everything was almost as it had been thirteen years before.

...On the sixth day of the month of Hekatombaion, a procession of three chariots drawn by pairs of Mitannian horses rolled out through the southern gates of Thebes and onto the road; the first chariot was driven by Amphitryon himself, the second by grim Telemos the Nasal, and the third...

No, now everything was as it had been, yet not quite; or even not at all.

The crowd was silent, no one waved branches of laurel, tear-streaked Alkmene walked behind the last chariot, supported by her half-brother Likymnios---a grown man now, husband of Perimede, Kreon's sister, and nothing like the wide-eyed youth with the unruly black hair he had been on the pilgrimage to Delphi; no soldiers sang, and indeed there were no soldiers at all... only the chariots moved on, and the reins of the last one were in the hands of Iphitos of Oichalia, and behind him, bracing themselves against the rails, stood the twin brothers Alkeides and Iphikles, riding to Kithairon, into exile.

The trial had concluded, the law of Rhadamanthys had been pronounced, acquitting Alkeides, everything had gone according to the design of Kreon, basileus of Thebes---though not without one vexing snag. One of the judges, Iobates from Phokis, a stubborn and exacting man, had demanded that Alkeides's confession to the killing of his teacher Linos not simply be taken on faith, and that they consult the oracle of some suitable god. Zeus the Olympian struck pigheaded Iobates as unsuitable---a father, he reasoned, would always shield his son; Hera likewise failed to satisfy Iobates, on account of her possible bias; then Apollo was rejected (too much under Zeus's influence), Ares (encourages killing), Hades and Persephone (too far off, might not hear), Athena and Artemis (both virgins; though quite what disqualified them in the eyes of much-wise Iobates was unclear)...

While Hermes's candidacy was under discussion, a breathless messenger burst onto the square where the trial was being held and announced that Iobates's house had been robbed, and that, to make matters worse, unknown brigands had driven off two of the ill-fated judge's sheep flocks; on top of that, Iobates's twelve-year-old daughter had confessed to being pregnant, but not to who the father was.

Before fainting dead away, Iobates decided all this was a thoroughly convincing sign from the trickster god---and after that the trial went like clockwork.

They slaughtered a frantically squealing piglet, the priest of Apollo raised a prayer to the Purifier over the slain animal, Kreon waved the piglet over sullen Alkeides's head---having first checked with Amphitryon that this was indeed Alkeides---the piglet's blood spattered the boy's face and hands, and with that the purification was complete, whereupon the priest claimed the piglet for the altar.

He was rather too fond of roast suckling pig...

The preparations took a week. In that time the brothers twice went to Linos's grave, where the ashes of the murdered kitharode rested, and the rest of the time they sat cooped up at home in silence; Kreon fretted over the palaestra's looming closure, which would at once cost Thebes some of its renown, and the basileus some of his income... Iphitos the archer had firmly resolved to go with the brothers to Kithairon, Kastor was preparing to visit his own brother Polydeukes, and it was plain from the Lakonian's eyes that he most likely wouldn't be returning to Thebes; that left Autolykos and Amphitryon, who had promised Kreon they'd whip the first cohort of former pupils---seventeen or eighteen years old now---into shape, so that in a couple of years they'd... in short, it was all still very much up in the air, and that only added to the basileus's headaches.

For Alkeides and Iphikles, everything was falling away behind them: the palaestra, the curious eyes of the Thebans, the wrinkled little face of mad, dwarfish Galinthias glimpsed for an instant in the crowd, Alkmene's stumbling step, their teachers Autolykos and Kastor, waving from the front row of onlookers---a little clear space had opened up around the Lakonian and the son of Hermes, and the townsfolk nearest them huddled timidly together---the gate-guards, the gates themselves, the city walls...

The past remained behind them, gazing after the twins.

"I think we're grown up now," Alkeides whispered to his brother, shivering slightly. "What about you?"

Iphikles nodded without a word.

As though sensing the brothers' mood, Iphitos the archer unobtrusively held the horses back, keeping them to a walk, unhurried, so as not to carry the present away from the past too quickly---and the chariots of Amphitryon and Telemos pulled ahead at first, and then vanished around the bend altogether.

Hooves clop.

Wheels rumble over the stones.

Seven-gated Thebes falls further behind.

And here's the bend.

Iphitos the archer looked ahead, at the road, while the brothers looked back, at the city they were leaving, and so they saw different things. The twins saw a storm pushing up from the east beyond Thebes, heavy with rain, unfurling across half the sky as though waiting for the moment the chariots would vanish around the bend, whereupon the storm would finally heave a relieved sigh, setting the cypress tops swaying, and crash down on abandoned Thebes in dense sheets, growling and baring its teeth...

But Iphitos, gripping the reins, saw something else. He saw Amphitryon's chariot pull up at the roadside, and Amphitryon himself jump down and walk toward a boulder on which someone was sitting.

Iphitos shut his eyes for an instant despite himself---it had seemed to him that the late Linos the kitharode was sitting on the boulder by the road.

Behind Iphitos, the twins cried out, turning to look.

The first shock passed, and the archer's sharp eye unerringly determined that the man sitting on the boulder differed substantially from Linos, though he did resemble him, resemble him closely---but he was some seven or eight years younger, and the oval of his face was softer, and he did not shave his beard, only trimmed it short along his cheeks and chin; the man also stooped slightly as he looked up at Amphitryon, and Linos had never stooped... while he was alive.

"Kastor said you were in Pieria," Amphitryon was saying as Iphitos rode up and reined in beside Telemos's chariot. "Or in Iolkos."

"But I'm not in Pieria," said the man who resembled Linos, with the faintest hint of a smile. "Nor in Iolkos. I am here. Though, believe me, entirely by chance."

"I... I'm so sorry..."

"Don't. Don't, Amphitryon. Introduce me to the children instead. I've heard so much about Alkeides... about them both, that the gods would never forgive me if I neglected this meeting."

Amphitryon waved a hand without looking, and the twins jumped down and ran to their father.

"This is Alkeides and Iphikles." Amphitryon dropped the words sparingly, one after another, like stones into water. "And this is... this is Orpheus, Linos's brother."

"The younger," Orpheus clarified, for some reason, studying the twins. "His younger brother."

"I---" Alkeides shifted uncomfortably from foot to foot, clenching and unclenching his fists. "I... I've been purified!... Kreon did it. With a piglet."

Orpheus nodded, without rising from the boulder.

"Of course," he agreed gently. "Of course you're pure, boy. You're Alkeides, aren't you? And I can see in your eyes that you're ready to offer me blood-price for my brother's death, or your own life in place of it. And your brother will offer me his life in place of yours... and then your father will do the same. You have good eyes, boys, easy to look into, like a clear mountain stream---but every now and then a stream gathers its strength and becomes a river, rushing downhill and furiously spraying foam!... No, I won't take blood-price from you for Linos: our meeting is blood-price enough. For I've learned today that Themis isn't the only one who's blind; the other gods are blind too, though they fancy themselves all-seeing!..."

Amphitryon stood motionless, the twins were silent, spellbound, the horses towered like stone statues, harnessed to marble chariots with marble charioteers, the sky held back its storm, forgetting to breathe, listening to Orpheus's speech, to its strange rhythm, to the odd consonances of his supple, faintly husky voice---Orpheus had long since fallen silent, yet it still seemed that invisible strings were sounding in the air, plucked by invisible fingers... and beside the boulder lay a kithara in its case.

"Orpheus!" came a voice from the roadside. "Orphe-e-eus!"

And the spell broke.

Across a meadow overgrown with wild tulips, a girl was running toward the road---long-legged, impetuous, hardly much older than the twins, looking like a limnas, a nymph of the meadows, hurrying off somewhere.

"Orpheus! I'm here!..."

And a banner of chestnut hair snapped and rippled on the wind.

"This is my wife," Orpheus explained, in an apologetic tone, and merry sparks flickered in his gray eyes. "This is my wife, Eurydike. The two of us are on our way to Thebes, to Teiresias. In Pieria, at our wedding, the seer Eperos declared that our marriage would be happy if I unlearned how to look back... and Eperos refused to explain what he meant. So I thought I'd ask Teiresias---maybe he can tell us something..."

Some time later the chariots continued on their way to Kithairon, while the storm over Thebes still held off, as though letting the two travelers enter the city first---a man and a woman: Orpheus, thirty-two years of age, and sixteen-year-old Eurydike.

To err is human, and most of all for those who tell one another tales in the evenings by a blazing hearth... for everyone believed that Orpheus, son of Oiagros and Kalliope, and Herakles, son of Zeus and Alkmene, had first met in the harbor of Iolkos, where the twenty-five-oared *Argo* was being made ready to sail...
