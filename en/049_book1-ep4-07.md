#### 7

Everything was almost as it had been thirteen years before.

...On the sixth day of the month of Hecatombeon, a procession of three chariots drawn by pairs of Mitannian horses rolled out of the southern gate of Thebes and set off down the road. The first chariot was driven by Amphitryon himself, the second by grim Thelem the Nasal, and the third...

No, now everything was like that, yet not quite; or even not at all.

The crowd was silent, no one waved branches of laurel, tear-streaked Alcmene walked behind the last chariot, supported by her half-brother Licymnius --- a grown man now, husband of Perimede, Creon's sister, and nothing like the wide-eyed, wildly black-haired youth he had been on the pilgrimage to Delphi; no soldiers sang, and indeed there were no soldiers at all... only the chariots moving, the reins of the last one held by Iphitus of Oechalia, and behind Iphitus's back, leaning against the rails, stood the twin brothers Alcides and Iphicles, riding into exile on Cithaeron.

The trial had concluded, the law of Rhadamanthys had sounded and acquitted Alcides, everything had gone according to the design of Creon, basileus of Thebes --- though not without one vexing snag. One of the judges, Iobates from Phocis, a stubborn and exacting man, had demanded that Alcides's confession to the killing of his teacher Linus not simply be taken on faith, and that they consult the oracle of some suitably fitting god. Zeus the Olympian struck stubborn Iobates as unsuitable --- a father, he reasoned, would always shield his son; Hera likewise failed to satisfy Iobates, on account of her possible bias; then Apollo was rejected (too much under Zeus's influence), Ares (encourages killing), Hades and Persephone (too far off, might not hear), Athena and Artemis (both virgins; though quite what disqualified them in the eyes of much-wise Iobates was unclear)...

While Hermes's candidacy was under discussion, a breathless messenger burst onto the square where the trial was being held and announced that Iobates's house had been robbed, and that, to make matters worse, unknown brigands had driven off two of the ill-fated judge's sheep flocks; on top of that, Iobates's twelve-year-old daughter had confessed to being pregnant, though not to by whom.

Before fainting dead away, Iobates decided all this was a thoroughly convincing sign from the trickster god --- and after that the trial went like clockwork.

They slaughtered a frantically squealing piglet, the priest of Apollo raised a prayer to the Cleanser over the slain animal, Creon waved the piglet over sullen Alcides's head --- first making sure with Amphitryon that this was indeed Alcides --- the piglet's blood spattered the boy's face and hands, and with that the cleansing was complete, whereupon the priest claimed the piglet for the altar.

He was rather too fond of roast suckling pig...

The preparations took a week. In that time the brothers twice went to Linus's grave, where the burned ashes of the murdered kitharode rested, and the rest of the time they sat cooped up at home in silence; Creon fretted over the palaestra's looming closure, which would at once cost Thebes some of its renown, and the basileus some of his income... Iphitus the archer had firmly resolved to go with the brothers to Cithaeron, Castor was preparing to visit his own brother Polydeuces, and it was plain from the Laconian's eyes that he most likely wouldn't be returning to Thebes; that left Autolycus and Amphitryon, who had promised Creon they'd whip the first cohort of former pupils --- seventeen or eighteen years old now --- into shape, so that in a couple of years they'd... in short, all of it was written in water, and the basileus only gained more headaches for it.

For Alcides and Iphicles, everything now lay behind them: the palaestra, the curious eyes of the Thebans, the wrinkled little face of mad, dwarfish Galinthias flashing by in the crowd, Alcmene's stumbling step, their teachers Autolycus and Castor, waving from the front row of onlookers --- a kind of emptiness had formed around the Laconian and the son of Hermes, and the townsfolk nearest them huddled timidly together --- the gate-guards, the gates themselves, the city walls...

The past stayed behind their backs, watching the twins go.

"I think we're grown up now," Alcides whispered to his brother, shivering slightly. "What about you?"

Iphicles only nodded silently.

As though sensing the brothers' mood, Iphitus the archer unobtrusively held the horses back, making them walk at a pace, unhurried, so as not to carry the present too swiftly away from the past --- and the chariots of Amphitryon and Thelem pulled ahead at first, and then vanished around the bend altogether.

Hooves clop.

Wheels rumble over the stones.

Seven-gated Thebes falls further behind.

There, the bend.

Iphitus the archer looked ahead, at the road, while the brothers looked back, at the city they were leaving, and so they saw different things. The twins saw a storm pushing up from the east beyond Thebes, heavy with rain, unfurling across half the sky as though waiting for the moment the chariots would vanish around the bend, whereupon the storm would finally heave a relieved sigh, setting the cypress crowns swaying, and crash down on abandoned Thebes in taut torrents, growling and baring its teeth...

But Iphitus, gripping the reins, saw something else. He saw Amphitryon's chariot pull up at the roadside, and Amphitryon himself jump down and walk toward a boulder on which someone was sitting.

Iphitus involuntarily squeezed his eyes shut for an instant --- it had seemed to him that the late Linus the kitharode was sitting on the boulder by the road.

Behind Iphitus's back, the twins cried out, turning to look.

The first shock passed, and the archer's sharp eye unerringly determined that the man sitting on the boulder differed substantially from Linus, though he did resemble him, resemble him closely --- only younger by seven or eight years, with a softer oval of a face, and he did not shave his beard, only trimmed it short along his cheeks and chin; the man also stooped slightly as he looked up at Amphitryon, and Linus had never stooped... while he was alive.

"Castor said you were in Pieria," Iphitus heard Amphitryon say, as he rode up and drew to a stop by Thelem's chariot. "Or in Iolcus."

"But I'm not in Pieria," said the man who resembled Linus, with the faintest hint of a smile. "Nor in Iolcus. I am here. Though, believe me, entirely by chance."

"I... I'm so sorry..."

"Don't. Don't, Amphitryon. Better introduce me to the children. I've heard so much about Alcides... about them both, that the gods would never forgive me if I neglected this meeting."

Amphitryon waved a hand without looking, and the twins jumped down to the ground, running up to their father.

"This is Alcides and Iphicles," Amphitryon dropped the words sparingly, one after another, like stones into water. "And this is... this is Orpheus, Linus's brother."

"The younger," Orpheus clarified, for some reason, studying the twins. "His younger brother."

"I," Alcides shifted uncomfortably from foot to foot, clenching and unclenching his fists, "I... I've been cleansed!... Creon did it. With a piglet."

Orpheus nodded, without rising from the boulder.

"Of course," he agreed gently. "Of course you're clean, boy. You're Alcides, aren't you? And I can see in your eyes that you're ready to offer me blood-price for my brother's death, or your own life in place of it. And your brother will offer me his life in place of yours... and then your father will do the same. You have good eyes, boys, easy to look into, like a clear mountain stream --- but every now and then a stream gathers its strength and becomes a river, rushing downhill and furiously spraying foam!... No, I won't take blood-price from you for Linus: our meeting today is blood-price enough. For I've learned today that Themis isn't the only one who's blind; the other gods are blind too, though they fancy themselves all-seeing!..."

Amphitryon stood motionless, the twins fell silent, spellbound, the horses towered like stone statues, harnessed to marble chariots with marble charioteers, the sky delayed its downpour, forgetting to breathe, listening to Orpheus's speech, to its strange rhythm, to the odd consonances of his supple, faintly husky voice --- Orpheus had long since fallen silent, yet it still seemed that invisible strings were sounding in the air, plucked by invisible fingers... and beside the boulder lay a kithara in its case.

"Orpheus!" came a voice from the roadside. "Orphe-e-eus!"

And everyone came back to themselves.

Across a meadow overgrown with wild tulips, a girl was running toward the road --- long-legged, impetuous, hardly much older than the twins, looking like a limnade, a nymph of the meadows, hurrying off somewhere.

"Orpheus! I'm here!..."

And a banner of chestnut hair snapped and rippled on the wind.

"This is my wife," Orpheus explained, in an apologetic tone, and merry sparks flickered in his gray eyes. "This is my wife, Eurydice. The two of us are on our way to Thebes, to Tiresias. In Pieria, at our wedding, the seer Eperos declared that our marriage would be happy if I unlearned how to look back... and Eperos refused to explain what he meant. So, I want to ask Tiresias --- maybe he'll have something to say..."

* * *

Some time later the chariots continued on their way to Cithaeron, while the storm over Thebes still held off, as though letting the two travelers enter the city first --- a man and a woman: Orpheus, thirty-two years of age, and sixteen-year-old Eurydice.

It is human nature to err, especially for people telling each other various tales in the evenings by a blazing hearth... for everyone believed that Orpheus, son of Oeagrus and Calliope, and Heracles, son of Zeus and Alcmene, first met in the harbor of Iolcus, where the twenty-five-oared *Argo* was being made ready to sail...
