#### 8

Perched on a pile of pine logs, sharp with the smell of the forest, Iolaos watched two thickset Abantian slaves---native inhabitants of Euboia, whose tangled, wavy locks fell over their faces not from neglect, but in keeping with an ancient tradition forgotten by everyone except the Abantes themselves.

The Abantes, stripped down to loincloths, were absorbed in roasting a whole sheep on a spit, now and then exchanging guttural cries and splashing the roast with wine vinegar from a little clay jug. Tongues of flame, pale, yet no less fierce for it, licked greedily at the mutton as it dripped hissing fat; the Abantes' muscular bodies glistened with sweat and looked as if they too would start sizzling and smoking at any moment; a tantalizing aroma spread across the courtyard---and Iolaos, not particularly hungry to begin with, could not resist.

He got up, walked over to the fire, and with one motion pulled a wide butcher's cleaver from the log lying nearby---and, driving the blade juicily into the mutton's flank, hacked himself off a good hunk of meat, slightly charred at the edges. The Abantes grunted approvingly; one of them handed Iolaos half a flatbread and a bunch of slightly wilted greens---and then roared with laughter, clearly delighted, when Iolaos sank his strong teeth into the smoke-steeped mutton flesh.

Servants shuttled across the courtyard, unhurried and businesslike; a hungover damate limped off somewhere, nearly knocked down by three burly fellows in goatskins hauling baskets of provisions; nearby a butcher swung his axe in short, practiced strokes; two young women, none too carefully wrapped in pale blue peploi, gave Iolaos promising smiles and slipped quickly past, since the scowling young man paid their smiles no attention at all.

The gates shrieked piercingly, as if complaining about the guards hauling them open, and a chariot drawn by a bay pair rolled unhurriedly into the courtyard.

Basileus Eurytos tossed the reins to one of the guards, leapt down to the ground with youthful ease, straightening his snow-white pharos---the mantle perfectly matched Eurytos's unruly gray mane---and waved to Iolaos, who had risen from the woodpile.

It was at that very moment---not a second before---that Iolaos made up his mind.

He understood that trying to worm anything out of Eurytos of Oichalia on the sly was like stealing stars from the sky.

To Iolaos---even were he Herakles's nephew three times over---the basileus of Oichalia won't tell the whole truth.

Not to Iolaos, he won't.

Not to Iolaos.

Well then, next to the maneuver that (perhaps!) will get Eurytos talking, the battle at Orchomenos will look like child's play...

"Not at the games?" the basileus asked, coming up and eyeing the young man, piece of mutton in hand, with surprised good humor.

"What would I be doing there?" Iolaos asked lazily, even a little rudely.

Eurytos's eyebrows crept upward, crowding his wrinkled brow.

"Don't you hope to win my daughter Iole's hand?"

"An archer like me? Not a chance. Now, if there were chariot races here---that would be another matter... No, let my... uncle and father do the competing. By the way, basileus, why are you here yourself, instead of showing the suitors your legendary marksmanship?"

"Already have." Eurytos smirked, twisting his thin mouth. "Now I have nothing to do on the field until the very last round."

"I don't doubt it. Seems that in a quarter century---since the day you first came with your son to Thebes, to teach the future hero---you've changed little."

Eurytos, who had already started to leave, froze in place; then he slowly turned back to Iolaos, who went on chewing his mutton, as though nothing had happened, wiping away the juices running into his beard.

"You cannot speak of such things, young man," the basileus said---quietly, but with firm stress on the words "young man"---his face turning to stone.

"I can't," Iolaos agreed, crunching on the charred crust. "But I do."

"You suppose we have something to talk about?"

"I suppose so, basileus."

"Then---shall we perform our ablutions?"

"On such a hot day? Gladly!" Iolaos made as if he had suddenly remembered his manners.

The household folk followed them with furtive, curious glances---two such different men, the stern basileus of Oichalia and Herakles's young driver---and only when both had vanished into the depths of the palace did the slaves and servants come to their senses and return to their interrupted work.
