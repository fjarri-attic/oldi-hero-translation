#### 8

Perched on a pile of pine logs, sharp with the smell of the forest, Iolaus watched two thickset Abantian slaves --- native inhabitants of Euboea, whose tangled, wavy locks fell over their faces not from neglect, but in keeping with an ancient tradition forgotten by everyone except the Abantes themselves.

The Abantes, stripped down to loincloths, were absorbed in roasting a whole mutton carcass on a spit, now and then exchanging guttural cries and splashing the roast with wine vinegar from a little clay jug. Tongues of flame, pale, yet no less fierce for it, greedily licked at the mutton, dripping with hissing fat; the Abantes' muscular bodies glistened with sweat and seemed about to start sizzling and smoking themselves any moment now; a tantalizing aroma spread across the courtyard --- and Iolaus, not particularly hungry to begin with, could not resist.

He got up, walked over to the fire, and with one motion pulled a wide butcher's cleaver from the log lying nearby --- and, hacking juicily into the mutton's flank, tore off for himself a good hunk of meat, slightly charred at the edges. The Abantes grunted approvingly; one of them handed Iolaus half a flatbread and a bunch of slightly wilted greens --- and then laughed with pleasure when Iolaus sank his sturdy teeth into the smoke-scented mutton flesh.

Servants darted unhurriedly and busily about the courtyard; a hungover damates limped past somewhere, nearly knocked down by three burly fellows in goatskins hauling baskets of provisions; nearby a butcher swung his axe in short, practiced strokes; two young wives, none too carefully wrapped in pale blue peploi, gave Iolaus promising smiles and slipped quickly past, since the scowling young man paid their smiles no attention at all.

The gates shrieked piercingly, as if complaining of the guards hauling the leaves apart in opposite directions, and a chariot drawn by a bay pair rolled unhurriedly into the courtyard.

Basileus Eurytus tossed the reins to one of the guards, leapt down to the ground with youthful ease, straightening a snow-white pharos --- the mantle matched perfectly the wild, grey mane of Eurytus's hair --- and waved to Iolaus, who had risen from the woodpile.

It was at that very moment --- not a second before --- that Iolaus made up his mind.

He understood that trying to pry anything, unnoticed, out of Eurytus of Oechalia was like trying to steal stars from the sky.

To Iolaus --- even were he Heracles's nephew three times over --- the basileus of Oechalia would not tell the whole truth.

To Iolaus --- he would not tell it.

To Iolaus.

Well then, the battle with Orchomenus would look like child's babble next to the maneuver that (perhaps!) would get Eurytus talking...

"Not at the games?" the basileus asked, coming up and eyeing the young man --- a piece of mutton in hand --- with surprised good humor.

"What would I be doing there?" Iolaus asked, lazy and even a touch rude about it.

Eurytus's eyebrows crept upward, crowding his wrinkled brow.

"Don't you hope to win my daughter Iole's hand?"

"An archer like me? Not a chance. Now, if there were chariot races here --- that would be another matter... No, let my... uncle and father do the competing. By the way, basileus, why are you here yourself, instead of showing the suitors your legendary marksmanship?"

"Already have," Eurytus smirked, twisting his thin mouth. "Now I've nothing to do on the field until the very last round."

"I don't doubt it. Seems that in a quarter century --- since the day you first came with your son to Thebes, to teach the future hero --- you've changed little."

Eurytus, who had already been about to leave, froze in place; then slowly turned back to Iolaus, who, as though nothing had happened, went on chewing his mutton, wiping away the meat juice running down his beard.

"You cannot speak of such things, young man," the basileus said quietly, but with a firm stress on the words "young man," his face turning to stone.

"I can't," Iolaus agreed, crunching on the charred crust. "But I do."

"You suppose we have something to talk about?"

"I suppose so, basileus."

"Then --- shall we not take our ablutions?"

"On such a hot day? Gladly!" Iolaus made as if he had suddenly remembered his manners.

* * *

The household folk trailed them with sidelong, curious glances --- such different men, the stern basileus of Oechalia and Heracles's young driver --- and only once both had vanished into the depths of the palace did the slaves and servants, catching themselves, return to their interrupted work.
