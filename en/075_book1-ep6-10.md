#### 10

The weather was divinely foul.

Zeus the Thunderer tossed in his half-sleep, tugging at the sky's patched-and-repatched blanket, soaked through since an hour before dawn; from time to time piercing gusts of wind swept past, peevishly stripping leaves from the shivering trees, tugging at the clothes of the rare passerby, howling through the desolate alleys of what seemed a dead Thebes.

Once, for some reason, the wind veered off toward the outskirts, went howling between the hills, slapping the offended, rustling bushes about --- and froze in astonishment, forgetting its innate flightiness and foul mood.

And no wonder --- it isn't every day you find, on the threshold of some hovel sprung from who-knows-where, so strange a pair: a youth in a ragged, hooded chlamys, from beneath which peeked the hem of a dandyish, festive chiton, and a sullen six-year-old boy, wrapped head to toe in a woman's woolen peplos (had he snatched it from his mother, or what?!), chewing with great concentration on a garlic flatbread.

Before the youth and the boy stood a jug and two cups.

The wind sniffed, wrinkling up (the garlic smell got in the way), and with some effort determined that the jug held wine. And, to judge by the strength of its aroma, not particularly diluted, either.

Holding its breath, the wind crept closer and began to listen.

"Shoo, get out of here!" the youth in the chlamys said quietly.

The wind pretended not to have heard.

"Did you hear me?!" the youth snapped, flinging a splash of wine into the wind. "What, Eurus[^41], you think I didn't recognize you? Go on, blow off somewhere else!"

[^41]: Southeast wind, brother of Zephyrus, Notus, and Boreas.

Eurus --- for it was indeed he --- sighed in disappointment and flew off.

And since there was no one around besides the wind, there was now no one left to be surprised, and nothing left to be surprised at.

"...so what did the Lord say?" Two wiry boyish arms emerged from under the peplos and, setting the flatbread aside, hoisted up, with some effort, the tall, elaborately patterned jug.

"Pour me some too," Hermeias's voice sounded bored and detached. "The Lord... it's child's play for my uncle to loosen a shade's tongue. And Galinthias is chatty enough as it is. In short, all our guesses were confirmed."

"About the Gigantes?"

"About them. The Fallen in Tartarus really have spawned a new tribe --- mortal, but invulnerable to the Family. So, Amphitryon, expect an uproar soon. A big one..."

"How soon?" The wine went gurgling into the cups.

It seemed as though Amphitryon were asking questions only to keep the conversation going, as if Hermeias's answers hardly interested him; but whether that was really so, no one could say.

"How soon, I'm asking?"

"I don't know. Even Hades doesn't know. Soon. In ten years, maybe. Or twenty. Or a hundred."

For a while the boy and the youth were silent, not looking at each other.

"So how is your... Alcmene?" the Trickster finally asked, still looking away, turning the half-emptied cup in his hand.

"Oh, well... she's getting married. Says she wants to live out her old age in peace."

"To whom?"

"Some fellow's come around... Rhadamanthys the Lawgiver. Courting her. There'll probably be a wedding soon..."

"Ah... I know him. He's the one they exiled from Crete --- so he moved to Boeotia."

"What sort of man is he, though? What do you think, Hermeias?"

"The man? Seems decent enough. Honest, at any rate."

"Good enough. What, was he kicked out of Crete for his honesty?"

"Mm-hm," Hermeias nodded.

"I see. Alcmene had Amphitryon the Exile for a husband, now she'll have Rhadamanthys the Exile, and the two of them will make it to the grave... in peace."

"They'll make it. What happens after, I don't know. My uncle has his own plans for this Rhadamanthys. Says he'll take him on as a judge. Says good judges only ever come from men... and only after they're dead, at that."

They fell silent.

They drank.

Amphitryon finished his flatbread, produced another from somewhere among the folds of his peplos, and set about rubbing it with great concentration with a clove of garlic he had saved, sprinkling coarse grains of salt on top.

"Any word on Alcides?" he asked, as though in passing.

"Word on two of him, actually. One, they say, is wandering the Peloponnese; the other has been three months gone, sailing on the *Argo* out of Iolcus. And both of them --- Alcides."

"Elaborate," Amphitryon said, half asking, half ordering.

"As if there's more to elaborate... I'm telling you, one turned up first at your acquaintance Thespius's. Thespius cleansed him of miasma --- as basileus, and out of old friendship --- and Alcides up and vanished by evening. The cleansing didn't take, it seems..."

"And would it have taken, for you? Would it have taken for you, after something like that, Trickster?! Answer me!"

"Don't be angry, Amphitryon. I'm telling you what I know. And what I know is that one Alcides left Thespius, and the other is on the *Argo* right now. Sailing somewhere near Mysia."

"Have you seen him?" Amphitryon wrapped himself tighter in the woolen peplos, which would easily have made two cloaks for six-year-old boys.

"I saw him... only I can't tell --- which one?! Gloomy, silent the whole time, taken a lover --- a quiet little boy, name of Hylas. They put in at Lemnos --- nothing but women there, no men at all, the local beauties ready to lie down under Pan himself! --- and no, he wouldn't even go ashore, just fussed over this Hylas the whole time. I understand, of course --- it happens... I've got a son myself, by that fool Aphrodite --- Hermaphroditus..."

"You don't understand a thing, god," Amphitryon rasped. "It's simply that boys don't give you children. Ones that can be killed. That's Iphicles... the one on the *Argo*."

"Are you sure? Then why did he call himself Alcides?"

"I'm sure. Each of them blames himself. Alcides --- you know what for, yourself... And Iphicles, saving me, killed two servants of Tartarus at the altar in the very moment of the sacrificial ritual. And now he thinks that was the sacrifice on account of which... in short, Iphicles considers himself guilty. And he knows that as long as the polluted Alcides the madman wanders Hellas, there will be no shortage of men wanting to kill him, to win glory and the gods' favor."

"And this way?"

"And this way --- Alcides sailed off on the *Argo*. Good luck finding him now! He's covering for his brother, that's what..."

"But Thespius cleansed him!"

"And how many people know that?"

They fell silent.

Hermeias, who had been about to refill his cup, changed his mind and took a long swallow straight from the jug.

Then another.

"And what of Zeus?" Amphitryon was the first to break the long pause.

"Dad? Beaming with happiness. And trying his hardest to hide it --- you see what the weather's like?! Well, of course --- what a fine excuse to send a hero off to perform his feats! In atonement. Gave Hera a good thrashing, just for form's sake..."

"Beaming, is he," Amphitryon echoed dully, and Hermeias flinched at the old man's rattle in that child's voice.

"Only Father hasn't decided yet," the Trickster went on, with deliberate cheer, "where's best to send Alcides. Thessaly, maybe, to chase Lapiths and centaurs about, or Mycenae, into service under Eurystheus, Sthenelus's son. Mycenae's serious business, after all, in plain view of everyone; and Father, foolishly, swore an oath once that Eurystheus would be lord over all the Perseids! He must have meant Alcides, of course, and Hera went and rushed the birth of that seven-month Mycenaean brat... well, you know how it is!"

"Mycenae?" Amphitryon seemed to snap awake, then quickly caught himself. "Now, there's a thought... both my boys' grandfathers were lawful Mycenaean wanaxes! Listen, Hermeias, it's pure genius: a hero and a rightful heir, performing feats at the beck of a premature bastard who's seized the throne! Won't Eurystheus be delighted, seeing us in Mycenae!"

Grim irony filled every word of it.

"So, should I suggest to Father that he send the hero to Mycenae?"

"Absolutely!" Amphitryon knocked over his half-finished cup, spilling it onto Hermeias, without even noticing. "What matters to me is that your blustering father not get in our way once we arrive in Mycenae, settle somewhere nearby --- Tiryns, say --- because I've no love for 'accidental' assassination attempts --- and go about performing whatever feats we see fit..."

"Wait, wait!" Hermeias cut him off. "Well, I'll forgive you the 'blustering father' --- but you said 'we'?!"

"Of course, we. Alcides, Iphicles, and me --- Iphicles's son Iolaus, companion of heroes... of the hero. But if you like, you can count yourself into that 'we' as well. But seriously, Hermeias..."

Amphitryon threw off the peplos, as though he'd suddenly grown hot.

"Seriously, though --- I'm sick of being a sacrifice! Sick of it for everyone --- for myself, for my wife, for my sons... is this any kind of life?! It's one continuous altar, and everyone who feels like it crowding around: the Fallen, the Possessed, the Olympians, you and I... spent eighteen years breaking Alcides in for Tartarus, and forging Iphicles into a chain for his brother; then they gave us five years to catch our breath, and, like a drunkard, held a cup of wine right under our nose --- and snatched it away! What happens to the drunkard, Trickster?"

"He turns into a beast," Hermeias answered mechanically. "And tries to snatch the cup back."

"Exactly. So why be surprised that my Alcides lost his mind and finished the interrupted sacrifice, killing the children and setting the house on fire?! Oh, no --- we had nothing to do with it, but you, madman, off you go and atone!.."

"He offered the children up to himself as a sacrifice?" the Trickster asked quietly.

"Not knowing what he was doing!" Amphitryon cut in sharply.

"So the Fallen got what they wanted?"

"Almost, Hermeias. Almost."

* * *

And in the mind of the ever-young god in winged sandals, one word burned like a freshly seared brand.

Sacrifices.

There --- grandfather Kronus the Timekeeper sacrifices great-grandfather Uranus, gelding his own father in the name of power yet to come; there --- father Zeus, Dias the High-Thundering, sacrifices grandfather Kronus and great-grandmother Gaia the Earth in the name of victory over the Fallen; there --- mother Maia Pleiades sacrifices the infant Hermeias for the sake of her Olympian lover (she'd gladly have called him husband, if only she could), abandoning the newborn in a grotto on Mount Cyllene; there --- he himself, Hermeias the Trickster, stands ready to sacrifice the twins in the interests of the Family, just as Hera the stepmother, not long ago, stood ready to sacrifice her pregnant rival, mother of these same two brothers; there --- Amphitryon the lawagetas sacrifices himself, raising his hand against Ares Enyalius in the battle at Orchomenus...

Sacrifices --- himself.

Himself.

The one human sacrifice that does not feed Tartarus.

The one offering unreachable to an immortal, incapable of sacrificing himself.

For the first time, Hermeias thought that even the gods might be flawed.

* * *

"What's wrong with you, Trickster? Hey, snap out of it!"

"Alcides is dangerous," the Trickster whispered tonelessly, not hearing Amphitryon. "He's dangerous... and I love him. Lawagetas, remember my words, and never repeat them! --- tthese children aren't just yours! Chiron is right: I'm a bad god... a very bad one. What am I to do, lawagetas?"

"You're a fine god," Amphitryon smiled. "The best I know, at any rate. So I'll let you in on a terrible secret --- parents don't kill their sick sons. They heal them!"

"They what?" Hermeias marveled.

"Heal them! What, you don't know what that means?!"

"Not really," the Trickster admitted. "I've... I've never been sick with anything, you see."

"Never mind, that can be fixed! After all, we've got feats ahead of us, and that's a fine medicine for the sick and a fine sickness for the healthy! And as for those who made human sacrifices to Alcides --- let's see how they like our boy in the role of a priest! Where did you say the *Argo* is now?"

"Near Mysia."

"Fly there and fetch Iphicles. Drag him by the ears if you have to! --- I want him here by tomorrow. Or hide him somewhere close by and come back for me. It seems to me the time has come to release Alcides from the oath he swore me five years ago!"

"Swore you?" the Trickster didn't understand.

"Well, yes! Me, of course! What, don't you remember? --- I just died then, at Orchomenus..."

Left alone, the boy shivered with cold, gathered up the crumpled peplos, and wrapped himself in the scratchy wool, trying not to let his teeth chatter from the chill.

All the confident strength that had just been ringing in him, sending the Trickster soaring into the sky and vanishing into the funnel of an opening Dromos, had been washed away as if by rain.

"Ah, Hermeias, Hermeias." The boy shook the empty jug regretfully and, failing to wring even a single drop out of it, gave it a spiteful kick. "You shouldn't have gotten yourself tangled up with us, friend! Learn to love --- and next thing you know, you'll learn to die too... Strange: such a father, and such a son! You must take after your grandfather, Trickster --- after Atlas the Sky-Bearer... oh, it's cold! No, I won't go home --- that Cretan bridegroom of Alcmene's is sitting there... well, let him sit, I'll go for a walk. With any luck, I won't catch cold!.. I'll wander a while, walk about..."

The boy got up and set to pacing back and forth with a rolling gait, dragging the hem of the peplos through the mud.

"And Eurystheus," he muttered, barely moving lips gone blue with cold, "well, let there be Eurystheus... The main thing is that his orders and our wishes fit in the same bed! Though if some blessed whim does happen to cross his mind, we'll carry that out too. I only hope our little Mycenaean bastard doesn't take to whims too often... or there's a nasty little spot in the next world --- the Isles of the Blessed, they call it..."
