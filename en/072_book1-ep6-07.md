#### 7

Today Iphicles had gotten too absorbed correcting the throw --- first for the discoboloi, then for the wrestlers (though in the first case the students were hurling a flat discus, and in the second, each other) --- and so he did not at once notice his son's disappearance.

In fact, he might not have noticed at all, since of late he'd grown used to Iolaus's independence, but a youth came running from the armory asking after young Iphiclid --- and it turned out the boy was nowhere to be found.

"Ah, what a shame!" the youth said, crestfallen. "And he told me --- come by the armory in the afternoon, we'll fit you a shield to your arm! Turns out he lied, then..."

"Fit you a what?" Iphicles echoed, surprised.

"A shield. Polycles keeps catching me in the chest with his spear every time, so I went to Iolaus, and he says the shield strap's set crooked, that's why I'm holding it too far out. We agreed to meet at the armory --- and your Iolaus never showed up!"

The students all started talking at once, discussing Iolaus's various pieces of advice (which, it turned out, he handed out quite freely), and when the astonished Iphicles asked whether they knew how old his son was, only one answered with any confidence --- and even he grew embarrassed at once, as though he'd never once stopped to think about it before.

Shrugging, Iphicles left the students with his assistant (that same Polyctor from the palaestra's first graduating class, with whom he'd traded more than a few childhood blows) and went to look for his son.

Iolaus was not at the palaestra.

Cursing his firstborn's peculiarities under his breath, Iphicles headed home, where he threw Alcmene and Asteropeia into no small alarm, since the women had no idea where Iolaus was wandering either.

"Your little dreamer won't come to harm," the willful Megara tossed off, stepping out of the gynaeceum. "His grandfather will help him from beyond the grave. Now if one of mine went missing, no one would even notice! You'd all just go to bed, I expect. Someone ought to watch their children better..."

Iphicles shot Creon's elder daughter a furious look --- and she fell silent. A little over four years ago, when Megara, on the pretext of not being able to tell the twins apart, had tried to climb into Iphicles's bed, the two of them had had a serious talk.

But not in bed, as buxom Megara had hoped.

Since then, only Iphicles had ever managed to tame that mare. And even that was only because, after the incident, she'd tried to slander Iphicles in Alcides's eyes --- except this time she'd genuinely mixed up the brothers and complained to the wrong one.

For which she had been soundly beaten.

And to this day she still couldn't work out --- by which one?

"Where's Alcides?" Iphicles asked.

Megara tossed her mane of carefully combed hair contemptuously and walked off without answering.

"A right little gift from the basileus!" Alcmene nearly blurted out.

"He's at Creon's," Asteropeia said, laying a reassuring hand on her husband's shoulder.

Like a swallow alighting on the edge of a cliff.

"He's at Creon's. A messenger arrived for Father from Euboea, from Eurytus of Oechalia --- so Alcides went to hear the news. They say Iphitus the archer, your old teacher, isn't getting on well with his father. Something to do with Iole, his five-year-old sister --- whether old Eurytus is beating her, or something else..."

Iphicles didn't wait to hear more; he snatched his cloak off the wall and bolted from the house.

The thin rain cooled him a little, and Iphicles began running circles through the surrounding streets, looking sharply from side to side and calling his son's name every so often.

Half an hour of fruitless searching later, he was caught up by the breathless youth --- the very one Iolaus had promised to fit a shield to.

"Teacher! We, um... well, we broke off the lesson! But Polyctor gave us leave, don't get me wrong!.."

"Get to the point!" Iphicles barked, automatically noting the youth's poorly trained breathing.

"Well, it's... we're looking for your Iolaus, run ourselves ragged... and..."

"Did you find him?!"

"Yes and no!"

"Yes or no?!"

"No! But we did find Phocus the drunk... you know, the one who's always hanging around the market!.."

Iphicles barely stopped himself from cuffing the chattering lout across the neck.

And a good thing he hadn't --- he realized a moment later.

"Phocus saw your Iolaus! With some big fellow! He says your Iolaus put two little fish in the bowl of the blind rhapsode --- you know, the one who's always singing Hermes's praises! --- and the big fellow hurried him along, and off they went!"

"Where to?!"

"Phocus says toward the Cadmea, toward the northern edge... Don't get me wrong, teacher --- Phocus was sober! Well, almost... Wait! Wait, teacher! I'm coming with you!.."

No such luck! --- the hopelessly lagging youth could only shake his head in admiration as Iphicles tore off and plunged into the strengthening rain, vanishing behind its shimmering veil.

And Nephele, goddess of clouds, went on milking her heavenly cattle...

* * *

Past the last of the huts, and bearing left of the Cadmea into the hills overgrown with yellowing tamarisk, Iphicles meant to make a wide circle around the fortress. The city was spreading southward, and the Cadmea, once the inner citadel, was becoming more of an outer northern bastion these days --- but that was the very last thing on Iphicles's mind right now. For some ten minutes already he had been winding along the sodden path, shouting his son's name at the top of his voice, when he suddenly slipped and fell into the mud.

Swearing, he made to spring up --- but stayed down, turning his head to the right.

Had he imagined it?

It seemed not... on the crest of one of the distant hills, a timid, absurd little spark of firelight glimmered --- invisible from the height of an ordinary man.

There was neither time nor inclination to dwell on that oddity.

Turning himself into a soundless shadow, and gratefully recalling Pan's lessons on Cithaeron, Iphicles began working his way toward the fateful hill, trying to approach from downwind; though there's the rain... no, better not to take chances.

For some reason it never even crossed his mind that the fire might simply belong to hunters or ordinary vagrants passing the time.

Already crawling toward the stone steps --- which had somehow ended up atop the hill, and looked like the chipped jaws of some monster --- Iphicles understood why.

Because the slick rain, groping over his body with hundreds of cold fingers, had begun to smell of mildew.

For five years --- an entire eternity --- Iphicles Amphitryad, Alcides's brother, had not felt that smell.

And the words that now reached him from the hollow beyond the steps, from the reeking maw of the dead monster, he had never heard before at all.

"...will surge forth as a river of blood, from mouth to source, from the Fallen unto the Hero; we offer up this sacrifice unto Alcides, scion of great Kronus, grandson of the Master of Time... ancestors, hear us, answer!.."

"We hear Tartarus," two low voices howled dully. "We hear, our fathers... soon now!.. soon..."

And the notched blade in the dwarfish old woman's hand trembled, forgetting to fall, hanging like a predatory beak over the six-year-old boy stretched out on the altar, as Iphicles, making no attempt to hide, strode into the maw of the shrine, tearing off his soaked-through cloak.

"So it was tummies you healed?" --- he seemed not to notice the two stocky men advancing on the uninvited guest. "So it was the Eileithyiai you chased from mother's bedside, was it?.. Rejoice, Galinthias, daughter of Proetus --- it's time to die!"

"Whose?!" the dwarf-woman, twisting like a snake, bared her teeth right in the newcomer's face. "Whose time to die, hero?! Here he is, your little son, an innocent child --- and here's the knife... the knife's thirsty, worn out with waiting! Oh, I'm afraid I won't be able to hold it back --- it'll come down! You just stand there quiet, hero, let us tie up those hands of yours --- good and tight, mind, or you'll snap right through them in your fury!"

"You are blind, Galinthias, daughter of Proetus..."

It was the boy speaking.

Straining to lift his head, which kept helplessly falling back, little Iolaus was smiling --- and a lump rose in Iphicles's throat when he saw that terrible smile on his child's face.

Like flame cupped in a palm.

"You are blind, Tartarus-Possessed! You look, but you do not see... it isn't Iphicles before you, but Alcides the madman, not Iolaus's father, but his uncle! Bring the knife down on the sacrifice, old priestess of the Fallen, let the blade drink, let the Fallen enter Alcides, if only for a moment! And consider: how long will you outlive your sacrifice?! Remember Linus the kitharode, foolish daughter of Proetus, remember the Minyan envoys; remember the battle at Orchomenus!.."

And before the startled old woman could collect herself, the boy suddenly thrashed like a caught fish, striking Galinthias's knife-hand from the side with his bound feet.

"They're picking on the little ones!" the furious cry cracked like a ringing whip.

These were the only words capable of tearing Iphicles from where he stood and hurling him forward.

It was just as well that his students from the palaestra couldn't see their teacher now, because Iphicles Amphitryad had never taught them what Hermeias the Trickster, patron of athletes and thieves, had once taught the twins.

His students would have sworn that Alcides himself stood before them, seized by madness!

The nearest attendant's twisted knee cracked dryly; a bronze grip clamped around the second man's wrist, and Iphicles's palm drove into the wretch's armpit like the blade of a broad dagger, choking the half-born scream in his swollen throat; twice the emptied altar met the twisted face of one victim or the other, as if trying to splash itself into a bloody bas-relief, and on the ground writhed a wheezing snake that had been a man before his spine was broken --- while the rain kept pouring, indifferent to it all.

A slight little body slipped past and suddenly stumbled mid-flight, fell onto the stone steps, writhing like a weasel caught in a snare --- was Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, about to gnaw off her own leg?!

"Halt!" --- it was as if Iphicles had been dragged back by the ears from the old woman, the sacrificial knife jutting from her thigh. "Halt, son! I need her alive!.."

"Yes, dad," Iphicles answered automatically, and turned, paling with belated understanding.

Amphitryon Perseid stood by the altar. And it did not matter that he wore Iolaus's face, that he barely came up to Iphicles's waist, that the arm with the frayed scrap of rope still around its wrist was thin and slight --- that arm had just unerringly thrown a knife after the fleeing old woman; it was with this very voice that Amphitryon used to shout at his negligent twins in years past, and it was this very gaze that haunted Iphicles's nights, when he dreamed of the boiling Cephissus, the broken crossing, and the fourth arrow he'd come up short of.

Iphicles looked at his son --- and saw his father.

* * *

...Still alive is Minos of Crete, and Triptolemus from Eleusis, and the pious, righteous Aeacus of the isle of Aegina; alive is wise Rhadamanthys the Lawgiver, who even now, exiled from his homeland, is journeying to Boeotia, where before long he will become Alcmene's second husband; and alive, too, is Autolycus Hermesid, whose grandson (like Iolaus, a grandson of Amphitryon) will be astonishingly like his grandfather --- a great wrestler, a trickster and a killer, an oathbreaker and a devoted friend, by the name of Odysseus, meaning "The one who angers the gods"...

Alive are all those to whom Anax Hades will, after their death, leave their memory, making them his judges, his counselors, his scouts and his confidants, breaking his own law to do it.

But the first, all the same, was Amphitryon-Iolaus, who would not, for a long while yet, be called Protesilaus --- "Iolaus the First."

* * *

"You know how to keep silent," Amphitryon said, in a tone that brooked no argument. "I know you do."

"Yes, dad," Iphicles repeated, blinking uselessly at the acrid raindrops clouding his eyes. "I know how to keep silent. Only... I didn't know you'd come back."

And at these words old Galinthias tipped her face back to the sky, like a sacrifice upon the altar, and let out a doomed howl.
