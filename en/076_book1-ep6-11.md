#### 11

This ragged holy fool came to sacred Delphi from the east.

A tall man with a wild mane of hair, of more than sturdy build, he kept glancing ceaselessly about him, hunching over and pulling the back of his head down into his broad shoulders, as though all three Erinyes at once were after him --- the avenging sisters Alecto, Tisiphone, and Megaera, born from the drops of blood of the gelded Uranus.

And the holy fool's inflamed eyes blinked in fright.

In flourishing Delphi there would always be work --- and with it a reliable flatbread or two a day --- for so hulking a slave, should the holy fool ever choose to sell himself to some fortunate merchant or skilled craftsman. The city grew richer by the day, and the people of Delphi never failed to thank silver-bowed Apollo the Far-Shooter every hour for the fact that it was here, at the crossroads of many paths near proud Parnassus and not far from the Gulf of Corinth, that the sun-god had struck down the dreadful Python and founded the Delphic oracle.

That the oracle had been seized by force — from Hestia, or perhaps from Hera the Stepmother herself — people preferred not to mention.

A better spot for a trading city with a harbor at Kirra would have been hard to imagine --- otherwise Phocis, the western region centered on Delphi, would forever have remained the stepdaughter of far more fertile Boeotia.

But as it was --- glory through the ages to silver-bowed Phoebus-Apollo!

In Delphi itself, however, the holy fool lingered no more than half a day at best, almost at once merging with the crowd of the latest pilgrims and heading for the oracle.

There the ragged man walked briskly along the paved road from the main gate to the temple entrance, not pausing even for a moment at the eastern rocky wall, where the divine water of the Castalian Spring, meant for the pilgrims' washing, ran down into a stone niche. Nor did the strange visitor pay any attention to the temple's many treasuries, terraced along the southern side; he remained just as indifferent to the statues lining the road, and to the other beauties wrought by the great architects Agamedes and Trophonius, Apollo's favorites.

The holy fool didn't even try to enter the temple itself, but instead sat down cross-legged by the monument to Phoebus the Radiant, towering beside the marble temple steps, and there he sat until evening came.

At sunset, one of the senior priestesses --- a commanding woman of middle years --- approached the man with the eyes of a spent horse.

"You must leave the grounds of the sacred precinct," she said, kindly but firmly. "If you like, come back tomorrow."

The holy fool got up without a word and shuffled off down the road.

"Mute," the priestess thought, filling with an inexplicable sympathy for this powerful, still quite young man, so terribly maimed by fate.

"Wait!" She caught up with the holy fool and fell into step beside him. "Who are you? What's your name? If you can't speak, show me with signs!"

The holy fool shrugged without stopping.

In her lifetime the priestess had seen no shortage of pilgrims: those who came to offer up prayers, those wishing to be cleansed of miasma, and mere idle onlookers; she had seen gawkers, fanatics, fratricides, and repentant brigands --- but none had ever roused such interest in her as today's visitor.

"He looks like a mad god," a blasphemous thought seared the priestess. "Or rather, like a fallen titan. Olympian strength, with the mark of Tartarus burned into his soul! Gods, what a pity..."

"If you want to stay," she offered, surprised at herself, "there are abandoned buildings beyond the wall, off to the left. During the Pythian Games, the rhapsodes and kitharodes who come to compete lodge there. But that happens only once a year; the rest of the time, no one lives there."

The holy fool nodded without the slightest sign of gratitude and quickened his pace.

The next day he was sitting again at the feet of the marble Phoebus.

Pilgrims who came to the oracle for a prophecy walked around him and went on, taking the seated figure for part of the temple pavilion. The holy fool's stillness truly was akin to the stillness of a statue; Apollo's attendants, too, grew used to him, and when, on the tenth day, he stayed within the sacred precinct overnight, everyone took it calmly, as a matter of course.

A loaded cart would get stuck in a pothole --- the holy fool would put his shoulder to it, and the wheel would be freed from its trap in an instant; a landslide would threaten to bury Cassotis, Delphi's second sacred spring --- the holy fool would work like a man possessed, hauling boulders so large that the male priests could only exchange glances; some buildings had fallen into disrepair --- he would lay roofing, dig in support posts...

They fed him.

They tried to talk to him; or rather, they tried to get him talking --- without success.

More than once the young priestesses spied on the newcomer while he bathed, and more than once, too, they made attempts to lure this magnificent male into the thickets --- Phoebus Apollo, himself no small philanderer, looked favorably on amusements of that kind, unlike his sister Artemis the virgin --- but women, it seemed, held no interest whatsoever for the holy fool.

In revenge, the priestesses nicknamed him Bridegroom, Hera's Favorite --- Hera, patroness of marriage --- insisting that the mark of the Thunderer's wife lay upon Bridegroom, who was being kept by her for herself. Apollo's priestesses generally allowed themselves great liberties where Gold-Sandaled Hera was concerned --- the rascals knew perfectly well that Apollo Musagetes could not stand his jealous stepmother, never having forgiven Zeus's wife for persecuting his own mother Leto, who had fled with two infant gods in her arms.

And so a new name settled onto the holy fool --- Heracles.

The Marked Hero.

"Hey, Heracles, run to the storeroom for a sack of dried apricots!"

"Heracles! Where've you gotten to?! Get over here, help brace the gate hinges!"

"Where's Heracles? Old Thrasybulus has taken to his bed, we need to carry him to the healer, and a carcass like that, even three of us together can barely..."

And no one knew that by night the holy fool crept, like a soundless ghost, into the innermost part of the temple, where neither pilgrims nor junior priestesses were admitted --- where only the Pythia the prophetess, having drunk water from Cassotis and chewed laurel leaves, would enter and take her seat on the golden tripod above the cleft in the rock, breathing in the pungent mist that rose from below, the mist that bred visions of the future.

The holy fool would sit down at the edge of the cleft, having pushed the tripod aside, and gaze for hours into the churning, bottomless chasm.

In those minutes he truly resembled a mad god, peering with a detached smile into the Tartarus of his own soul.

Sometimes he would laugh quietly --- this would happen when two languid serpents crawled out of the haze to his feet, licking Heracles's hairy shins with forked tongues and jerking their heads oddly, as though the serpents' necks had been broken.

Following the serpents, a lean man of about forty would approach the holy fool from the direction of the chasm, clean-shaven in the Mycenaean fashion, carrying a kithara and a forked teacher's staff. Setting the staff aside, the visitor would sit down nearby and, tuning the instrument, run his fingers soundlessly over the strings; and the kitharode's head, like the heads of the ghostly serpents, kept tilting to one side, as though a broken neck refused to bear its weight.

Time passed, and more and more shadowy figures gathered around the silent Heracles: an enormous lion baring its sharp fangs, several warriors in dented armor plainly forged in Orchomenian smithies, five boys between one and four years old, their faces charred black...

And before dawn the holy fool would rise, turn his back on the abyss, and leave, only to return again the next night.

In the third month of these nightly vigils, just as he was about to rise and leave his accustomed spot, the holy fool saw someone on the far edge of the cleft.

And he lingered, peering into the mist.

Sitting across from him was himself.

"Are you me?" Heracles asked.

The words came together poorly, unfamiliarly.

Of late he had grown unused to thinking in words.

"I am you," the double answered.

"Then why are you there --- and I here? It seems to me that this isn't right."

"That was your own choice."

"I chose nothing. It happened by itself."

"Not true. On the side where you are, there are only sacrifices. So you're a sacrifice too."

"And you?"

"And he isn't," a stranger's voice answered.

Beside the double stood a boy. A wrong sort of boy. He stood the way men no longer young stand, and answered the way old men answer --- dryly and mercilessly.

"And who are you?" The Marked Hero wasn't even surprised to hear himself speaking aloud. "Are you a sacrifice or not?!"

"No, though I could have been one."

"Then who are you?"

"Your father."

"You can't be my father. You're little. Please --- go away... don't hurt me."

"You're the little one. And the little ones always get picked on. No, I'm the father of a grown son... a son who swam to me through a raging Cephissus."

"Did I really have a father?"

"If you're sitting on that side --- no. If you still want to think of yourself as a sacrifice, hiding from the past and from yourself, the way you used to hide from madness --- then you have no father, and never had one. Which means I fought a god for you for nothing."

Something was stealthily coming to a boil in Heracles, something long forgotten and buried at the very bottom of his soul, beneath a layer of stupefying fumes, forcing him to tear away, with blood, the crust of oblivion dried onto the wound; and he bit his lip, glaring with hatred at the double and the aged boy.

The hatred was searing and curative, like a healer's knife.

"I have... I had a father," each word cut into raw flesh, and it took great effort not to break into a shout. "You're lying, both of you! I remember... no, I don't remember, but it seems to me that once I swore by his name! I had a father --- do you hear?!"

"No."

"I had a father! And... and I also had a brother! He stood there with a stone in his hands, so I could get up off the ground! Do you hear?! I had a brother!.."

"No," the double cut him off, and the aged boy nodded in agreement. "If you're a sacrifice, then you had no brother, because a sacrifice is always alone. Sacrifices don't get up off the ground --- which is why they need no brother."

Heracles got to his feet.

He stood on the edge of the smoking abyss, head lowered like a bull's, fists clenched; and the mist that bred visions of the future parted and drew back in fear before a living future standing, ominous, on the very edge of the past.

"I'll kill you," Heracles flung at the double. "Leave, or I'll kill you."

"Me?" the other marveled. "You'll kill me? But you've already done that, when you killed yourself! Remember: 'I am you'?"

"I am you?" The pain was utterly unbearable. "Is that true?"

"It's true," the boy-father answered in the double's place. "If you don't remember that it's true, then every sacrifice was in vain. All of them --- the ones made to you, and the ones you made yourself... and you yourself --- because you'll live out your days as the son of Zeus, a sacrifice who never had a real father or a real brother. Olympus, Tartarus, the Possessed --- if you stay sitting on that side, nursing your madness, it means *they* were right to sacrifice a man to their whims. Your great-grandfather Perseus killed Medusa..."

"At the gods' command!"

"No! He simply could no longer bear to see marble statues in place of living people! And I didn't hunt the Teumessian fox for the sake of Creon's fields, damn them --- I was afraid to look into the eyes of the mothers of the young men who were sacrificed every month to appease the Beast! And I didn't name you Alcides --- meaning the Strong One --- so that you could wallow in oblivion and suffering among the shades!"

"I have a name?!"

"Yes. You have a name. Jump, Alcides!"

And he flung himself across the cleft.

* * *

...toward the hands stretched out to him.

...toward memory, taking it into himself as one takes a spear in the frenzy of battle --- as deep as possible, impaling oneself on the shaft with a growl, to reach the enemy half a heartbeat before death.

...toward his father and his brother.

...toward the miasma that no one else has the power to cleanse; not a god, not even oblivion.

...toward fate, toward Ananke the Inevitability, whom one does not choose --- whom those who have forgotten how to choose, do not choose.

...through the mildew-smelling mist.

...and stood on the other side.

* * *

"Is life starting over?" asked Alcides, breathing hard. "Tell me yes --- and I won't believe you."

"No," answered Amphitryon, looking up at his son from below. "Life goes on, and it isn't always a gift. I know that better than most."

"Will we go back to Thebes?" asked Alcides, drawing his brows together. "Tell me yes --- and I won't go with you."

"No," answered Iphicles, putting an arm around his brother's shoulders. "We're going to Mycenae, to Eurystheus Sthenelid --- our first cousin once removed, and our age-mate. And then --- to Tiryns, where we'll wait for orders. Zeus assigned us ten years of cleansing in service to this premature lord over all the Perseids --- well, that's not much... Ten years for five children --- two years apiece!.. forgive me, brother, I've lost the knack for joking lately. It happens when there are too many heroes around, like on the *Argo*!"

"Yes, Iphicles. Ten years is a very short time. Vanishingly short for a cleansing --- the cleansing of Hellas from the miasma of human sacrifice! But I... but we'll try. We'll try very hard. Won't we?"

"We will, Alcides. We'll try very hard. After all, it'll be easier for us than for other heroes --- a hero stands alone, and there's still three of us... not counting Dummy."

"And Chiron."

"And Chiron. Let's get out of here, Alcides! There's a bypass path, and Hermeias promised to wait for us outside."

"Let's go. Only... what about the oath I swore at Orchomenus?"

"I release you from it," Amphitryon said, with the faintest of smirks. "You swore by your father, didn't you? I'm glad your memory's come back to you, Alcides, but from now on, hold off on oaths, all right? Or swear the way Autolycus does --- knowing a safe way to break the oath beforehand! Don't tell me all you learned from Autolycus was how to break other men's necks."

"No, Father. I also learned from him how not to break my own. Shall we test it?"

"We'll test it. Once I've grown up a bit, son, we'll test it."

"And more than once," Iphicles added, glancing back at the smoking chasm.

It seemed that, sailing with the Argonauts, he really had lost the knack for joking.
