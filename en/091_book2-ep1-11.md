#### 11

The white, richly maned horse --- a tall beauty with finely turned pasterns and a swan's neck --- crunched juicily on its oats, its Roman-nosed head bent to the tripod, caring not at all for its own fate.

And the sky over Euboea was tenderly transparent, like the gaze of an oread in love.

Fate, though --- firstborn Ananke the Inevitability, who (so they say, for who has ever seen her!) stands above men, gods, and horses alike --- stood nearby and smiled serenely in a way that all the Sphinxes, from sunrise to sunset, begrudge with black envy; and doomed glints of light fell upon the glossy rump, the pride of Oechalia's herds, upon the two small silvered tripods --- no longer holding oats, of course, but spring water and Thasian wine for the washing of hands and the solemn libation --- upon the ceremonial mantle the color of an autumn wheat field, with a blood-crimson border along its edge...

"My stomach's bloated," Alcides complained quietly, hunching his broad shoulders against a chill; and ripples ran across the ripe wheat, across the gifted pharos his father-in-law-to-be had sent Heracles that very morning. "The pork they cook here --- can't eat any more, yet still do! With wild garlic, barberry, and nuts... should I just kill the cook, or what?"

"You're about to make a sacrifice to Apollo," Iphicles said instructively, never one for fatty meat. "And after that --- get married. So hold your tongue and think elevated thoughts."

"I can't" --- and the tormented grimace twisting Alcides's puffy face made it plain: no, he really couldn't. "Should I go get some water? Or... what do you think, Iphicles?"

The courtyard was packed tighter than peas in a pod; the rulers and their sons crowded closer to the yellowish, faded steps of local marble, toward the colonnade leading to the anteroom of the megaron, from which Eurytus of Oechalia was due to appear any minute now with his daughter-bride (and kept not appearing), thereby opening the sacrificial ceremony; the rest of the rejected suitors angled to stand near Heracles the victor and the white horse destined for Apollo Aigletes.

Iolaus, sullen and wary, disliked this arrangement for some reason, but couldn't work out why.

An unexpected victory and a looming wedding, the Brotherhood of Salmoneus, the memorable conversation with Eurytus the Possessed, Lichas the reveler gone missing since the night before --- all this was more than enough to keep a rotten aftertaste from leaving his mouth, and to keep the side gashed open in the labyrinth of Crete dully aching; he badly wanted to vanish from Euboea (with the absurdly won bride or without her, it hardly mattered) and end up anywhere at all, though preferably in Tiryns, under the protection of its mighty towers, known to him by every chipped battlement, and walls five orgyiai thick.

He sighed hopelessly and turned to the twins.

The two of them, it seemed, had been arguing about something for a while now and couldn't come to an agreement.

"Iolaus, dear fellow," the miserable Alcides whispered, pained, "you tell him, at least! Let him stand in for me... I'll be quick, no one will even notice! Eurytus is taking his time anyway --- what with this and that and the other... In the end, what does Apollo care which of us slits the horse's throat for him, me or Iphicles?!"

"Oh, all right, glutton, go relieve yourself," Iphicles relented, swapping mantles with his brother unnoticed by those around them. "Just hurry, or you'll let your young wife slip through your fingers... or doesn't it matter to her either, you or me? Should ask, if it comes up..."

Alcides, not listening, sighed with relief and began shouldering his way backward toward the side gallery, meaning to skirt the palace from the rear; Iolaus, meanwhile, stroked the horse, indifferent to the caress --- ah, how the mares of the Oechalian herds would weep, strewing earth upon their manes! --- and, for no particular reason, set off after him.

Already turning the corner, he glanced back in passing --- no, still no one was coming from the megaron... what on earth was keeping Eurytus, really?!

Was his stomach troubling him too?

* * *

Stopping by the small side gate on the east, which opened onto a gentle slope overgrown with festive hyacinth blossoms, Iolaus paused for a moment --- the murmur of the crowd awaiting the start of the rite barely reached him here, a dull, muttering whisper --- and raised his head, idly studying the second-floor terrace, the carved balusters of the railing, the darkened ceiling beams, a rope hanging almost to the ground with its end frayed to shreds...

It was Lichas's rope; and the triple bronze hook glinted dully at the base of the railing, gripping the pliant, fibrous wood like a bird of prey's talon.

"Well then, shall we head back? Or Iphicles will give it to the both of us..."

Alcides slammed the gate loudly, cheerfully threw an arm around Iolaus's waist --- and suddenly fell silent.

"Maybe he climbed up to see some broad in the night," he guessed, without much conviction, "and overslept after his righteous labors?"

"Lichas?" was all Iolaus said. Alcides, lowering his head like a bull, tugged at the rope --- the hook held fast --- gauged the distance to the railing, and with one powerful heave flung his body almost up to the terrace itself, catching hold with his left hand beside the hook in a flash. Then, still hanging there, he pulled himself up, peered at the terrace floor invisible to Iolaus below, and swung himself over the creaking railing.

"There's blood here," he growled dully from above. "Here... and here."

The rope burned his palms, and the side that had been aching since morning suddenly let go --- that always happened whenever a premonition turned real --- and Iolaus, climbing up onto the upper terrace, also saw the dried brown stain on the outermost baluster.

Scratching at it with a fingernail --- dirty flakes crumbled silently to the floor --- Iolaus wrenched the hook free, coiled the rope into a loop, and turned to Alcides.

Alcides was gone. Gone was the hired dock porter, the reveler who'd gambled away his money at dice, the good-natured brawler and lover of pork with wild garlic and barberry; half a year of idleness had plunged into some unimaginable abyss, crashing down from ledge to ledge, and Iolaus had only one thing left, the last, familiar thing: to cover Heracles's back, and hope that Iphicles, if it came to that, would hold the way to the gates...

"He'll hold it," Heracles tossed over his shoulder with certainty, and with a short shove flung wide the door leading into the inner chambers.

Iolaus had seen him like this only once before: in Thrace, during the battle with the warlike Bistones, when the accursed man-eating mares tore apart sixteen-year-old Abderus, Hermeias's youngest son, who had been guarding them; and Heracles had smashed his way through the ranks of spearmen in their peaked felt caps, hearing the terrible scream of the dying boy, trampling the living and the dead, and not making it, not making it, not making it in time...

It was then, two days later, that he wouldn't let Iolaus drive off the stray boy Lichas who had attached himself to them.

"This is my own little Hermes..."

The palace turned itself inside out, collapsing upon them in corridors and empty rooms, flinging open the sleeves of its dim passageways, hurling underfoot floorboards that seemed ready to burst into sobs, scrambling in a mad rush to confuse, to bewilder, to keep them from the innermost place, the very heart of it; "Mold..." Heracles mutters twice, without stopping, "mold..." --- and Ananke the Inevitability watches with his furious eyes threaded with red branching veins, breathes with his even, soundless breath of a beast on the trail, clenches his fingers into stone boulders of fists; emptiness crumbles in that deadly grip, the walls flinch away to either side, paling with faded frescoes, the noise of the crowd now recedes, now surges close again, rolling in like surf and shattering into the spray of separate, agitated cries --- then suddenly it grows light, and, dodging a jug hurled at his head, Iolaus understands that Heracles has just killed a man.

A man who has been standing beside the bound Lichas.

The jug shatters to pieces against the doorpost, spraying small, sharp shards in every direction; it flashes across his mind in a single image: snarling Heracles tearing the ropes off Lichas, spread-eagled across some strange, squat altar, ropes upon ropes, far too many for such a scrawny boy with a gag in his mouth; a corpse with its throat crushed has slumped heavily across Lichas's legs, and behind them all --- Heracles, Lichas, and the unknown dead man --- the balcony is visible, the heads of people below in the courtyard, the sacrificial tripods, the white flame of the nervously prancing horse, and another flame, golden-purple, and above Iphicles's mantle his eyes have turned to stone, only the eyes, faceless, turned toward Iolaus... no, not toward Iolaus, but toward the colonnade before the megaron, above which the balcony stands; a swift black shadow slashes across everything he sees, his body answers on its own, out of habit and indifference --- and, tipping the cracked weight off his knee onto the floor, Iolaus understands that he, too, has just killed a man.

A man who lunged from the balcony toward the door into the corridor.

A moment later, Iolaus is on the balcony.

Not even noticing that on the way he threw Lichas his rope and hook, which the boy caught with his freed hands and barely managed to snatch away from Heracles in time --- or else Heracles, blind with fury, would surely have torn that innocent rope apart too.

Below, beneath Iolaus --- steps.

Steps the color of old ivory.

On them --- Eurytus of Oechalia.

Alone.

Without his daughter.

And the long arm of the gray-haired giant points accusingly to where, above the rippling wheat field with its blood-red border, burns the furious gaze of Iphicles Amphitryad.

"A father's heart!" the basileus cries, his voice breaking.

"A madman!" the basileus appeals to the gathering. "Accursed by Hera, the goddess of marriage!"

"Shall I give my only daughter to great Heracles?" the basileus inquires, his voice full of feeling.

And at once:

"Shall I give my daughter to the killer of his own firstborn children, and his brother's children besides?! I cannot, Achaeans, grieving in advance for the fate of grandchildren yet unborn! Gods, give a sign! Hearken, immortals..."

Instead of a sign, Lichas lets out a furious shriek behind Iolaus. Turning, Iolaus sees: the boy's numbed legs failed to hold him when he leapt off the table, and his body slid lizard-like across the floor to the door, half his length already out into the corridor; Lichas rears up, sending the hook flying low down the corridor, the rope draws taut as a bowstring for an instant --- and goes slack.

A hoarse, guttural cry, and the sound of footsteps receding down the corridor.

"He got away!" Tears of hatred choke the boy; he thrashes, trying to get up, and can't. "Slipped off, the bastard! They wanted --- they wanted to sacrifice me, the swine! I climbed up to see a broad past midnight, and they got me from behind... and laughed while they did it, the filth! 'Rejoice,' they said, 'you scrawny wretch, what an honor --- straight from the muck into a sacrifice for Heracles himself!'... And I really did start rejoicing, out of sheer despair... at least some use had come of me..."

"Use-e-e!" someone laughs inside Iolaus, rustling like cold ash.

Below, beneath Iolaus --- steps.

Old ivory.

And along the gallery leading from the megaron's anteroom to the steps, a fat little man runs, hurries, rushes toward Eurytus of Oechalia, clutching a torn shoulder with one hand. He stumbles, knocks over some object wrapped in sackcloth, long and narrow, that had until then been standing by a column a step away from the basileus; the sackcloth falls open, and everything inside Iolaus drops away when he sees, at the foot of the column --- a bow.

A massive bow of wood and horn, already strung, as long as the distance from the ground to a tall man's shoulder, its string made of three tightly twisted ox sinews; and a leather quiver with war arrows of oak.

A draft playfully ruffles the arrows' fletching --- gray with a bluish sheen.

The little man, reaching him, all but hangs off the basileus, spraying spittle as he whispers hurriedly into his ear; Eurytus flinches as if burned, shakes off the wounded man, and turns, raising his head.

And sees Iolaus on the balcony.

The white horse whinnies wildly.

The noise in the courtyard chokes; quiet, quiet, quiet...

Everything that should have happened and did not is crammed into a single, impossibly short instant, into an entire lifetime between two heartbeats: here is Alcides beginning to offer praise to Apollo; here is basileus Eurytus announcing his fear of giving his only daughter to the mad killer of his own firstborn; the sacrificial knife plunges secretly into Lichas's chest, gifting Alcides a Tartarus breaking loose within him --- after which neither gods nor men will condemn Eurytus of Oechalia for shooting down a madman of a hero in the courtyard of his own palace before numerous witnesses; those who stood far enough from the raging Heracles and lived.

The heart beats a second time, and what did not happen dies.

Iolaus jumps down.

The marble steps rush up to meet him, jolting hard against his legs; Iolaus nearly falls, by some miracle not crushing the whimpering little informer, but twists aside at the last moment --- and throws his whole body into slamming Eurytus of Oechalia against the shuddering balustrade of the outer gallery.

"Myrtilus the Theban wasn't enough for you?!" --- the voice is dead, someone else's, it tears its way out of his throat like a barbed arrowhead lodged in a wound, with a rasp, with blood, and there is no holding it back now. "Who else goes on the altar, Possessed one?! Myrtilus, Lichas, me? Who, you filth?! Who?!.."

There it is, right up close --- the twisted, wrinkled face, an old man's face, not the benevolent, majestic countenance of one who decides other people's fates; in the wide eyes of the kinsman-that-never-was, fear sloshes in a murky wave, the fear of a trapped animal, the gut-deep howl of frightened flesh, the kind that, once survived, is never forgiven... Hippocoon's sons, who had stood closest to the steps, understanding nothing, hang off Iolaus, hanging on him in a whole pack, a heap of pungent young bodies getting in each other's way, and that is good, because the reason-fogging hatred is coming out in short bursts, like blood from a severed artery, and the Spartans are shouting, and that too is good --- if they're shouting, it means they're alive, it means he managed to hold back; and now the main thing is to manage to hold on...

He managed.

* * *

...They were leaving in silence. Eurytus, having come to his senses, shouted something after them, cursing madmen and violators in a jumble of words for trampling the laws of hospitality and raising a hand against the master of the house; the suitors blinked in bewilderment, stepping aside and trying not to meet anyone's eyes; the trampled little man with the torn shoulder writhed on the steps like an earthworm after rain; the courtyard sand happily soaked up the water and wine spilled from the sacrificial tripods overturned in the chaos; the white horse snorted, eyeing the scattered oats; they were leaving in silence, like a beast to its den.

To answer?

To justify?

Pointless.

Dike the Justice, daughter of blind, venal Themis, was as mad as Heracles.

Who would believe it?

They were leaving in silence, unafraid of a blow to the back; unafraid of anything, anymore.

And so they did not see (nor did anyone else) how a man who had appeared from who knew where --- the same man Iolaus and Lichas had first met in the harbor of Oropus, where he had watched with a smile as the twins hired themselves out as porters --- how this man, refusing to mix with the crowd the way oil refuses to mix with water, approaches the balustrade, bends down, and, unnoticed by the rest, picks up the massive bow of wood and horn, broken in the scuffle; picks it up and looks at it the way one looks at an old friend not seen in a long while.

The frayed ends of the twisted bowstring swing limply.

"Myrtilus the Theban?" the strange man asks the broken pieces, tonelessly, as though they owed him an answer. "Well, well..."

And he tosses the broken bow back down.

* * *

...They were leaving in silence; only Lichas tried to snap back, but Iphicles, who never once asked a single question, would touch the boy's elbow, and Lichas would fall quiet.

Only much later, down below, once the terracotta rooftiles of Eurytus's palace had finally vanished from sight, did Alcides speak.

"Dad," Alcides said, barely audible, "I can't go on living with this. Maybe that's enough --- of living?"

Lichas glanced back angrily at the rocky cliffs of Euboea, then looked up at the sky, clearly addressing Alcides's incomprehensible words there, upwards; the boy was shaking, wiping cold sweat from his brow again and again --- just now Lichas looked very much like that scrawny waif of six years back, the one who, by sheer chance alone, had never quite made it into the jaws of Diomedes's fanged mares.

"We left the club back there," Lichas jerked his tousled head backward, and his voice broke into a sob, "at those bastards'... And the bride... too bad."

"What's worse --- the club or the bride?" Alcides asked, without a trace of a smirk.

"The club's worser," Lichas answered honestly, and sniffled. "Brides, now --- there's plenty around..."
