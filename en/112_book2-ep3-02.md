#### 2

The city is burning.

Rich Iolcus is burning --- Iolcus, which had never before let an enemy within its walls.

Heracles's driver Iolaus is running through burning Iolcus; Amphitryon the lawagetas, risen from the dead, is running through burning Iolcus.

One man runs --- his own grandfather and grandson.

Taut serpents of smoke crawl along the streets, as though not daring to soar freely into the sky; tongues of flame lick greedily at their prey; the brave and proud die on the thresholds of their homes, the fearful hide in secret cellars; bareheaded women tear themselves out of their captors' clutching hands, only inflaming the laughing victors with their resistance; the soles of sandals sewn with little bronze plates thunder on the planks of the wharf from which, long, long ago, the twenty-five-oared *Argo* set sail for Colchis --- and here it is, the Golden Fleece, right beside you, within arm's reach, loot to your heart's content, and no need to sail anywhere!..

The city is burning.

A man is running through the streets, and even the hottest heads dare not step into his path, having barely glanced into Iolaus's frenzied eyes --- better for a hot head to cool off a little now than for the whole man to go cold afterward!

Unrolling beneath his feet are the steps of the basileus's palace, the galleries of the ground floor, the terraces fenced in by railings...

A woman's shriek.

Iolaus stops dead in his tracks.

He has heard this shriek before.

Three soldiers are dragging a wailing woman away from a huddled corpse.

All three wear closed helmets that hide their faces.

Only their gazes burn with lust and greed through the slits.

The woman shrieks, struggling in the death grip of a soldier who has thrown a spotted leopard skin over his armor...

...the wounded animal roared, dropping onto its haunches; there was no time for a second swing, and Iolaus flung the axe at the female's bared snout, seized the accursed cat by the scruff of the neck --- and, as if she were a wrestling opponent in the palaestra, hurled her over himself and away, off toward safety, away from Iole the bride, who had not so much as flinched, and shrieking Laodamia, who had scrambled with both feet up onto the litter as though that could save her...

"Laodamia! Daughter of Basileus Acastus!"

One of the ravishers turns and walks unhurriedly toward Iolaus, without even drawing his sword. Drawing near, he makes a short, imperceptible movement of the shoulder --- and the world explodes in Iolaus's head.

He is lying on the floor, and the fist-fighter stands over him in silence, waiting for Iolaus to get up.

But Iolaus does not get up.

His right foot hooks the fist-fighters's ankle, while the sole of his left strikes sharply at the knee from the side and slightly on a slant. There is a crunch; the soldier cries out and falls --- Iolaus helps him fall a little closer, sits halfway up and drops backward onto the pugilist, arms flung wide.

His elbows clang heavily against the armor; the soldier cries out and groans, but that no longer matters, because Iolaus is already standing with someone else's sword in his hand, waiting for the second attacker.

Why is he hesitating?!

He toys with his blade pensively, then thrusts it into its scabbard and takes off his helmet.

Before Iolaus stands Castor Dioscurus.

A gray-haired man of sixty.

"Only the late Autolycus, son of Hermes, or Amphitryon the lawagetas of Thebes could have done that to my brother Polydeuces, even aged as he is!" the Laconian laughs, quite sincerely. "Which of them did you study under, hero? Though, judging by your age, you couldn't possibly have studied under Amphitryon at all... So, Autolycus?"

"Rejoice, Castor!" Iolaus tosses the sword to Polydeuces, who has sat up; the sword lands nearby, ringing. "I am Iolaus, Amphitryon's grandson, Heracles's driver!"

"And what else can I say to you, old friend?" he adds in his mind.

"Heard," is all Castor answers.

One word; a precious, iron word.

The third soldier lets go of the girl, who has fallen silent --- for some reason Laodamia does not even think of running away --- and takes off his helmet too.

"Sorry, Iolaus, didn't recognize you in the heat of it," Peleus mumbles guiltily --- a slender, light-haired northerner, nothing like the burly Telamon, his own brother. "Take whatever you want from the spoils, only don't be mad... Agreed?"

Laodamia weeps quietly over her father's body.

That this is the slain Acastus is clear without words.

"What did you kill him for?" Iolaus asks, understanding that he is too late.

"He had it coming," Peleus answers firmly, and it is plain he is barely keeping himself from kicking the corpse. "Set me up, the bastard! I was living at his place, Acastus's, and his wife first tried to climb into my bed, then, when I drove the bitch off, slandered me to her husband! Well, Acastus got angry... the swine got me drunk, abandoned me unarmed on Pelion, and set the centaurs on me too... Thanks to grandpa[^78] Chiron --- helped me fight them off!"

[^78]: Endeis, the daughter of the centaur Chiron, was the mother of Peleus and Telamon.

"Are you sure?"

"Of what?"

"That Acastus believed his wife, that it was he who got you drunk, that he set the centaurs on you?! Maybe you got drunk yourself, fell asleep --- and then the centaurs just happened to come along?!"

"Oh, come on, Iolaus!" Peleus smiles uncertainly. "The things you say... I asked the gods, and all the oracles said with one voice --- Acastus is to blame! And the Dioscuri volunteered to help me..."

Iolaus is silent.

"Some Argonauts you are!" he finally throws out, looking at the Dioscuri, and they fall quiet under his gaze; then he takes the weeping Laodamia by the hand and leaves.

No one tries to stop him.
