#### 3

...No, guests were the very last thing Alcmene needed today.

Since night her usual monthly purification had begun, but in recent years (O jealous Hera!) it had come on so painfully that Alcmene tried to burrow into the farthest corner of the gynaeceum and, for at least the first two days, keep herself out of everyone's sight, like a beast in its den, showing her face to no one but her two trusted Phoenician slave women, who never took offense at their irritable mistress.

So today too Alcmene had fallen asleep only at dawn, risen around noon utterly wrung out, scolded Tephia (who had grown considerably plumper of late, and sluggish and good-natured with it) for some trifling fault, and an hour later given her a silver fibula shaped like a hazel leaf --- the children were at the palaestra, her husband was there too, and life went on as usual, which was to say, was vile.

Alcmene knew this would pass soon, but that made it no easier.

Toward evening the children came back --- sweaty, disheveled, scratched up --- and Alcmene badly wanted to hug them, to comfort them, to hide them if only for a moment from the stern world of men, where people fight and are punished, but Amphitryon had strictly forbidden her from doing so; and for another hour or so Alcmene pitied herself for never having managed to bear herself a daughter (O petty Hera!..), and then Alcides and Iphicles dashed off somewhere, and Amphitryon, who had come in shortly before his sons, sat in the megaron either busy or pretending to be...

That was when the unexpected guests turned up.

Having very nearly ordered the servants to drive off the excessively noisy crowd that had filled the whole courtyard, Alcmene took a closer look at the guests at the last moment and understood --- these were anyone but vagrants. Two dozen armed men with the hoarse voices of soldiers, a dozen women in dusty clothes, one girl of about nine, a very tall man a little older than Amphitryon...

They were loud, but not rowdy; they took swigs from travel kylikes[^25] with hinged lids, but none of them seemed particularly drunk; the women danced little steps and laughed with defiant loudness, but resembled neither cheap pornai nor raving bacchantes; the girl kept quiet and watched Alcmene with huge blue eyes (O Hera... what did I do to deserve this?!); and the tall man stood in the very thick of the crowd, arms crossed over his chest, as if waiting for something.

[^25]: A ladle-shaped drinking cup.

One of the slaves, a native of Euboea, flinched at the sight of this giant and ran off to the megaron, from which a beaming Amphitryon soon emerged, throwing on a festive cloak --- part of the Taphian spoils --- as he walked.

"Rejoice! I am overjoyed to welcome the basileus of Oechalia, the renowned Eurytus, into my house!" Amphitryon said, smiling broadly, and went lightly down the steps toward the guests.

"And I am glad to greet you, valiant Amphitryon, descendant of great Perseus!" the tall man replied, taming his retinue with a lazy wave of the hand --- for all the world like the Earth-Shaker, Poseidon Ennosigaios, calming a stormy sea.

"No, guests today are certainly the last thing we need," Alcmene thought resignedly, plunging headlong into the whirl of household chores, familiar but no more pleasant for it.

By the time the boisterous Euboeans had been settled in, and Amphitryon --- entertaining Eurytus meanwhile ("A basileus!" only now did it register with Alcmene, and even her lower back suddenly stopped reminding her of itself) --- was sitting with him on the terrace before the megaron, a chariot rolled into the courtyard.

"This is my son Iphitus," Eurytus introduced the new guest, and Amphitryon nodded warmly, offering a few words of praise about the newcomer's skill with horses.

Iphitus turned out to be a lithe, bashful youth, almost as tall as his father, but utterly unsuited to the merry company accompanying the basileus; he had none of Eurytus's grim composure, and his thick eyebrows did not draw sharply together over the bridge of his nose, only faintly sketched in soft strokes --- but his hands were already filling out with a man's ripening strength, and his gaze hid a quiet firmness in its depths.

The young man was open, and every thought and feeling of his showed plainly on his face.

"A good boy," Alcmene thought in passing.

"You have a good son, basileus," Amphitryon said, as if he'd overheard her thoughts, glancing at a blushing Iphitus. "Please, everyone, come inside --- the table is already laid for our dear guests."

The men, and Alcmene as mistress of the house, went into the megaron, where Alcmene settled onto a bench by the window, excusing herself with a passing indisposition and no appetite, while the rest took their places in the chairs beside the table laden with food. Amphitryon might not be a basileus (and could have even been a wanax, had he not been exiled from Mycenae) as Eurytus was, or Creon, who had taken him in at Thebes, but Amphitryon's table yielded nothing to a ruler's, neither in the refinement of its costly dishware nor in the variety of its fare.

Amphitryon could afford as much. All the more so with such distinguished guests in the house, and the reason for their visit entirely unknown.

...At last Eurytus leaned back blissfully in his chair, still holding in his hand a half-empty cup of red Pramneios, the pride of Amphitryon's cellars.

"My thanks for a kingly welcome," the basileus said, smiling with his lips alone, gazing spellbound at how the light played in the cup, sparkling on the blood-red surface.

Young Iphitus drank no wine, though no one had forbidden it to him --- he knew the custom.

"I am glad to have pleased guests who do my house such honor," Amphitryon replied, pouring out a demonstrative splash of wine toward the threshold.

Alcmene knew that her husband only made offerings to Zeus the Hospitable in front of others --- she knew the reason for this aversion to the Thunderer all too well, too, and tried not to dwell on it --- but what surprised her was that Eurytus of Oechalia made no answering gesture at all, only glanced at Amphitryon with irony and drained his own cup just as demonstratively, without spilling a single drop.

Though he was the guest, after all, and it fell to him to...

"Time we talked business," Eurytus declared, as if nothing had happened. "I expect that later today we'll be paying our respects to our good friend basileus Creon, but it's you we came to see, dear Amphitryon."

"I'm listening, basileus."

"Basileus, basileus..." Eurytus suddenly sang out in an unexpectedly thin little voice. "Best pour your basileus some wine!"

Iphitus winced faintly, plainly disapproving of his father's behavior, while Amphitryon hastened to grant the guest's request.

"Your son's growing up," Eurytus said unhurriedly, done with his clowning. "A hero in the making."

"Sons," Amphitryon gently corrected him.

"Well, that's what I said --- a son," Eurytus nodded, not listening. "Euboea's far off, but even there I've heard of your palaestra. And of the teachers you've gathered. You're a wise man, Amphitryon, you aim true, you never miss the mark... but your son will make a poor bowman."

"And why is that?" Amphitryon took offense, but didn't show it.

"Well, tell me, is there a single decent archer in your Thebes, apart from Apollo?" Eurytus chuckled, answering a question with a question.

Alcmene flinched. No direct insult to the wrathful Sun-God had been spoken, but even so...

"Surely, basileus, we've all heard of your great skill, and your son too, I'd wager, doesn't fall far short of his father," Amphitryon said carefully, letting his gaze travel from Eurytus's fingers --- long as a musician's, but with a far stronger grip --- to the very similar hand of young Iphitus, who had propped his cheek on it, leaning back on the kline; yes, there was no doubt --- he had seen the hands of outstanding archers before, and he could not be mistaken.

"Few can match you in archery, basileus Eurytus --- but Thebes isn't poor in archers either. The current teacher of Alcides and Iphicles, Myrtilus..."

"Never heard of him," Eurytus shook his head lazily. "No... never heard of him."

"...hits a tossed apple," Amphitryon finished stubbornly, as if he hadn't noticed the mockery, "from fifty paces."

Eurytus smirked disdainfully, and even his bashful son's lips were touched by a faint smile.

A moment later Eurytus took an apple from the table and hurled it straight at the door. Almost at once another apple flew after it, loosed by Iphitus. The two apples collided right by the door and shattered, spattering the doorframe.

"Fifty paces, you say?" the basileus drawled, took another apple, bit into it with a crunch, and set about chewing it with evident relish.

Amphitryon had long since understood what his guest was driving at, but was afraid to believe it. To win a teacher like Eurytus the archer... True, something in the guest's manner troubled him; and besides, surely he wouldn't abandon his own Oechalia to spend years teaching the twins the art of the bow!

Amphitryon said as much to Eurytus directly.

"And I'm indeed not planning to abandon Oechalia for *your* son" ("Sons," Amphitryon corrected him again, trying not to notice the pointed emphasis on "*your* son"; while Eurytus, for his part, paid no attention to "sons"). "I'll leave Iphitus in Thebes --- let him teach. And I'll drop by from time to time..."

Amphitryon breathed a little easier. He liked Iphitus far better than his rather strange father. But then again, what would the teacher Myrtilus say? --- Eurytus's fame rang out across all Hellas, while his little-known son...

"And if this fellow of yours --- what's his name?! --- Munit gets stubborn about it," the basileus of Oechalia seemed to read straight through his host's thoughts, "we could always arrange a contest."

"With you?" Amphitryon blurted out.

Eurytus's gaze turned heavy, and his long body in the chair suddenly brought to mind a well-fed but dangerous snake.

"I've no rivalry with the likes of Munit," he said flatly.

And added quietly:

"Except perhaps with Apollo."

* * *

As she was already seeing the guests off, Alcmene made up her mind to ask Eurytus about the little blue-eyed girl, so out of place amid the basileus's boisterous retinue.

"Is that your daughter? She looks to be the same age as my..."

"That's my wife," Eurytus answered indifferently.

Seeing Alcmene's squeamish surprise, he spread his hands and corrected himself:

"My future wife. My current one's given me a heap of boys, but this one's been foretold to bear me a girl. So your son, my lady, will grow up, become a great man --- and by then my future wife will have borne the hero his future wife. We'll be kin, eh? I've even thought up a name for the girl --- Iole. A good name! Worth dying for, a name like that..."

"A good name it is, but will my boys really ride so far off for wives? I'd wager there's no shortage of girls in Thebes either," Alcmene tried to make light of it, but it came out somehow joyless, even a little frightening.

"They will," basileus Eurytus said, looking her straight in the face, for which he had to bend nearly double. "I'm an archer, my lady --- I'm used to picking out distant targets... They will, my lady, oh, they will indeed. Eros --- he's an archer too, my lady, same as me and Apollo..."

And off he went, bathing in the whoops and din of his retinue, scooping up in his arms the silent, blue-eyed girl.

That night Alcmene tried to talk her husband out of the idea of the archery contest, but couldn't manage to say anything coherent besides the plain "I don't like him!" --- and she and Amphitryon had their first real quarrel in years.
