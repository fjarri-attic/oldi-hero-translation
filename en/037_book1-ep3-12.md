#### 12

"Y-yes, a god! So what?! Eh, our life's a full cup... a full cup --- step right up!.."

Chiron, who until then had been lying peacefully in his cave, raised himself up and glanced with interest toward the entrance.

A crashing of bushes, some incoherent muttering, strange sounds like slaps --- and Hermeias tumbled into the cave. He was moving on all fours, shaking his head; grass blades and rotted pine needles were spilling out of the Trickster's tousled hair, his bleary eyes had drifted together toward the bridge of his nose; the torn, hooded chlamys had vanished somewhere, but it could easily be replaced by the chiton still left on Hermeias --- once dashing, now just as torn and filthy as the lost chlamys.

The Trickster's famous sandals flew after their barefoot master, their little wings fluttering indignantly.

The cave instantly filled with the aroma of a wine cellar.

"Y-yes, a god! Th-this kind! Pray, be kind to me and hold me dear! Or not be kind to me, and not hold me dzzz... and not dzzz..."

Hermeias suddenly stopped buzzing and hiccuped.

"Quiet, you! Can't you see --- the children are asleep! You'll wake them," Chiron tried to calm the Trickster, but the latter let the centaur's words pass by unheard. Fortunately, the twins, curled up on a bed of grass in the far corner of the cave, showed no sign of waking from Hermeias's drunken wailing.

"Children dear!" the Trickster wailed, purposefully shifting his arms and legs in the direction of the brothers. "My darlings! Forgive me, the scoundrel that I am! What a childhood I had... grew up homeless, in a cave!.. Dad on Olympus, mom in the sky, grandfathers --- one in Tartarus, the other holding up the sky!.. Not a single caress, not a single gift on my birthday! I stole, I cheated... and this is what I grew into, such a d— hic!.. Such a d— hic!.. Such a d-divinity! Forgive me, boys! I didn't want it, truly, I didn't! And I still don't wa-a-ant to!.."

At that moment, a whole waterfall of ice-cold spring water crashed down on the Trickster's penitent head. It was the wise centaur who, seeing that words would do no good here, had tipped over onto Hermeias a huge wooden bowl of water that until then had stood peacefully by the entrance.

Hermeias howled like a wounded Chimera, shook himself off with a snort, sending spray flying in every direction; then stood for a while on all fours --- and suddenly demanded, in an unexpectedly brisk voice:

"More!"

Chiron had no second bowl at hand, but he did find a hefty tub --- quite roomy enough for a centaur to bathe in --- which, with some effort, he tipped and poured part of its contents over the much-suffering Hermeias.

The Trickster shook himself off once more, grunted approvingly, and crawled fairly briskly over to the wall, where he assumed a posture more befitting a god, seating himself on the earthen floor and leaning his back against the cool, moss-covered stones.

Almost at once, Chiron's powerful hand appeared before his eyes, holding a hollowed-out bowl filled to the brim with some greenish sludge.

The bowl doubled before his eyes, which made it seem twice as unappetizing.

"Wine again?!" Hermeias said in horror, fighting down his nausea. "Have you all conspired against me here?!"

"Not wine, don't fear! Drink it, you'll feel better. I know from experience --- Silenus is a frequent guest of mine..."

These last words plainly convinced the Trickster. With disobedient fingers he seized the bowl, nearly spilling it, brought it to his lips, and began hastily gulping down the astringent herbal brew, letting drops fall onto his chiton, already hopelessly ruined. Bitterness filled his mouth, his head still buzzed, but the objects around him gained sharpness, and he even managed to shift his position slightly without cracking the back of his head against the wall in the process.

"You're a good healer, Chiron," Hermeias said with a crooked smile. "A little more, and I'll be perfectly sober. And what a shame... what a shame. It's so good to be drunk, to remember nothing, to know nothing, to suffer over nothing... To lose yourself. And to forget... I suppose I'm a bad god," he added, apropos of nothing in particular, but the centaur only nodded in agreement, lying down opposite him and studying the Trickster --- so wretched, in his soaked-through chiton clinging tightly to his body.

"Yes, you're a bad god. And I'm a bad god too. Because when the Family began seriously settling scores among themselves, I stepped aside. It probably wasn't the best way out, but for me it was the only one."

Chiron was silent for a while, twitching his tail slightly.

"You're at a crossroads too right now, Hermeias. If only because you've understood what danger these children pose. To the Family. To us. To everyone. A hope for either side --- it's a very, very dangerous thing."

"Got it," Hermeias said hoarsely, half asking, half stating.

"And you wanted to kill them. Not now --- earlier. I remember, you already told me about it almost three years ago."

"I wanted to. But not them --- him. Alcides. And Iphicles... could have taken his brother's place. A hero stands alone. It's better that way --- for the Family, and for the people, and for the boy himself."

Hermeias's voice faltered, and what he said sounded unconvincing.

Chiron thoughtfully wound a lock of his beard around one finger.

"You say it, but you don't believe it, Hermeias. They really are dangerous. Alcides especially. There I agree with you. Kill him, Hermeias. Kill the boy."

"You're the one telling me this?" the Trickster said, taken aback.

"I am the one telling you this. Yes, it didn't work out for you the first time. And I won't let it happen here, on my Pelion. But who's stopping you from trying again? You're a god, aren't you! Why shouldn't a discus, for instance, *accidentally* strike Alcides on the head? Or why shouldn't some mishap occur while the brothers are training with weapons? Or the horses could bolt. I don't need to teach you your business, Trickster. If you like --- go consult Hera."

"And Olympus?!" Hermeias said, thoroughly at a loss. "And the giants?! Dad, after all... he's pinning so much hope on the Lone Cleaner!"

"So what?" Chiron gave the Trickster a hard look, and Hermeias averted his eyes. "You've known about this today, too. Did it stop you? Answer me!"

"No. But... if I kill Alcides, and Iphicles takes his place after all, becoming the Slayer of Monsters --- then I'll be the first monster he tries to kill. Which means the other members of the Family will be next in line... and Tartarus will end up with the ally it's been waiting for. That's no way out, Chiron!"

Chiron listened to the Trickster with a strange, narrowed gaze, glancing now and then at the sleeping children.

"That's wise, Hermeias. You're growing up --- don't smirk, I don't mean in years lived. But is that really the only reason you don't want to kill Alcides... or both of them? Try to get drunk again, and, sober, try to understand yourself drunk. Try it, Hermeias! Why do you want them to live?!"

Hermeias was long in answering, and the Trickster's gaze in those minutes was both unfocused and intent at once, as though he really were trying to peer into the depths of his own being, into his own personal, inner Tartarus, whose bronze walls were now being struck by Chiron's words, like by the hands of the Hecatoncheires.

"I really am a bad god," Hermeias said slowly at last, looking Chiron in the face and this time not turning his eyes away. "It's just... it's just that I've grown too attached to these boys! A piece of me is already invested in them --- and I'm sorry for that piece, Chiron, because it's also 'I.' Yes, I understand that they'll die all the same --- not now, but in forty, fifty, seventy years! For them, that's a lifetime. For us, an instant. And still I can't kill them, simply because I can't! The Family is the Family, but --- though for my part I'd rather have the Family than the Fallen! --- I won't kill these two, not even for the Family's sake! And so what if I'm a bad god... I am what I am, and I'm not going to be anything else."

"Now you've said it, and I've heard the truth," the centaur nodded, satisfied. "You really were standing at a crossroads, Hermeias: gleaming Olympus, the dark depths of Hades --- and the middle world, which knows neither heavenly omnipotence nor subterranean peace, a world of daily, hourly choice; a world of those who live in it, ones like me, Dionysus, Pan, Amphitryon, Castor, Alcmene, these children here, old Silenus... all of us, having made our choice, are equal --- whatever we're called and however many legs we walk on, two or four! In saying, 'I've grown attached to them!' --- you made your choice, Hermeias, and I very much hope you weren't wrong. Now you have the right to come to me on Pelion without waiting for an invitation or permission, whether with the children or without, simply, the way one comes to a friend's house. Pelion is no place for the Family's games, but for friends it is always open."

Hermeias listened to the centaur, smiling in bewilderment, a little embarrassed.

"And one more thing," Chiron added after a pause. "The children are one matter, but... it would be good if you got to know their father better."

"Which one?" Hermeias nodded at the twins, stirring in their sleep. "Which father would that be?"

Chiron did not take the joke --- if, of course, the Trickster was joking.

"*Their* father," he repeated with emphasis. "I believe that will help you understand the children better; and not only the children. Because these twins' great-grandfather Perseus did not become a hero when you, on Zeus's order, gave him the sword and led him to the Graeae. He simply saw boys just like these, turned to stone, in the streets of Seriphos and Argos --- and understood that Medusa must no longer live."

Hermeias suddenly reached out and lightly touched Chiron's horse-flank.

"Tell me, Kronid," the Trickster asked, "do you really think that today I chose something? I'd like it to be so..."

He got up, went over to the half-emptied tub, and began splashing water on his own face.

Hermeias's sandals, which until then had been hiding behind the tub, fluttered out of their shelter and settled at their master's feet.
