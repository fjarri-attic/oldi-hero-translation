#### 14

Thebes welcomed Iolaos the way a doting grandmother welcomes a famous grandson who is hardly ever at home, but of whom she can boast to the neighbors.

He was received in every house of even the slightest distinction.

He was fed lavishly and given drink lavishly.

He was showered with gifts.

The line of maidens aspiring to share Iolaos's kline, if only for a moment, grew so long that it could have satisfied Priapos[^84] himself.

[^84]: Priapos---son of Dionysos and the nymph Chione (in another version, of Aphrodite), the possessor of manly endowments so exceptional that they had to be carried about on a cart.

The obligatory list of events requiring Iolaos's presence included: a visit to the temple of Zeus the Father, erected (or rather, founded) by the young Herakles; a visit to the temple of Herakles himself; the contemplation of the enormous tholos where Amphitryon the lawagetas had once been laid to rest; attendance at sacrificial rites dedicated to Iolaos personally, as one of the national heroes of Thebes...

And finally, a tour of the sites of martial glory, where the Thebans had valiantly repulsed the marauding raid of seven shameless chieftains from Argos, who had laid down their reckless heads here.

Iolaos dutifully stood at the Proitid Gates, at the Elektran Gates, at the Neitan Gates, at the Athenian Gates, at the Borraian Gates, at the Homoloid Gates, and at some other gates whose name he had forgotten---he stood there, nodding knowingly, listening to yet another panegyric to the might of the Thebans and silently rejoicing that Thebes had only seven gates, and not a hundred or, say, two hundred.

[A cenotaph,]{.thoughts} he kept thinking. [It's all a cenotaph...]{.thoughts}

A cenotaph was what they called an "empty grave"---a tomb raised for those who had died in foreign lands, gone missing, or been left unburied; in short, for those whose death had not been reliably confirmed. It was believed that a restless shade would sooner or later find its cenotaph, after which it could depart for Hades in peace.

To Iolaos, Thebes seemed a cenotaph of the past, which had died in a foreign land.

A meaningless grave without a body.

The only thing that caught Iolaos's interest at all was the story of how the Argive breakthrough at the Neitan Gates had been stopped short only thanks to the desperate courage of the Theban gate-guard Telemos, grandson of Telemos, who in life had borne the nickname "Nobody," and after his death had become Telemos the Theban.

"Nobody is bad," Iolaos recalled his first meeting with the fussy, shyly blinking guard. "Nobody is very bad. A man must not be nobody. Understand?"

"I understand," Telemos the Theban answered quietly out of the gloom.

Telemos, grandson of Telemos.

On the fifth day, having given his importunate escorts the slip, Iolaos set off for the market---not to buy anything, but reasoning, rightly, that no one would look for him there, and that, should the need arise, it was not so very hard to get lost in the bustle of the market.

And now, quite unexpectedly for himself, Iolaos felt that he had come home---though not because of the market, which is the same in every city of Hellas, but because of a blind rhapsode strumming on a battered lyre.

"It can't be," Iolaos muttered, stunned, studying the blind man, "no, it can't be... How old must he be by now? Probably not the same one---the old one was always singing Hermeias's praises... And this one?"

Iolaos listened.

Iolaos caught his breath---so close to him did the singer's words seem, as if through his sightless, clouded eyes the rhapsode saw far more than a man is meant to see, even a man touched by the favor of the Muses.

The local beggars were very surprised when the richly dressed newcomer suddenly broke away and dashed off to the fish stalls---but he soon returned and carefully dropped his mite into the rhapsode's bowl.

Two little dried fish.

A trifle, a cheap thing.

The beggars would have been more surprised still, had they seen that same newcomer the next morning set off for the north-eastern outskirts of the city, pass between two hills, and vanish.

As if he had never been.

* * *

"Sit down," Hermeias said instead of a greeting, patting the threshold of his hovel.

Iolaos sat.

"I won't offer you a drink." Hermeias was looking at his feet. "I've got none myself."

"Why?" Iolaos asked.

"Because," the Trickster answered seriously. "I'm afraid Dionysos will overhear... and report to whoever needs to know. He's dad's favorite now, our Dionysie! And I mustn't meet with you, lawagetas, I mustn't at all..."

The sky had clouded over with foam, it had grown cool, some restless bird was bawling at the top of its lungs on the roof, voicing its opinion of life---and Iolaos sensed that Hermeias was afraid, desperately afraid, so afraid his knees were knocking... of what?

Of having met an old acquaintance?!

"You might at least have come to the funeral," he flung out.

"Eastern Elis, the town of Pheneos," Hermeias grumbled, his jaw muscles working. "If you go down from the acropolis and bear left from the stadium, you'll come right to the hill where the grave is... I was at the funeral, lawagetas, stop kicking me, please!.. I feel sick enough as it is."

"Should I go?"

"You'll go. But later. I'd like to see you in my place, lawagetas, with that bronze temper of yours---when the Family was rocking Olympos after the Gigantomachy! Poseidon and Stepmother all but went for dad's throat---why are there two of Herakles?! Why are they identical?! Whose son is which?! If there are two---does that mean there could be three? Four? A hundred?! Whose idea was it anyway---these Half-Man Cleaners?! And me---I'm a quiet god, I don't like fighting... I kept silent, lawagetas! All I did was keep silent, step back into the shadows---and it turns out I'm a traitor!.. I couldn't even warn you afterwards, they kept such an eye on me that there was no slipping away, however I twisted and turned!"

The Trickster clutched his head in his hands, rocking from side to side, spurting out the words in jolts, like blood from a wound:

"I'm scum, lawagetas... a coward! Would you believe it, when I came to our boy's funeral in secret, like a thief---I wanted to kill myself! Only I didn't know how... But back then, on Olympos---I kept silent! The Trickster went sour! You have a family, lawagetas, and I have a Family... How can I go against my own? And my own all say the same thing: it's either us or them! The Fallen are in Tartaros, the Gigantes are wiped out, the Mess on mama Gaia has been cleaned up---time to get rid of the Cleaners! Before they get rid of us... First, no more appearing to people in the flesh, except in extreme cases---no sense letting them get used to the gods! Second, a hero stands alone! Not two, not five, not a hundred---one! One alone! The rest---get rid of them! Do you understand that, lawagetas?!"

"I understand." It was hard to see the Trickster like this, but Iolaos knew that any consolation right now would be like a sweet poison; and besides, he could find no words of consolation, because he was not looking for any.

Nor did he intend to look.

"What's there not to understand? It's either us or them... The Mess is cleaned up, now we'll get rid of the Cleaners! A hero stands alone; and after that---none left standing. Plain as day..."

"Kill me, if you like." The Trickster looked at Iolaos with the eyes of a beaten dog. "I know, lawagetas, you could do it... Do you want to?"

Iolaos shook his head.

"You've as good as wiped out our generation, root and branch," he said quietly. "There are only scraps left, you can count them on your fingers... What will you do with our children? Don't answer, I know it myself. In ten or twenty years you'll herd them all together at some lousy city---Troy, say---and whoever doesn't want to go, you'll force; then you'll toss them a bone, one for all of them---and we'll run each other through with our spears on our own! And the Family will watch from the hilltop and enjoy it... that'll keep them entertained for another ten years or so."

"How do you know?" Hermeias choked.

"I know us. And I know your lot. That's enough for me. And I also know that now no one in the Family will lay a finger on Herakles. He'll live out his days as the son of Zeus, the favorite of the gods! You can't touch him---it's frightening, and there's no point in destroying the myth. Have I got it right, Trickster?"

"You have, lawagetas. But has it never occurred to you that the Family is right about something? That it really is: us or you?! Did you see the shields of those seven warriors who came to Thebes?"

"I did."

Iolaos had indeed seen the seven shields, the seven trophies, and even back then he had been struck by their strange symbolism: on one---a hero threatening the sky with a torch, and the inscription: "In Defiance of Zeus"; on another---a warrior on a tower, and the motto: "Not even Ares could cast him down!"; on the third and fourth---Typhon and the Sphinx, monsters, enemies of the gods; the fifth---the smooth black shield of Amphiaraos the Soothsayer; the sixth---bearing the image of a stormy night sky...

And only the seventh was ordinary.

On it, a goddess was leading a hero by the hand.

"I did," Iolaos repeated. "Only I don't want to talk about it, Hermeias. I have one regret---that I can't reach Olympos, that I can't be in your place, Trickster! Well, never mind; when I die, I'll go down to Hades, meet the Sovereign... will you take my soul down, Hermeias? If not, I'll take it out on him myself!.."

"That won't work."

"Why?"

"The Sovereign's orders. You're not to set foot in Hades. Ever."

"How's that?" For a moment Iolaos was at a loss.

"Just like that, lawagetas! What's on the Sovereign's mind is shrouded in darkness. In short, you'll never be in Hades. Live while you're alive, then roam Gaia as a shade---if you like, take up someone's body; if you like, just wander... or else crawl into a stone and lie there a thousand years. I don't even know whether it's a gift or a punishment. Only, once the Sovereign has decided something, the Family can kick up all the fuss they like, but they won't go against him! Got it?"

Iolaos stood up.

"So you're driving me out, then?" he smiled unkindly. "The Family on Olympos, the shades in Hades, the Fallen in Tartaros---and Amphitryon the lawagetas, not one step off Gaia?"

"Is it the first time for you?" Hermeias answered quietly. "What do you say, Amphitryon the Exile?"

"I'll say something, Trickster, but not what you're expecting. I'll tell you a tale. On Crete there lived a bronze giant, Talos, an invulnerable colossus. And the bronze Talos had a nail in his ankle, which stopped up the only opening in Talos's only vein. Do you know what happened next, Hermeias?"

"Obviously," the Trickster said in surprise. "The nail fell out, the blood ran out, Talos died. What's your tale for, lawagetas?"

"For this: a hero must stand---if only one. Like the nail of Talos. If the Family pulls out the last nail, human faith will drain away like blood from a bronze body---and the Olympians will lie in a dead heap of metal nobody needs. Don't say afterwards, Trickster, that I didn't warn you!"

Hermeias sat on the threshold and watched the man walking away.

"I won't," he muttered dully, "I won't, lawagetas... You'd have done better to hit me, or something?"

The little wings on his sandals twitched convulsively, like the fingers of a dying man.
