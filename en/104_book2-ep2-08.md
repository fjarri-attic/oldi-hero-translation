#### 8

Iphikles did not doubt for a single second that his brother was alive.

Small shells, resembling birds of prey with their wings spread, crunched underfoot; from the cove on the far side of the headland came excited shouts---Iolaos and his Myrmidons were putting the storm-battered ships back in order; the sea fawned on the shore of Kos like a guilty puppy, licking at the sandy shallows, and yesterday's battle in the pouring rain seemed like a bad dream.

And Alkeides was alive.

Not here; but---alive.

Beyond the coastal cliffs, timid wisps of smoke rose toward the sky: the people of Kos were gradually coming to their senses, returning to their everyday lives, cursing the false, bloody night, and preparing a funeral pyre for their dead---first and foremost, for their own basileus, Eurypylos.

And Alkeides was alive.

Iphikles knew it.

He only didn't know what would happen if one of the twins---it didn't matter which---died first, without waiting for his brother.

And so---Alkeides is alive.

They will meet soon.

Iphikles had no idea how soon that would happen.

To his right, at a rock fissure twice the height of a man, there came a faint sigh, as though the great mass of stone had quietly cleared the lungs of its depths; and Iphikles, his feet sinking into the sand, made his way toward the glassy whirlpool of an opening Dromos.

"Hermeias!" he called out joyfully, hoping to learn something about his brother's fate from the light-footed god.

But it was not the Trickster.

A tall, statuesque girl stepped out of the shimmering funnel; her large features and stern, even severe gaze made her look older; from beneath a light helmet pushed back on her head, its crest cropped short into a bristling brush, escaped thick locks of light-brown hair; in her strong hand the girl held a long spear, while a round shield hung across her back.

"Rejoice, Herakles!" the girl announced quietly, examining Iphikles without ceremony, as if he were a slave put up for sale; or an ally foisted on her---whichever you prefer.

Her face was unfamiliar, but it bore the unmistakable mark of the Family: disdainful, hard creases at the corners of a young, beautifully shaped mouth; brows knit together out of habit, like silken storm clouds above the ridge of her slender nose; dangerous flickers of heat lightning playing in blue eyes ready to darken in an instant...

And the spear, the spear in her hand.

"Rejoice, Promachos[\*](#fn-71){.noteref}!" Iphikles nodded, not bothering to correct her, well aware of how he must look: a graying man of forty, all but naked, covered in old scars and the fresh, barely dried gifts of the previous night. "Do you happen to know what's become of my brother?"

Either the question pleased the Warrior, or Iphikles's appearance concealed merits visible only to the eye of a fearsome virgin goddess---in any case, the girl's face softened and warmed.

"He's safe," Athena answered, in a low, slightly husky voice. "Father carried him out of the battle, mistaking him for you. Though I imagine that I myself would not have confused Herakles with his earthly brother, even in the crucible of a night battle."

"I won't argue." Iphikles nodded politely; he genuinely had no intention of arguing.

The situation spoke for itself.

Besides, Athena's answer held another meaning, one that had perhaps escaped even her own notice: "would not have confused"---and, naturally, would not have carried him out.

"Then why don't you fall to your knees," Athena inquired innocently, "and offer me praise?"

Iphikles smiled, running both palms over his beard, stiff with sea salt.

"Would that really please you, Promachos? Just picture it: you step out of the Dromos in all your splendor, and I go crashing heavily to my knees, scraping them raw on the sharp edges of the shells, and, barely holding back a curse at the pain, bawl out, in a godawful voice, words worn smooth as pebbles! Is that worthy of you; and is that worthy of me?"

With the butt of her spear the goddess traced wavy lines in the sand, ones that plainly held no hidden meaning; she traced them and immediately smoothed them away, only to start new ones.

"No, this isn't pride." Athena was speaking to herself, as if there were no one else on the shore. "It's something else... What is it? I suppose I ought to feel offended, or even angry---and instead I feel pleased, and unburdened. Why?"

"Because you're smart," Iphikles answered seriously. "And that's the thing that makes equals of---"

At first he'd meant to say---"gods and men"; then---"you and me," but something in Athena's face stopped him after all.

"Makes equals---just as foolishness does," he concluded instead.

And he understood that their easy conversation had come to an end.

"Go and get your weapons," the goddess ordered, in a different voice now---imperious and brooking no argument. "And don't forget your bow and arrows. I'll wait for you here. Today is a great day, Herakles, a day worth having been born for; the day of the Gigantomachy. The Family has struck at the Phlegraian Fields; you and I follow."

"And my brother?" Lowering his head a little, like a bull, Iphikles fixed the Warrior with the heavy stare of a fighting man used to choosing his own allies---not with the moist, adoring eyes of a dog whose masters have finally taken it hunting.

"I already told you he's safe." Athena raised her eyebrows in surprise. "And besides: aren't you paying him rather too much attention, given that sooner or later the two of you will have to part?"

"When?"

"After death. Herakles will go to Olympos; Iphikles---I believe his name is Iphikles?---to Hades; Herakles will be received into the Family, becoming a god; while Iphikles will dissolve into the murk of Erebos, becoming a shade."

"I'm going for my weapons," Iphikles answered, after a pause.

Alkeides was alive; wounded, but alive.

That was enough to set off for Phlegra with his mind at ease---not in the name of the Family, but in fulfillment of a promise made to Iphitos the archer.

Nothing else mattered.

Small shells, resembling birds of prey with their wings spread, crunched underfoot.
