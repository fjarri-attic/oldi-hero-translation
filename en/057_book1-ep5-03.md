#### 3

...These were not the best days in the life of Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, who loved --- whether in jest or in earnest --- to call herself the "Shadow of Thebes."

Chance travelers on the roads from seven-gated Thebes to the northeastern slopes of Cithaeron gazed with astonishment and pity at a tiny, wizened old woman with the sharp little face of a weasel, scurrying with her last strength after two sullen, unfriendly hulking men, one of whom carried in his arms a drowsy boy of about six, his hair cropped short in the manner of a slave.

Such careful treatment of a slave not yet of age and therefore useless astonished passing travelers no less than the wandering dwarf-crone herself, who by rights ought to be thinking of her journey into the darkness of Erebus rather than roaming the roads of Boeotia; and had they learned that Galinthias had hastily bought the drowsy boy at double price through a chain of middlemen just before leaving the city, the travelers would have grunted, shrugged, and gone on their way, gossiping and trading rumors.

It was just as well that this strange company made its stops in secluded, deserted places, for the hulking men, worn out by the day's march, would hastily choke down a bite that stuck in their throats and collapse into the embrace of Hypnos the Sleep as into the encircling arms of a longed-for woman; the slave boy first chewed obediently, then just as obediently stopped chewing, plainly unable to tell day from night, or visions from waking life; but long into the stillness and dark the eyes of the frail-looking old woman went on gleaming with a yellowish fire, and the beasts of the surrounding country gave the bright-eyed apparition a wide berth, an arrow's flight around her, trading reason for instinct and curiosity for wariness.

As for people --- it was better still that they saw none of it.

Long, very long had it been since Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, once a servant of Hecate the Three-Formed and now a Tartarus-Possessed, had left the city... oh, long since she had left it, having sniffed out every corner in it, tasted every draft, felt out the visible and the invisible, studied Thebes end to end the way a predatory weasel knows its burrow with its many hidden stores and secret exits, having merged with the city, having truly become the shadow of Thebes --- and now, having ventured out into the dangerous and unpredictable open country, the daughter of Proetus did not allow herself to relax for a single moment, retreating into memory instead of sleep.

There she is --- young, impetuous, tireless in her service to the Mistress of the Crossroads, whether it be the nighttime running through freshly plowed fields or the ritual lovemaking after which the earth takes into itself the corpses of males who could not survive Hecate's ecstasies --- the bodies of men who resembled animals, and of animals who resembled men.

There she is --- mature, focused and assured, raising the stone blade over a trembling victim, and a sweet, viscous wave surges up within her, clouding her mind with the scent of eternity and, for some reason, also of mildew; the goddess is pleased, and for a moment Galinthias feels herself to be the Three-Formed one: priestess, sacrifice, and goddess --- a single being, wrapped in the languor of death and rebirth.

There she is --- frightened, trembling, forgetting the not-yet-finished victim writhing on the altar, listening to the voice of Hecate, until she realizes that it is an entirely different voice, and that she, Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, is from now on and forever --- someone else.

That was the day she heard Tartarus; the day the Fallen first spoke to her.

At first she did not believe. No, not that she had been granted conversation with the ancient Titans and Kronus the Forefather himself --- great was the pride of the daughter of Proetus, too often had she identified herself with the divine, knowing all the while that any of the immortals had the right and the power to call upon the Fallen and speak with them, so long as the Hundred-Handed had not yet struck the bronze walls of Tartarus.

What Galinthias would not believe was something else. It would have been easier for her to let a notched knife into her own heart than to believe the truth of the Fallen: that the sacrifices were not made to Hecate, that it was not a goddess she fed, but Tartarus; and that the food of the immortals she called upon over the altar was the priestess herself.

Her ecstasy, her fervor, her faith and her service.

"All of you mortals are sacrifices --- the slain and the slayers alike," ran the truth of the Fallen, "but if the soul that flies up from your altar passes by Hades and dissolves into us, the Ancient Ones, vanquished but unsubmissive, then you, priestess, sucked dry by the divine, will descend into Hades a voiceless, memoryless shade, the husk of a butterfly that has passed through the claws of a ravenous spider. It is all the same to the divine whether the altar holds a human body, ears of grain, a lamb, or a wedge of cheese; it is you, mortals, on whom the gods feed, casting the leavings down into Hades!"

"And you?!" Galinthias cried out soundlessly, the earth reeling beneath her feet. "And you --- you yourselves?!"

"We are not gods; we are the shepherds of the Golden Age," came the answer. "That is why we rose up in our time. Answer this for yourself, daughter of Proetus: if, in raising the knife, you called out to Hecate --- why did we answer? How did you manage to make your voice heard all the way down to the underworld?!"

And Galinthias felt herself to be not a vessel for the divine spirit, but a dirty bowl, one of many, from which some stray dog that had crept up was greedily lapping.

Many times more did the priestess of Hecate turn to the Fallen; she argued, doubted, tried to prove them wrong, refused to believe...

Great was the pride of the daughter of Proetus.

And having come to believe --- she did not forgive.

Now the Tartarus-Possessed feared nothing, burned out from within by a terrible hatred, save for one thing --- that she might not manage to depart in the final rite in time to bypass Hades and flow into Tartarus, which thirsted for freedom.

That was why she was wary of leaving Thebes, as a beast is wary of leaving its den.

She had to.

Earlier than usual, the Fallen had turned to the daughter of Proetus, and strange was their command: to go to Cithaeron and there make a sacrifice to Alcides, son of Zeus and Alcmene --- but in such a way that Alcides himself would not see the rite, while remaining visible to Galinthias.

Tartarus wished to see the hero-madman through human eyes.

That was why two sullen men trudged along the slopes of Cithaeron with the perpetually drowsy slave boy in their arms, and why the little dwarf-crone scurried after them, glancing sharply about her.

To the right of the winding path along which Galinthias and her companions were now moving rose a gloomy, virgin forest; to the left, below a small clay bluff some six cubits high, a lively, twisting mountain stream chattered merrily.

All in all, an unremarkable landscape, except for one thing: a dry pine with a core rotted clean through had chosen this very moment to come down across the path, and it fell, one must say, with remarkable good fortune, some ten paces short of the man walking in front with the child in his arms.

The man froze as if rooted to the spot; his less quick-witted companion bumped into his broad back, swore under his breath --- and then swore again, louder this time, because the child in the first man's arms swayed limply and, losing his balance, went flying down into the river.

Having gone under, the boy surfaced almost at once and, waving his arms senselessly, stared up at the sky as though it were meant to explain to him the meaning of what was happening. The current was quickly carrying the child toward where menacing breakers foamed and roared around the sharp rocks jutting from the water.

"Down!" shrieked Galinthias, who had grasped the situation in an instant, baring her small, ratlike teeth. "Down, quickly! Pull him out, you idiots!"

The daughter of Proetus's two companions leapt from the bluff at the same instant and threw themselves into the water. One of them --- the one who had been carrying the boy --- was noticeably limping, apparently having bruised his leg on an unlucky landing.

Strangely enough, it was the limping man who got there first. Splashing the glittering surface of the water noisily, he found himself in the middle of the current in an instant and very nearly reached the boy with the curved end of his traveling staff, which he had never let go of. He managed to hook the half-unraveled rags that made up the little slave's clothing; it seemed the deed was done --- but the next moment only a bundle of rags remained on the staff, while the bare little body slipped on down the current.

The second servant ran clumsily along the bank, stumbling on the wet pebbles, but Galinthias could already see --- he would not make it in time.

And indeed he did not, slipping and falling flat on his face just as some swift body literally tore through the tangle of bushes on the far bank, entering the current almost without a splash, not far from the seething foam of the rapids.

"A lion!" flashed through the stunned Galinthias's mind. "The Man-Eater of Cithaeron!"

And a moment later, when a sun-browned young man, breathing calm strength, rose from the water right beside her, holding the rescued child in the crook of his slightly tensed left arm (the boy still looked drowsy and indifferent to everything, including his own life), Galinthias realized that it was not for nothing she had taken the chance rescuer for a lion --- for a supple, graceful predator that even a true mountain lion would be unlucky to cross.

The young man pressed a bundle of short javelins to his side with his right elbow and smiled reassuringly at the old woman, seeing her cry out and clap a hand over her mouth --- but he had misjudged the cause of the daughter of Proetus's alarm. Five years had passed since Galinthias had last seen this face, but she could not be mistaken: before her stood Alcides, exiled to Cithaeron, son of Zeus and Alcmene, the living target of the daughter of Proetus.

An arrow loosed by Tartarus.

"Rejoice and be glad, O godlike hero," the old woman wailed, instantly finding the right words, "for praise shall I raise up — to you, and the gods guiding you, when forth from the thicket you came, Alcides, pride of Thebes, and---"

"You're mistaken, grandma!" the young man laughed, handing the child to the servant who had come running up, limping on his left leg. "I'm Iphicles, Alcides's brother. Sorry if that's a disappointment..."

"Oh, Iphicles!" the old woman clasped her hands together tenderly, instantly forgetting her lofty manner of speech. "Oh, my little one! However is one to tell you apart from your brother?! Both heroes, both fine lads, your mom's treasure, your dad's pride! You don't remember me, I'd wager, but I'm granny Galinthias --- I used to chase the Eileithyiai away from your mom's bedside, gave you herbs to drink so your little tummies wouldn't ache, and your mama used to say: 'I, Galinthias dear, remember a kindness my whole life long...'"

Iphicles was not listening very closely to the endless stream of the old woman's chatter. Galinthias's face seemed vaguely familiar --- his mother really had told him something of the kind once... and for some reason he also remembered a frenzied crowd in front of the house, come to kill him, Iphicles.

Now why would that be?

Iphicles shook his wet head, chasing away the unpleasant vision, and suddenly hit on a way to get rid of the pestering old woman.

"Granny Galinthias dear!" he bellowed in an unnatural voice, whirling up onto the bluff and tossing the shrieking daughter of Proetus's small dry body into the air. "What joy, what joy this is! And Alcides --- oh, Alcides will be overjoyed, there are no words! He's right here, close by, carrying the hide of the Cithaeron lion after his feat --- you just need to go up the little path, granny, and turn left past the old elm split by lightning... Just shout 'Alci-ides!' and he'll appear at once! And how glad he'll be!.. He simply won't believe his own happiness..."

Giving a last spiteful little chuckle, Iphicles took off at a run along the bluff, and so failed to notice the expression on the old woman's sharp little face, which seemed to have turned to stone on the spot, so much so that she even forgot to scold her negligent servants.

Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, had long since stopped trusting coincidences of this kind.

But... she had to hurry.
