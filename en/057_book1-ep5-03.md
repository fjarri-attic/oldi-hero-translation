#### 3

...Those were not the best days in the life of Galinthias, daughter of Proitos, who loved---whether in jest or in earnest---to call herself the "Shadow of Thebes."

Chance travelers on the roads from seven-gated Thebes to the northeastern slopes of Kithairon gazed with astonishment and pity at a tiny, wizened old woman with the sharp little face of a weasel, scurrying with the last of her strength after two sullen, unfriendly hulking men, one of whom carried in his arms a drowsy boy of about six, his hair cropped short in the manner of a slave.

Such careful treatment of a slave not yet of age and therefore useless astonished passing travelers no less than the sight of the wandering dwarf-crone herself, who by rights ought to be thinking of her journey into the darkness of Erebos rather than roaming the roads of Boiotia; and had they learned that Galinthias had hastily bought the drowsy boy at twice the price through a chain of middlemen just before leaving the city, the travelers would have grunted, shrugged, and gone on their way, gossiping and trading rumors.

It was just as well that this strange company made its stops in secluded, deserted places, for the hulking men, worn out by the day's march, would hastily choke down a bite that stuck in their throats and collapse into the embrace of Hypnos the Sleep as into the encircling arms of a longed-for woman; the slave boy first chewed obediently, then just as obediently stopped chewing, plainly unable to tell day from night, or visions from waking life; but long into the stillness and dark the eyes of the frail-looking old woman went on gleaming with a yellowish fire, and the beasts of the surrounding country steered a bowshot clear of the bright-eyed apparition, relying on instinct in place of reason and on wariness in place of curiosity.

As for people---it was better still that they saw none of it.

Long, very long had Galinthias, daughter of Proitos, once a servant of Three-Formed Hekate and now one of the Tartaros-Possessed, kept to the city... oh, long had she kept to it, sniffing out every corner, tasting every draft, feeling out the visible and the invisible, learning Thebes inside and out the way a predatory weasel knows its burrow with its many hidden stores and secret exits, merging with the city, truly becoming the shadow of Thebes---and now, having ventured out into the dangerous and unpredictable open country, the daughter of Proitos did not allow herself to relax for a single moment, retreating into memory instead of sleep.

There she is---young, impetuous, tireless in her service to the Mistress of the Crossroads, whether it be night runs across freshly plowed fields or the ritual lovemaking after which the earth takes into itself the corpses of males who could not survive Hekate's ecstasies---the bodies of men who resembled animals, and of animals who resembled men.

There she is---mature, focused and assured, raising the stone blade over a trembling sacrifice, and a sweet, viscous wave surges up within her, clouding her mind with the scent of eternity and, for some reason, also of mold; the goddess is pleased, and for a moment Galinthias feels herself to be the Three-Formed one: priestess, sacrifice, and goddess---a single being, wrapped in the languor of death and rebirth.

There she is---frightened, trembling, forgetful of the half-slain sacrifice writhing on the altar, listening to the voice of Hekate, until she realizes that it is an entirely different voice, and that she, Galinthias, daughter of Proitos, is from now on and forever---someone else.

That was the day she heard Tartaros; the day the Fallen first spoke to her.

At first she did not believe it. No, it was not the honor of conversing with the ancient Titans and Kronos the Forefather himself that she doubted---great was the pride of the daughter of Proitos, too often had she identified herself with the divine, knowing all the while that any of the immortals had the right and the power to call upon the Fallen and speak with them, so long as the Hundred-Handed had not yet struck the bronze walls of Tartaros.

What Galinthias would not believe was something else. It would have been easier for her to drive a notched knife into her own heart than to believe the truth of the Fallen: that the sacrifices were not made to Hekate, that it was not a goddess she fed, but Tartaros; and that the food of the immortals she called upon over the altar was the priestess herself.

Her ecstasy, her fervor, her faith and her service.

"All of you mortals are sacrifices---the slain and the slayers alike," ran the truth of the Fallen, "but if the soul that flies up from your altar bypasses Hades and dissolves into us, the Ancient Ones, vanquished but unbowed, then you, priestess, sucked dry by the deity, will descend into Hades a voiceless, memoryless shade, the husk of a butterfly that has been in a ravenous spider's clutches. It is all the same to a deity whether the altar holds a human body, ears of grain, a lamb, or a wedge of cheese; it is you, mortals, on whom the gods feed, casting the leavings down into Hades!"

"And you?!" Galinthias cried out soundlessly, the earth reeling beneath her feet. "And you---you yourselves?!"

"We are not gods; we are the shepherds of the Golden Age," came the answer. "That is why we rose up in our time. Answer this for yourself, daughter of Proitos: if, in raising the knife, you called out to Hekate---why did we answer? How did you manage to make your voice heard all the way down to the underworld?!"

And Galinthias felt herself to be not a vessel for the divine spirit, but a dirty bowl, one of many, from which some stray dog that had crept up was greedily lapping.

Many times did the priestess of Hekate address the Fallen; she argued, doubted, tried to prove them wrong, refused to believe...

Great was the pride of the daughter of Proitos.

And having come to believe---she did not forgive.

Burned out from within by a terrible hatred, the Tartaros-Possessed now feared nothing save one thing---that she might not manage to depart in the final rite in time to bypass Hades and flow into Tartaros, which thirsted for freedom.

That was why she was wary of leaving Thebes, as a beast is wary of leaving its den.

She had to.

Earlier than usual the Fallen had spoken to the daughter of Proitos, and strange was their command: to go to Kithairon and there make a sacrifice to Alkeides, son of Zeus and Alkmene---but in such a way that Alkeides himself would not see the rite, while remaining visible to Galinthias.

Tartaros wished to see the mad hero through human eyes.

That was why two sullen men trudged along the slopes of Kithairon with the perpetually drowsy slave boy in their arms, and why the little dwarf-crone scurried after them, glancing sharply about her.

To the right of the winding path along which Galinthias and her companions were now moving rose a gloomy, virgin forest; to the left, below a small clay bluff some six cubits high, a twisting mountain stream chattered merrily.

All in all, an unremarkable landscape, except for one thing: a dead pine with a core rotted clean through chose this very moment to come down across the path, and came down, it must be said, with remarkable good fortune, some ten paces short of the man walking in front with the child in his arms.

The man froze as if rooted to the spot; his less quick-witted companion bumped into his broad back, swore under his breath---and then swore again, louder this time, because the child in the first man's arms swayed limply and, losing his balance, went flying down into the river.

Having gone under, the boy surfaced almost at once and, waving his arms senselessly, stared up at the sky as though it owed him an explanation of what was happening. The current was quickly carrying the child toward a spot where menacing white water foamed and roared around the sharp rocks jutting from the stream.

"Down!" shrieked Galinthias, who had grasped the situation in an instant, baring her small, ratlike teeth. "Down, quickly! Pull him out, you idiots!"

The two companions of the daughter of Proitos leapt from the bluff at the same instant and threw themselves into the water. One of them---the one who had been carrying the boy---limped noticeably, having apparently hurt his leg in a bad landing.

Strangely enough, it was the limping man who got there first. Splashing the glittering surface of the water noisily, he was in midstream in an instant and very nearly reached the boy with the curved end of his traveling staff, which he had never let go of. He managed to hook the half-unraveled rags that made up the little slave's clothing; it seemed the deed was done---but the next moment only a bundle of rags remained on the staff, while the bare little body slid on downstream.

The second servant ran clumsily along the bank, stumbling on the wet pebbles, but Galinthias could already see---he would not make it in time.

And indeed he did not, slipping and falling flat on his face just as some swift body literally tore through the tangle of bushes on the far bank, entering the current almost without a splash, not far from the seething foam of the rapids.

[A lion!]{.thoughts} flashed through the stunned Galinthias's mind. [The Man-Eater of Kithairon!]{.thoughts}

And a moment later, when a sun-browned young man, radiating calm strength, rose from the water right beside her, holding the rescued child in the crook of his slightly tensed left arm (the boy still looked drowsy and indifferent to everything, including his own life), Galinthias realized that it was not for nothing she had taken the chance rescuer for a lion---for a supple, graceful predator that even a real mountain lion would have been sorry to meet.

The young man pressed a bundle of short javelins to his side with his right elbow and smiled reassuringly at the old woman, seeing her cry out and clap a hand over her mouth---but he had misjudged the cause of her alarm. Five years had passed since Galinthias had last seen this face, but she could not be mistaken: before her stood Alkeides, exiled to Kithairon, son of Zeus and Alkmene, the living target of the daughter of Proitos.

Of the arrow loosed by Tartaros.

"Hail and rejoice and be glad, O hero, the peer of immortals," the old woman struck up in a loud singsong, instantly finding the right words, "praise shall I raise unto you and the gods who have guided your footsteps, out of the thicket who led you, Alkeides, the pride of Thebes, and---"

"You're mistaken, grandma!" The young man laughed, handing the child to the servant who had come running up, limping on his left leg. "I'm Iphikles, Alkeides's brother. Sorry if that's a disappointment..."

"Oh, Iphikles!" The old woman threw up her hands in delight, instantly forgetting her lofty manner of speech. "Oh, my little one! How's anyone to tell you and your brother apart?! Both heroes, both fine boys, your mom's treasure, your dad's pride! You don't remember me, I'd wager, but I'm grandma Galinthias---I chased the Eileithyiai away from your mom's bedside, gave you herbs to drink so your little tummies wouldn't ache, and your mama used to say: 'Galinthias dear, I never forget a kindness...'"

Iphikles was not listening very closely to the endless stream of the old woman's chatter. Galinthias's face seemed vaguely familiar---his mother really had told him something of the kind once... and for some reason he also remembered a frenzied crowd in front of the house, come to kill him, Iphikles.

Now why would that be?

Iphikles shook his wet head, chasing away the unpleasant vision, and suddenly hit on a way to get rid of the pestering old woman.

"Grandma Galinthias dear!" he hollered at the top of his lungs, whirling up onto the bluff and tossing the frail little body of the shrieking daughter of Proitos into the air. "What joy, what joy this is! And Alkeides---oh, Alkeides will be overjoyed, he'll be beside himself! He's right here, close by, carrying the hide of the Kithaironian lion after his feat---you just need to go up the little path, granny, and turn left past the old elm split by lightning... Just shout 'Alkei-ides!' and he'll appear at once! And how glad he'll be!... He simply won't believe his luck..."

Giving a last spiteful little chuckle, Iphikles took off at a run along the bluff, and so failed to notice the expression on the sharp little face of the old woman, who stood as if turned to stone, forgetting even to scold her negligent servants.

Galinthias, daughter of Proitos, had long since stopped trusting coincidences of this kind.

But... she had to hurry.
