#### 9

For most of the way back, Iphikles carried Amphitryon in his arms, paying no attention to the latter's feeble protests; only once did he threaten sternly, "I'll tan your hide!"---and then laugh mirthlessly.

Fortunately, the former lawagetas was, just then, no heavier than a talent.

The child's still-frail body, unable to bear the strain of this mad night, had refused to serve him---decisively and irrevocably, the way an exhausted horse collapses to the ground and cannot be made to rise again by any promise or blow; Iphikles carried his father the way one carries a son, or the way one man carries another.

While still some way off, catching the smell of smoke, both of them knew, by some seventh sense, that it was their own house burning. Iphikles drew a hoarse breath and quickened his pace, remembering the fake Iolaos's words about a fire twenty years past that had nearly cost the pregnant Alkmene her life; Amphitryon, meanwhile, thought about how everything in life comes around again---even life itself.

The gate stood wide open, and the courtyard was thronged with people.

It was hard to make sense of anything in the chaos, except that ghostly coils of smoke were creeping over the left wing of the house, where the children's quarters were. Slave women shrieked, three maidservants fussed over the unconscious Alkmene, buckets and jugs of water passed from hand to hand, yet no one seemed particularly eager to press into the heart of the fire, contenting themselves with bustle and shouting. Neighbors, roused from sleep, streamed in from the street, trying to put questions to anyone at all, Iphikles included---so that Iphikles had to tell two or three gawkers to go to Charon, set Amphitryon down in a safe spot by the fence, and grab hold of the cloak of some talkative bearded man with an empty jug.

Amphitryon couldn't hear what the bearded man was saying to his son---he only saw Iphikles turn terribly, inhumanly pale, tear the man's thick woolen cloak off him, wrap it around his head, snatch a bucket of water from someone and upend it over himself, and plunge into the house.

The way a traveler exhausted by heat plunges into a cool lake.

The bearded man made an eloquent circling gesture at his temple and sat down beside Amphitryon, leaning his back against the fence.

"If I didn't know it was the younger one," he announced excitedly, "I'd have said it was Alkeides the madcap. Lucky you, Iolaos, you'll save yourself the price of a funeral pyre..."

The emptied bucket struck the loose-tongued bearded man full force across the cheekbone.

"I'll kill you, scum!" The second blow of the bucket only grazed the joker as he leapt back, vanishing at once into the crowd of volunteer helpers.

Trying to rise, to go to Alkmene, who still hadn't come to her senses---the flare of anger had burned up whatever strength he had left---Amphitryon heard astonished shouts coming from the left wing. The crowd parted---and a smoking Iphikles, like a lion with its teeth locked in the throat of a slaughtered boar, dragged out an enormous body ([Alkeides!]{.thoughts} Amphitryon understood at once) and, handing his brother over to the servants, dove back into the fire.

Seconds blazed by one after another, piling up into the ash of minutes---until a staggering figure appeared again in the doorway, a scorched cloak over his head, walking blindly toward the crowd.

In his arms he carried a woman with badly burned legs, the whole right side of her face one solid bruise.

The woman was clutching a half-suffocated one-year-old girl to her chest.

"Asteropeia!" someone shouted from the crowd. "Asteropeia, with her daughter! But where's Megara? Did she burn to death?!"

People began explaining to the shouter that Megara wasn't in the house, that she'd gone to her mother's the evening before and stayed the night---but the crash of the collapsing roof cut all their explanations short.

And Amphitryon squeezed his eyes shut, picturing them all trudging to Hades even now: gray-eyed Therimachos, Megara's favorite; sturdy little Deikoon, a three-year-old brigand who so resembled Alkeides; behind him, hobbling along, Kreontiades, fallen silent for the first time; and on either side of him, moving without a sound, both of Iphikles's sons by Asteropeia...

And leading this shadowy procession, for some reason, was he himself... no, not he, but six-year-old Iolaos, killed by his grandfather, who had been sent to live a second time.

The children walked into a murk lit by crimson flashes.

Former children.

Shades.

...sacrifices.

"Murderer!" a desperate scream rang out, and Amphitryon, jolted back to himself, began looking around, trying to understand what was happening.

Asteropeia, brought back to her senses---her daughter had already been pried from her stiffened arms---simply collapsed onto Alkeides, who sat on the ground shaking his head blankly, and clawed at his face with her nails.

"It was him!" wailed the tormented woman, in whom it was impossible to recognize the once quiet, gentle Asteropeia. "Curse you, monster! People, I wanted to stop him---but who could ever stop him, a raging bull like that!... He killed them, killed them all, the cursed madman, and set the fire besides---people, beat him, beat him, in the name of the innocent dead! Murderer! My boys, my boys..."

Still wearing the same blank expression on his scratched face, Alkeides suddenly stood and walked slowly toward the gate. People drew back before him, staring in fear at his mighty arms, hanging limp and powerless, as though expecting to see traces of children's blood on them; people shied away from the madman, whispering of the gods' curse and inescapable retribution.

"Whom Zeus would destroy, he first makes mad," someone remarked, quite close to Amphitryon.

"Zeus?" a young woman's voice echoed doubtfully. "More likely Hera the Stepmother... oh, what difference does it make now! Woe, woe, such bitter woe..."

Alkeides walked out of the courtyard, and no one followed him.

And by morning, Iphikles too had vanished.
