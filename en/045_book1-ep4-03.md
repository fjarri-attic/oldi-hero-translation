#### 3

The palaestra by night was strikingly unlike the palaestra by day --- noisy, sharp with the smell of healthy sweat, ringing with excited voices; discuses soaring into the sky, arrows tearing free of bowstrings, the clatter of blunted practice swords, the stern shouts of teachers, the whoops of carried-away boys...

None of that was here now.

A sleepy kingdom, a forge of heroes gone cold, the Asphodel Meadows, where the shades of unrealized dreams wandered unheard, clutching the ghostly shafts of spears that missed their mark, crossing again and again a finish line that others had crossed first, gathering up weightless discuses that had fallen short and astray... the nighttime palaestra, tediously meaningless, like an abandoned house, like a cracked jug; like a body with a broken neck.

When one of the shades rose from the far bench of the western stands and waved a hand at Amphitryon --- he first decided he had imagined it, sober as he was.

"Rejoice, Amphitryon!" the shade said quietly, leaning too hard on the vowels, as the Laconians did.

"Exactly, rejoice," a bench nearby answered in a muffled voice, stirring slightly. "A wise man you are, Castor, always ready with the right word at the right time..."

Amphitryon walked up to the stands, climbed the aisle, and sat a little below Castor, so that his own head came level with Autolycus, who lay stretched out on the bench.

"I'm rejoicing," he muttered. "Rejoicing indeed... enough joy to drive a man mad."

"It's all my fault," Autolycus announced unexpectedly. "You understand, Amphitryon, Alcides started showing me a throw, but done his own way, not how I taught it... in short, I take one look and it's a dangerous throw, meant for an enemy, not a palaestra! You mustn't do such a thing at thirteen, you mustn't even attempt it --- too much nonsense still in the head, the arms not yet come into their full strength, they won't hold it the way they should... and I'm not even speaking of the rules! I made a fool of him in front of everyone, Alcides, I made him demonstrate it on me --- and, curse the boy, he demonstrated it well, only I was the only one who understood just how well, because it's too early yet for Alcides to be throwing Autolycus not by the rules... I knocked his knee out from under him at the end, rolled him in the dust, the young ones braying around us like horses, and he tore free and ran off! Half an hour later he's back as if nothing happened, and I go at him with my staff for the willfulness of it --- and he flings a handful of gravel at me and runs off again... I watch him go and think: why has Alcides gone and changed into a chiton? And then, closer to evening, Polyctor's mother raises a commotion by the palaestra: seems the sons of Amphitryon broke Linus the songster's neck, either together or one after the other... The fool! Screeched it across the whole town, the venomous shrew! But to you, Amphitryon, I said it and I'll say it again: I'm the one to blame! I forgot that boys that age are at their most colt-headed, when pride fogs the mind worse than wine..."

Autolycus shifted his weight heavily, drawing air through his nostrils with a rasp --- it seemed these words had come hard to Autolycus, son of Hermes, whom the shrewdest judges of all Hellas had never once managed to find guilty of anything.

"What if we just... let it drop, and be done with it?" Castor suggested uncertainly. "We spread word that Linus---"

"Broke his own neck!" Autolycus snapped.

"No, well, why not? There are no witnesses to any killing, a couple of boys' words don't count, and Linus has no kin left besides Orpheus --- and Orpheus is off somewhere in Pieria now, or maybe Iolcus... By the time word reaches him, by the time the matter's settled --- whatever we say will be what's remembered! And we'll say that Linus, son of the muse Calliope and Oeagrus, grew proud, took himself for something he wasn't, and challenged Apollo himself to a musical contest! And Apollo, well, he up and killed the impious wretch..."

"Who won the contest, at least?" Amphitryon asked, indifferent.

"Apollo, obviously... Musagetes."

"Ah... well, then it's all in order, since it was Musagetes," Autolycus snorted. "Now if the late Linus had won, and Apollo had gone and cracked him over the neck with his kithara for it---"

Castor took offense and fell silent.

"Don't sulk, Castor," Amphitryon said, patting the Laconian's knee in conciliation. "Somewhere off in Pieria, perhaps, they'd believe it, but here in Boeotia, and in Thebes especially --- not likely... And besides --- you can't stop every mouth. I bet it isn't only Polyctor's mother howling by now --- the whole city's boiling over."

"They're taking wagers," Autolycus put in bitterly, rolling onto his side. "The gossiping vermin!"

"Wagers?"

"On which of the boys killed Linus: Alcides or Iphicles?! Neither will confess... or rather, they both confess --- both of them! The Thebans remembered, Tartarus their souls" ("Take their souls to Tartarus," Castor corrected, out of habit), "that Alcmene had twins! Some say it was Alcides --- who else but the future hero to go breaking other men's necks?! And Hera struck him mad, besides. And others say Iphicles --- that the whole line runs that way in his family, and anyway he's not the son... well, of that one... I mean, he is a son, but not..."

"Not the son of Zeus," Castor finished for him. "I'm not one myself, either, unlike my brother, Polydeuces. Don't take offense, Amphitryon --- I'm allowed to say such things. I know it on my own hide."

"I'm not offended," Amphitryon smiled faintly. "You can't imagine, Castor the Dioscurus, just how much I'm not offended..."

Nyx the Night's veil had begun to fade, thousand-eyed Argus was blinking more and more often, and the outlines of the palaestra's inner buildings emerged more sharply from the dark, which was growing reluctant to remain dark. A chilly little wind went racing along the running tracks, brushing its wing in passing against the three men in the stands, and swept off, offended by such indifference to itself.

"Someone's coming," Castor said suddenly. "Do you hear?"

A figure appeared from behind the gymnasium, barely distinguishable at such a distance, but Amphitryon knew, some inner instinct telling him even a moment before she came into view --- it was Alcmene. His heart stumbled like an athlete who has turned his ankle, then limped onward, and Amphitryon watched, listless, as his wife rounded the gymnasium and turned toward the western stands.

Her ankle bracelets barely ringing, Alcmene walked past the stands, and the wind, returned and playful, tugged at the edge of her festive striped himation, pinned at the shoulders with six silver brooches.

Amphitryon partly saw all this, and partly simply knew it --- because this was exactly how Alcmene dressed for the great festivals; and the six silver brooches slashed across his eyes like six bolts of lightning, scattering like dry sand beneath his eyelids.

"So here you are," Alcmene said, toneless, stopping by the front row without raising her head; and the men above looked down at her, and any words caught in their throats, breaking free only as hoarse breath. "Here you are... I thought as much. Everyone's asleep, and the boys are asleep... I went in to see them, and they'd thrown off their covers, sprawled out, both of them damp with sweat, and their faces so angry, so angry... You won't believe it --- for the first time I couldn't tell them apart. Both the same... I wanted to cover them again..."

She was unspeakably beautiful in that moment, when the pre-dawn dimness smoothed with its merciful brush every implacable mark of age, lighting only the white patch of her face with its bottomless depths of eyes; and dark-haired Lyssa the Rage touched Alcmene with her fingertips, shook her head in refusal, and stepped back from the Thunderer's last woman.

"I want a grandson," Alcmene whispered, rising onto her toes and flinging her arms up toward the sky. "A grandson... or a granddaughter. Only it's too soon yet... What are you punishing me for, gods? You took my love, you took my husband, now you're taking my sons... Am I the guilty one? Should I have borne only one? I want a grandson... Not a hero. An ordinary one..."

She turned and walked away with a light, gliding step, murmuring something inaudible, dancing a little as she went, girlishly.

Amphitryon could not bear it --- he turned away.

"You'd better go after her," Autolycus breathed hotly into Amphitryon's ear, and, seeing him rise obediently, added: "No telling --- she might lose her way, or worse... And one more thing --- I didn't want to say it before, but I see now I'll have to. That throw, the one I made a fool of Alcides over in front of everyone---"

"The throw?" Amphitryon turned back, bewildered, making his way into the aisle. "Ah, yes, the throw... What of it?"

"The thing is, my own father taught me that throw once. A bit different, true, but then I was older than your boys are now, and taller too... but otherwise --- a familiar habit of hand. You ought to think on that, all right?"

"Yes, all right," Amphitryon nodded, descending from the stands, barely taking in Autolycus's words. "All right, of course..."

Already at the very exit from the palaestra, almost catching up with his wife, he suddenly remembered whose son Autolycus Hermesid was, and shuddered, feeling a wave of faint cold pass somewhere deep within him.

An old, half-forgotten sensation... like before his very first battle, the one he had come through alive by a miracle.
