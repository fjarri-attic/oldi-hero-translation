#### 6

This old blind rhapsode[^38], embodying, so to speak, a kind of Helicon[^39] of the Theban marketplace, had been plinking away at an out-of-tune lyre here almost since the founding of the city.

[^38]: Rhapsode (otherwise called an aoidos) --- a reciter of poetry.

[^39]: Helicon --- a mountain where the Muses gathered.

And his songs were always the same ones.

"Hermeias, swift-footed Trickster, herald of the gods, son of Maia Pleiades and of dread Dias the Kronid..."

If the blind man ever called other gods to mind, it was always in connection with the aforementioned:

"Phoebus-Apollo, of the silver bow, whose curved-horned kine Hermeias Cyllenios stole..."

"O Poseidon, Earth-Shaker, whose mighty trident wing-footed Hermes once bore off..."

"The helm that one from sight hides, worn by the Anax of Hades, whose name is forbidden to mortals --- stolen once, and by whom, would you guess? Aye, Achaeans! By Hermes the Trickster!.."

That Hermeias, for instance, had invented the lyre --- of this the rhapsode rarely spoke, preferring to describe the beloved god's shadier doings in finely chiseled verse, styling them more often as deeds and less often as feats.

Still, people gave generously enough --- the marketplace had its hand in that.

Who, after all, was going to sing Apollo's praises here?!

Amphitryon gazed grimly at the one-god rhapsode, listening to his piercing voice, and simply could not work it out: why was it enough to walk up to the blind man and drop two cured fish into his begging bowl (necessarily cured, never salted, or, say, smoked) --- and the next day, having walked the Dromos first traveled by a furious Amphitryon five years before, he would unfailingly find a half-ruined house, on whose threshold a hook-nosed youth in winged sandals would invariably be sitting and waving a greeting?!

Hermeias himself had taught Amphitryon this foolproof method of getting in touch, once Amphitryon had left his exiled sons on Cithaeron and returned to Thebes --- though Amphitryon himself still could not fathom what cured fish, a blind rhapsode, and the Trickster's appearance in a particular spot had to do with one another.

Over the past five years the former lawagetas had made use of Hermeias's trick some six or seven times, whenever he longed to see his sons but business kept him from leaving the city for a month or two on a trip to Cithaeron.

In the morning (having honored the rhapsode with the requisite fish the evening before) Amphitryon would set off for the northern outskirts almost at a run --- though not quite at a run, feeling the weight of half a century lived upon shoulders still sturdy --- would find the right spot among the hills on the third try, take a step or two, feel an unpleasant chill low in his belly and a faint dizziness, wave back to Hermeias rising from the threshold, and a short conversation would follow...

And once again Amphitryon understood why the youth-god was called the Guide. The chill low in his belly would grow, he'd have to squeeze his eyes shut and grip the other's slender wrist tighter, his ears would always pop, and then Hermeias would laugh, Amphitryon would swallow convulsively and open his eyes, to see his sons running toward him.

In the evening Hermeias would bring him home.

But now, today... no, it was not for another journey to Cithaeron that Amphitryon wanted to see the god in winged sandals.

Not for that at all.

Two days before, a messenger had come running into Thebes and collapsed, gasping, at the city gates.

Less than an hour had passed before every Theban, young and old, was repeating a single word --- a word in which many words of human speech had fused into one, because this word was a boundary, a border separating the unshakeable "now" from the possible "never."

War!

War with Orchomenus.

The madman Alcides had lain in wait for the Minyan envoys coming for the yearly tribute and, for no apparent reason, savaged the venerable Orchomenians, cutting off their hands, noses, and ears, then hanging the severed members around the wretches' necks and driving the envoys back home with kicks.

"The boy wasn't hurt, at least?" --- that was all Alcmene asked, on learning what had happened.

What could one do --- a mother is a mother, all the more so since her one-year-old grandson Iolaus, a thin and sickly little boy, was at that moment the only reality that mattered to Alcmene.

When Amphitryon walked to Creon's palace the following afternoon, the young people hailed with shouts the earthly father of the great hero who had freed Thebes from its shameful tribute, while their elders kept a grim silence or whispered of the gods' wrath and the hardships of the coming war.

But it was not the old friend and patron Creon who received Amphitryon at the palace --- it was Creon the basileus, a statesman thinking broadly and with foresight.

First, Amphitryon was to understand that Thebes was not ready for war, whatever the wild-eyed youths --- who had nothing to lose but their own lives, of no use to the city --- might shout about it in the streets; and Alcides's overreach could in no way be excused.

Second, if it turned out that this act of brigandage againsts the Minyan envoys displeased the gods --- and why should it please them, when it displeased him, Creon of Thebes?! --- then Thebes would submissively bow its head before warlike Orchomenus, just as it had bowed once before, when the Minyan Clymenus, father of the present Orchomenian basileus Erginus, was accidentally killed by the Theban charioteer Perieres during athletic games; and since a hundred cattle a year would hardly settle matters this time, all of Amphitryon's property might very well be handed over to the Minyans as ransom.

Third, if the Orchomenians should demand that the villain Alcides be handed over, well --- you understand yourself, dear Amphitryon, and, by the way, how is the fair Alcmene enjoying being a grandmother?... Ah well, in that case everything's fine, give her my Nausicaa's regards.

Fourth, should Alcides's deed turn out after all to be pleasing to the gods, the valiant Amphitryon, that brilliant commander and seasoned warrior, was to work out a plan of military action, and to send messengers in good time to Autolycus Hermesid and Castor Dioscurus, who had left Thebes, with a view to enlisting them as allies; as for Iphitus of Oechalia, he unfortunately should not be approached, since basileus Eurytus was recalling his heir to Euboea, as he had informed them by messenger.

With that, the audience was over.

And now Amphitryon stood waiting for the blind rhapsode to finish singing his latest panegyric to Hermes Psychopompos, so that he might drop two cured (invariably cured, never sun-dried, or, say, fried) fish into the man's chipped bowl.

He waited it out.

Dropped them in.

And walked home, feeling the crowd's stares on his back: admiring, from the young; irritated, from the old; doubtful, from those of middle age; frightened and hateful, from the women.

All of them were Thebans.

And Amphitryon himself had long since come to feel a Theban, having all but forgotten his native Mycenae.

...The fish worked without fail --- the Cyllenian was waiting for Amphitryon on the threshold of his utterly unchanged house; and Hermeias himself looked exactly as he always had.

Except, perhaps, that he was not smiling as he usually did.

"Rejoice, Hermeias," Amphitryon said wearily, sitting down beside him and trying not to settle on the hem of Hermes's chiton of Pontic linen.

"I wonder who does his laundry," flashed a thoroughly foolish thought.

"Rejoice, Amphitryon," the ever-young god answered seriously, and it suddenly seemed to Amphitryon that bitter creases had settled at the corners of Hermeias's sensuous mouth, and that from his watchful eyes --- the one thing that betrayed the Trickster's age --- telltale little wrinkles had spread and almost at once vanished. "Rejoice, Amphitryon, though there's little enough for either of us to rejoice about."

After these words the two of them were silent for a while.

"What of the boys?" Amphitryon finally forced out.

"With them everything's just fine --- which is more than can be said for the Minyans. Only... there are far too many coincidences here. The three of us sat down --- Dionysus, Pan, and I --- and decided to wet the occasion... you understand, it's not every day that brothers go killing Cithaeron lions." (Amphitryon started, seeming to grow younger all at once, but did not interrupt Hermeias.) "We'd barely finished off the first wineskin after our heroes left, when up runs an old acquaintance of ours --- the notch-eared satyr Phoroneus. And shouts that he's seen my dear brother Ares on Cithaeron! No less!"

"Enyalius?" Amphitryon couldn't hold back. "The god of war?! Was he really on Cithaeron?!"

Hermeias spread his hands.

"I don't know. Though, on the other hand --- why would Phoroneus lie? Especially since there's nothing to be proud of in such a meeting. Phoroneus goes: 'Rejoice, great helm-bearing Ares!' --- and Ares gave Phoroneus a kick in the backside and vanished. Opened a Dromos and left, most likely. Anyway, I heard Phoroneus out, felt uneasy, and we finished off the second wineskin..."

Amphitryon's eloquent stare achieved the impossible --- the Trickster looked embarrassed.

"Yes, I can see for myself now that I caught on too late... well, I just don't like Ares! So I decided to sit it out with Pan somewhere out of the way, so as not to run into him again! By evening word of the slaughter was already spreading through the forest; I went straight to the shepherds' camp, and --- no brothers there! They only turned up by morning, pale, eyes fixed on the ground... A fit, they say, had come over Alcides at the worst possible moment, and then --- the Minyans, as if on purpose!"

"So it was a fit after all," Amphitryon whispered, resigned, and all his false youth was blown away as if by the wind.

Fifty years, to the year, half his head gone gray.

"And Iphicles --- where was Iphicles?!"

"Exactly, I pounced on him too: where were you, you scoundrel, why did you let it happen? I laid into him, and he laid into me..." Hermeias, for some reason, rolled his head as if working out a stiff neck, and the Trickster's eyes darkened with the memory. "In short, your Iphicles got the better of me. No one seems to be at fault, really: the fit was unexpected, Iphicles ran on ahead about some business, the Minyans fell on them like rain out of nowhere, and Ares was all but passing through on business of his own --- yet somehow it all came together in the worst possible way. I don't believe in coincidences like that!"

"No one is at fault," Amphitryon repeated slowly, as if not hearing Hermeias's last words. "The envoys are maimed, war is at the door, and no one but Alcides is at fault..."

"Not quite so, worthy lawagetas," the Trickster smiled faintly, for the first time in the whole conversation. "Not quite so. Alcides, exiled from Thebes --- that is, not entirely a full-fledged Theban --- met the Orchomenians on neutral ground, where they, in turn, were not yet full-fledged envoys to him." (Amphitryon's head snapped up, drinking in the words of the youth-god as greedily as a dry sponge drinks in water.) "Six armed Minyans, coming upon one unarmed Alcides, forgot their manners and took to insulting our hero --- overstepping themselves until, at last, they insulted his father!"

"So what?" Amphitryon didn't understand. "Some reason for war, that --- the Minyans insulted me!"

"What do you have to do with it?" Hermeias asked, very convincingly astonished. "I'm telling you: they insulted Alcides's Father! Got it --- or shall I repeat?"

"But... the fit?" Amphitryon faltered, never before having stopped to think what exactly had earned the Trickster his name.

"What fit? A fit of righteous fury that seized the hero when he heard blasphemies against the Thunderer himself?!"

"Did they... did they really blaspheme against Zeus?"

"And what do you care? It's enough that Zeus himself thinks so."

"So Alcides is innocent?"

"So, innocent. And at this very moment a delegation of Theban notables, led by basileus Creon himself, is on its way back from the seer Tiresias, who has told them all they need to know."

"That's good... Hermeias, that's very good! That we'll be in the right in the eyes of the Achaeans --- that's simply wonderful! This very day I'll take up the authority of lawagetas and..."

"Are you planning to attack, or to defend?"

"I didn't interrupt you, Hermeias!" Amphitryon cut in, hard. "But I'll answer: we're forced to attack. The war with Orchomenus will be backed mostly by the young, and the young don't know how to defend --- they'll burn themselves out waiting behind the walls... and our walls, besides, have fallen into disrepair. I'll lead the Thebans against Orchomenus myself!"

"Then Alcides and Iphicles will join you at the Eastern Pass."

"Very well. But first I'd like to know --- whose side will you gods be on? A poor lawagetas is one who doesn't try to learn the plans of every side involved... because otherwise there's no telling friend from foe."

"And you, a mortal, ask this of me?! Of me, one of the twelve Olympians?!"

"Yes, I ask. I'd ask the others --- but whom? I can hardly go to a temple, or to some fortune-teller... Well?"

And Hermeias began to speak --- slowly, stumbling, astonished at himself.

* * *

...on that occasion, he had come in time. It was from him, swift-footed Hermeias, that dread Dias the Father had learned of the blaspheming envoys and of the punishment that had overtaken the Minyans at the hero's hand. So that when the troubled Family gathered on Olympus, the Thunderer only smiled, for he knew more than the rest.

Or so he thought.

The gods did not dare contradict the Kronid --- his son by Alcmene had proven blameless before the Family. But when Zeus wished to spare his beloved son the hardships and dangers of war, to preserve him for deeds still to come, the gods grumbled, even those who had always obeyed the word of Dias.

Only bloody Ares was absent from Olympus, but who did not know Enyalius's opinion of any war, and of heroes' part in it? Nor did Anax Aides (Hades, in mortal speech) appear, but sent word instead through Hermeias: "The wars of the living are no concern of mine. The shades of the dead, however, I will receive."

And Hera, Spouse of the Sovereign, said: "The godlike hero has begun a war --- well then, let us see what he is capable of!"

And Poseidon the Earth-Shaker spoke: "I agree with my Sister. I have more hero-sons of my own than the sea could hold. I'd like to learn from Zeus just what sort of heroes one ought to beget!"

And they were seconded by silver-bowed Phoebus-Apollo, and his sister Artemis the virgin, and Athena Tritogeneia, the Defender of Cities, and Hestia, and others.

But when they asked Bacchus-Dionysus for his opinion, and the curly-haired god duly gave it, Zeus the Father drove him out by the scruff of the neck, together with his bubbling opinion, for the Family council was threatening to turn into just another drinking bout.

"So be it," the Thunderer decreed. "My son shall take part in the war between Thebes and Orchomenus. But let the Family, too, stand aside and take no part in the quarrel! You wished to see the hero in action --- watch, then, but woe to whoever meddles in the fighting! Everyone dismissed."

And the gods gathered on Olympus swore by black Styx that they would be mere spectators in the war of mortals.

* * *

All this the Trickster related to Amphitryon.

"Thank you again, Hermeias," came the answer. "That's what I wanted to know: the outcome of the war depends on us, on men, alone. And still, you're right --- there are too many coincidences. Alcides's sudden fit, Iphicles not being nearby, the Minyans turning up... no, first Iphicles, then the Minyans, and only then the fit... ah yes, and Ares roaming about Cithaeron, kicking satyrs! Listen, Hermeias --- was anyone else seen nearby?"

"Isn't one Ares enough for you?"

"Well, if not one of the gods --- a mortal, or something else?!"

"What are you badgering me for?! There's no telling who was there --- satyrs, nymphs, shepherds, billy goats and nanny goats, that old woman with the grandson Iphicles saved, and then I was there myself, with Pan and Dionysus!.. Is that enough for you?!"

"Wait! What old woman?"

"That Iphicles of yours met some old crone along the way... but what does she have to do with any of this?! An old woman, that's all..."

"I'm the one asking the questions here!" the reddened Amphitryon barked, as if interrogating a prisoner.

And caught himself short.

"Well, you understand," he finished, quite out of place, in an apologetic tone.

But the taken-aback Hermeias apparently did understand something --- or pretended to --- and set about recalling aloud the details of Iphicles's account, details that had already begun slipping from his memory.

"Her name --- what was her name?!" Amphitryon all but shouted by the end of the Cyllenian's reminiscing. "Did Iphicles tell you?"

"He did," the Trickster answered, at a loss. "And I forgot."

"Remember, Hermeias, try!"

"What's gotten into you over this old woman! Well, she was walking from Thebes to Cithaeron, wanted to visit some kin, most likely... says she nursed the brothers when they were children, their little bellies used to ache, the gluttons!.. And she used to chase the Eileithyiai away from the birthing kline..."

"Galinthias!" Amphitryon breathed out sharply. "Galinthias, daughter of Proetus!"

"That's it, Galinthias!" Hermeias said, delighted. "But... what of it?"

"Nothing, for now. Just another coincidence. Here's what interests me --- Galinthias used to say she served Hecate... tell me, Hermeias, do you gods keep track of your regular servants?"

"Of course. Not all of them, but many."

"Then do me the kindness of asking Hecate what she knows about her priestess Galinthias, daughter of Proetus of Thebes."

Hermeias gave his companion a strangely sidelong glance, but nodded all the same.

"And another thing," the lawagetas went on. "One more strange point you've forgotten..."

"What is it?"

"Why didn't Alcides kill the Minyans?"

"What's so strange about that?" Hermeias shrugged, bewildered, looking straight at Amphitryon.

At a gray-haired, heavyset mortal of fifty, his face a mass of hollows and folds of flaking skin, the veins bulging on his hairy shins, the biceps of his right arm --- once slashed open by someone's sword --- knit back together crookedly.

"If those Minyan bastards" --- Amphitryon's eyes flared for an instant with scorching flame --- "had insulted *my* father, Alcaeus of Mycenae, I'd have killed them! And I swear by Aphrodite's armpits, not one damned soul would ever have learned why the envoys of Orchomenus vanished on Cithaeron without ever reaching Thebes!"

"Even the gods?" the Trickster asked, half in jest, half in earnest.

Amphitryon did not answer.

His eyes went out, his body, tensed a moment before, went slack --- and Hermeias suddenly, for no reason at all, remembered that it was this very man sitting before him who, twenty years ago, had heeded the pleas of a tormented Creon, whose fields the monstrous Teumessian fox was laying waste, a beast that had to be bought off with sixteen-year-old youths, since no one, ever, could overtake the wonder-fox; that it was Amphitryon the Exile who, vanishing from Thebes for half a year, had persuaded --- at what cost no one knew --- Cephalus the hunter to give up his wonder-hound, born not of any bitch, from which no quarry could ever escape --- and, returning to Creon, had set the impossible upon the impossible.

Neither pursuer nor pursued had been seen by anyone since. It was said that Zeus had turned them both to stone --- but then, where were those stones?..

And Amphitryon did not like it when people called that work of his a feat.
