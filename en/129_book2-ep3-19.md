#### 19

In southern Thessaly there is a mountain ridge called Oeta.

It is renowned for nothing among the other mountains.

It is no match for white-headed Olympus, from which the gods ascend to the sky, no equal to wooded Pelion, the dwelling of the wise centaur Chiron, no peer to gloomy Taygetus, birthplace of the brothers Dioscuri.

Oeta will be famous for one thing only --- the pass of Thermopylae; but before the days of that fame, when three hundred Spartans with their king Leonidas will lay down their bones at Thermopylae, eight hundred years more must pass.

And another fame, a terrible one, of this obscure mountain ridge was being born today, at sunset, at a sunset crimson as a clotted wound, crowning the day of Heracles's death...

::: {.small-scene-break}
:::

Everyone was looking at Iolaus.

It seemed that even the disheveled pines, bristling with brittle yellowed needles, even the mossy stones scattered fancifully all around, even the bustling red ants underfoot and the pair of hawks tirelessly circling over the warriors' heads --- the whole world was looking at Iolaus, waiting for him to take the burning torch and make the first step.

A step toward the huge woodpile, almost the height of a man, stacked from dry trunks and generously strewn with brushwood, at the very top of which, hunched over, sat Heracles in a torn chiton soaked with blood, others' and his own.

At his feet, half hidden by branches sticking out in all directions, lay the dead Lichas.

It had been Heracles's only sensible act over the last several days --- he had ordered the messengers's body, broken in the fall, to be brought to him, had looked at it for a long time with dry, feverish eyes, and then whispered something and turned away.

"My little Hermes..." --- so it seemed to Ceyx, who was standing nearby.

But Heracles did not allow Lichas's body to be taken away: even raging, thrashing in agony and tearing the cursed chiton off along with his own flesh, demanding a funeral pyre, for it is not fitting for the dead to remain in the world of the living --- madman that he was, he did not stir a step from his messenger, and took him along onto the pyre.

He carried him in his arms; and blood from Heracles's lip, bitten clean through, dripped onto the chest of Lichas, whom he himself had killed.

Now Heracles and Lichas were the only ones not looking at Iolaus, waiting for his first step.

"Come on!" Philoctetes, standing nearby, blurted out, unable to bear it, forgetting himself in morbid excitement. "Go! Well, what are you waiting for?!"

Iolaus, turning sharply, thrust the burning torch into his hands, nearly singeing the Thessalian's tousled beard.

"Want it?" he shouted, and for a moment choked on a racking cough, having swallowed the acrid resinous smoke. "Want to get into legend?! I don't give a damn, I've been there a long time, I've had my fill of that happiness! --- but for you this is the only chance! There won't be a second one! Step into eternity, Philoctetes! Here it is, the straw for you to clutch at --- the torch! Jab your fire into the era! When they remember Heracles --- they'll remember you too, Philoctetes the Thessalian! They'll call you a friend, a hero, one of the few... Light it!"

"Will you give me the bow?" Philoctetes asked quietly, licking his lips.

And, seeing that Iolaus was staring at him uncomprehendingly, he began to mutter quickly:

"Heracles's bow --- to me, as an inheritance! As a keepsake! And the arrows... the ones in the Lernaean poison! Give me the bow with the arrows --- and I'll light it! Well?!"

Iolaus burst out laughing.

[Light it, vulture!]{.thoughts} Iolaus laughed, and those of the warriors who stood closer warily backed away, as if afraid of catching the hereditary madness. [Gods, isn't this a mockery?! --- Philoctetes the scavenger lighting the funeral pyre under a still-living Heracles!]{.thoughts}

Taking Iolaus's laughter for consent, the Thessalian basileus frowned in concentration, took several hurried little steps and, evidently afraid that Iolaus would change his mind, hastily jabbed the torch into the woodpile.

The dry brushwood caught at once.

Dove-gray worms crawled, writhing, among the branches, fussily jerking their fiery little heads; the yellow wrinkled leaves crackled, curling up into tubes; a whiff of smoke drifted through the air; a lone tongue of flame darted out from behind the outermost trunk, tenderly licked Lichas's leg and timidly hid back in the woodpile; a sudden gust of wind splashed against the palisade of boughs, making the logs yield obediently to the power of the fire; the air around the blazing pyre trembled glassily, as if opening a new, unseen Dromos between life and death...

The warriors stared wide-eyed at the raging flames, from which no sound came --- except the usual hymn of a blazing fire.

The warriors were afraid.

Because in the rare gaps between the fiery bursts, in the rents of the curtain of smoke, they saw what no mortal may see.

Heracles was still sitting in the very heart of the scarlet flower that had opened at an untimely hour near the summit of Oeta, and the tongues of fire fawned on the motionless man like a litter of blind puppies, nuzzling the living body but unable to bite.

The pyre was on its own; Heracles --- on his own.

The sky grumbled in bewilderment, shaggy clouds moved swiftly in from the northwest, from the direction of great Olympus, a single raindrop fell on Iolaus's nose, he started, raised his eyes to the sky --- and at that instant, out of the darkness thickening overhead, a lightning bolt struck the pyre.

And sank, dissolved in the funeral flame, like a river that has run all the way to the sea.

A second lightning bolt.

A third.

Fire was taking fire into itself.

The warriors fell to their knees.

"Zeus is calling his son!" someone rasped, and the words rushed about among the people, gaining flesh and weight, becoming faith and conviction.

"Zeus is calling his son!.."

Iolaus remained standing, looking over the pyre --- and that was why he saw the stormy darkness crack and split apart, a silvery web weave itself into the lace of a Dromos, and out of it, out of the corridor flung open, a golden chariot came hurtling down upon Oeta.

The Thunderer was driving straight at the fire.

[He's afraid,]{.thoughts} --- Iolaus's experienced eye did not miss the barely noticeable tremor of Zeus's mighty hands gripping the reins, the shadow of bewilderment that had fallen over the imperious bearded face, the god's feverishly glittering eyes and the fear beating under his bushy brows, ordinary human fear. [He's afraid... Come for your son, Junior? For *my* son?! Why is it you only ever come to dying men, Dias the father? --- to me at Orchomenus, to Heracles on Oeta... I bet you showed yourself to the women more often?! And as a driver you're... good thing the horses pull you through! And you've brought your daughter along for courage, your favorite, Athena Promachos --- there she is, clutching at the sides, just like Philoctetes... afraid of flying out, I bet...]{.thoughts}

All around, the warriors gasped in shock.

Heracles was rising from the pyre to meet the chariot.

He was enormous, far bigger than in life, his bare arms bulged with monstrous muscles, a lionskin hung from his shoulders, glossy, brand new, as if only just flayed from the beast; and Heracles's face was indifferently calm, like the face of a man receiving the agreed-upon payment for work done.

It was a face Iolaus did not know.

Someone else's.

And when this new, majestic Heracles, shedding his mortal form, stepped into the chariot that had halted beside him --- it at once became crowded.

The horses neighed, arching their swan-like necks, and rushed toward the Dromos pulsing with silver light.

Iolaus wanted very much for the hero standing beside Zeus to look back.

No.

He did not look back.

:::

...The warriors had long since dispersed, talking excitedly, but Iolaus still stood looking at the ashes, in which the embers of fifty years of his life were smoldering.

Cooling embers, filming over with ash.

I have to go, he told himself.

I have to go.

He forced himself to turn around, to take a few steps...

He stopped.

Before him stood Hermeias.

"Come for the funeral?" Iolaus asked quietly. "Let's go, Trickster, let's drink to the memory of the ascended one... did you see what a fine fellow he was?! The son of Zeus..."

Hermeias was silently looking past Iolaus.

At the ashes.

"What did you see there?" Iolaus forced out a smirk. "Ashes and cinders..."

Hermeias was silent.

It seemed to Iolaus that an invisible hand came down on his shoulder and made him turn around.

By the burnt-out pyre stood a shade in a lionskin.

The old, shabby, moth-eaten skin stirred faintly in the wind, and the shade was slightly doubled, trembling in the glow of the embers, as if it could not make out: was it one, or were there two of them?

At the shade's feet sat Lichas, pensively pouring a handful of ashes from palm to palm. He looked almost alive, only he resembled not the grown warrior whom Iolaus had recently recognized only by his voice, but the sixteen-year-old boy he had been when he rode to Euboea.

The shade in the lionskin looked toward the father and the god --- Iolaus caught his breath, because the shade's face was precisely the face he had been used to seeing beside him for half a century, one face shared by two, not at all like the cold visage of the hero in the golden chariot --- and waved its hand.

The Trickster, as if obeying a soundless call, moved forward.

He walked slowly and warily, the way a dog approaches its master after having just bitten his hand and not knowing whether it will be forgiven or not; he walked and walked, the Trickster, Dummy, Psychopompos the Soul-Guide, and when he came up, the shade put its arms around the god's shoulders.

And so they went away, accompanied by Lichas, who ran skipping after them.

Iolaus wanted very much for his son to look back before leaving forever.

He looked back.

::: {.small-scene-break}
:::

"Shall we drink?" asked a rather tipsy Philoctetes, peering into Iolaus's eyes. "After all, Heracleses don't die every day..."

"Yes," Iolaus agreed. "Not every day. All the more so since today a man died --- and a god was born."

"Is that good?" Philoctetes raised his brows in puzzlement; Ceyx's men had lost no time in nicknaming him Archemorus --- the Beginner of Doom.

Iolaus did not answer.
