#### 7

"...This Eurytus is truly a good archer," Autolycus muttered thoughtfully, running his fingers through his thick beard from habit. "A good teacher too... but otherwise, it seems, a bastard."

"What's Eurytus got to do with it?" Castor shrugged in bewilderment, sitting beside Autolycus on the western stand and watching the lanky Iphitus and the stocky Theban Myrtilus --- the two had just nocked their arrows and were now drawing their bows.

From here the whole stadium field was clearly visible, and Castor noted to himself that the target stood a little crooked; then he turned his gaze back to the archers and thought that Iphitus's bow suited its owner --- about half again as long as Myrtilus's.

And Iphitus drew it not to the chest, as Theban archers were used to doing, but barbarian-style, all the way to the ear.

And Castor, being a true Laconian, also thought that the bow was a coward's weapon. Well, perhaps not a coward's, exactly, but certainly not a real man's.

"It's got to do with the fact that it wasn't Apollo who taught Iphitus to shoot, but Eurytus," Autolycus explained his point to Castor as though to a child. "His father, after all..."

Autolycus was watching the field without much attention, thinking that he shouldn't have sat down next to Dioscurus.

"A-a-ah," Castor nodded knowingly.

Autolycus glanced sidelong at Dioscurus's serene face and did not speak to him again for the rest of the contest.

Young Iphitus's bow straightened with a quiet hum --- and the arrow, whistling through the air, struck the center of the target with a dull thud. Frowning and intent, Myrtilus took aim a while longer, then he too loosed the string.

A second arrow blossomed in the center of the target --- and the crowd on the stands broke into cheers.

They were cheering for their own man, the Theban.

Myrtilus smiled with effort and wiped the sweat that had broken out on his brow. For some reason, at the moment the arrow left the string, the elderly teacher recalled the expression on the face of the visiting Oechalian, Eurytus, when the two of them had nearly collided at the entrance just before the contest. Right then that mad old woman, the dwarf-woman Galinthias, had slipped between them like a snake --- and it had seemed to Myrtilus that basileus Eurytus gave the beggarly old woman a dry nod before proceeding to the place of honor reserved for him.

Him, Myrtilus, the basileus had forgotten to nod to at all --- as though seeing him for the first time...

Teacher Myrtilus surveyed the raging stands with a hidden ache and thought about what the spectators would howl if they learned of the condition Eurytus had set --- and that he, Myrtilus, had accepted.

Eurytus the Oechalian had come to Myrtilus's house by night, and the condition had been a nighttime thing, a dark thing, the kind that could not be accepted, but could not be refused either...

Well, a teacher always sacrifices himself for his pupil --- though not in the way basileus Eurytus, a man with an archer's fingers and a serpent's eyes, had demanded.

Myrtilus could not see the boy for whom the loser of today's contest was secretly to sacrifice himself. Off somewhere in the crowd with his brother, no doubt... A hero. A future hero. The greatest hero of Hellas, with chapped bare feet.

Teacher Myrtilus wiped his bumpy brow with receding hair once more and forced himself to focus on the target.

* * *

"...Hey, Dummy, come over here! You can see better from here! We're right here, right under your nose! You're looking the wrong way!" both brothers were thoroughly enjoying themselves, capering about on a towering stone parapet they had somehow managed to climb.

Dummy --- a typical layabout gawker, a stale flatbread in one hand and a wineskin in the other --- turned his head this way and that, then spotted the boys (or pretended only just to have spotted them) and soon shoved his way through to the brothers, hopping up onto the parapet with ease.

Rather too much ease, perhaps... but who cared about that?

No one --- since new targets were already being set up on the field, considerably smaller than the previous ones; and they stood not fifty paces from the line where the archers waited, but half again as far.

Dummy threw an arm around the beaming twins' shoulders, nodded toward the archers, and clicked his tongue in admiration --- which Alcides and Iphicles wasted no time aping.

And once more the tall Oechalian youth loosed an arrow almost without aiming, striking the target unerringly, and once more Myrtilus took long, careful aim, aimed, loosed the string, and the happy spectators roared --- their own man, a fellow townsman, a Theban, was so far giving nothing away to the visiting celebrity. And if he aimed longer --- well, the rules said nothing against that!.. He could take a whole week to aim, for all it mattered.

"Hey, Dummy, who are you rooting for?" Iphicles, damp with excitement, tugged at his friend's chlamys. "I'm for Myrtilus!" he added proudly, without waiting for an answer, then broke off a piece of Dummy's flatbread without asking and began chewing it.

"And I'm for Iphitus!" Alcides broke in at once, bouncing on the spot in agitation and risking a fall from the parapet at any moment. "Myrtilus takes forever to aim, and that lanky fellow --- bang, and it's dead center!"

"Myrtilus is better anyway!" Iphicles couldn't hold back. "Because he's ours! Dummy, come on, Dummy, tell him --- who's better?! Who's going to win?!"

"May the strongest one win," Dummy answered smoothly.

"Of course, the strongest one," both boys pouted. "But who are you rooting for?!"

"I'm for Apollo!" Dummy smiled, though his eyes stayed serious, fixed on something off toward the edge of the field. "You cheer away to your hearts' content, lads, and I'll go say hello --- spotted a dear little relative of mine..."

There he was --- and then he wasn't. The twins still could not get used to this strange trait of Dummy's --- his knack for vanishing instantly and without a trace.

How astonished the brothers would have been (and not only they) to learn that their remarkable friend was quite nearby, close enough to touch --- only one could see him at all if one knew how to see truly, and that was a knack far from everyone possessed.

Among the assembled spectators, for instance, only the blind Tiresias could do it.

* * *

...Hermeias could not help smiling; he tousled the back of Alcides's shaggy head (the boy waved him off without looking --- a fly bothering him, perhaps?) --- and, unseen by mortal eyes, made his way toward the edge of the field.

To where one of the Family awaited him, from whose eyes one could hide only in Uncle Hades's helmet, that handiwork of the underground smiths, the Cyclopes.

God stood against god; "I" against "I."

The "I" of wayfarers, of thieves and traders, of orators and of souls trudging toward Acheron, of envoys, shepherds, and young athletes --- the Cyllenian, the Trickster, Dummy, Psychopompos or the Soul-Guide, the Keeper of the Flocks --- swift-footed Hermeias, son of Maia Pleiades, a mountain nymph, daughter of Atlas the Sky-Bearer; and the "I" of poets and archers, of builders and musicians --- the Far-Shooter, the Avenger, the Leader of the Muses, the Oracle, the Plague-Bringer, the Purifier --- sunlike Apollo Pythius, son of persecuted Leto, daughter of the titan Coeus and Phoebe.

Brothers by their father.

As it happened, the boy capering on the parapet, Alcides (or was it Iphicles?.. no, Alcides, most likely) also counted among the brothers of these two --- but gods and men alike are deaf to the laughter of Ananke the Inevitability.

"What brings you here, brother?" Hermeias called out while still some way off, arranging his face into the most carefree expression he could manage.

"A stunning beauty," he appraised Apollo, who stood with hands proudly on his hips. "And one who knows it about himself --- which makes him rather less stunning. I wonder, did he truly forgive me for that stolen herd, or did he simply decide not to bother with it?.."

"Just passing through," Apollo said, striking a bit of a pose, flashing a dazzling smile. "To take a look at the contest, and so on... You know how it is, dad's forbidden the whole Family to show up in Thebes in proper godly fashion, full regalia --- so I just dropped in quietly... like you, wanderer that you are."

"Mm-hm," Hermeias nodded. "That's the right way to do it. It fosters closeness..."

Closeness between whom exactly, and in what way --- that Hermeias did not say, and Apollo did not ask, and for a while the two of them watched in silence as the rivals tried to hit a tossed apple. Iphitus scored a clean hit once again, without aiming, splitting the apple into two nearly equal halves; Myrtilus very nearly missed his chance, loosing the string at the last instant, and only a rather small piece flew off his apple --- which counted all the same, however.

As Myrtilus lowered his bow after the shot, his hands trembled slightly.

"The Theban's nervous," Apollo remarked.

"He has good reason to be," Hermeias agreed. "Pupil of your pupil..."

"Not my pupil!" Apollo cut him off sharply, darkening. "My dear little pupil is way over there... grown up, hardened. Meets me --- doesn't even say hello, doesn't make an offering... and rarely enough as it is, come to that."

And he nodded carelessly toward the seats of honor, where the basileis Creon and Eurytus were conferring about something, with a pleased Amphitryon and a few more of the city's aristocracy sitting beside them.

"Mine or not mine," Hermeias could not resist a jab, "what's this, brother, talking just like a mortal? Where's your 'I'?"

"I'm no mortal," Apollo snapped. "And Eurytus is not me."

Hermeias noticed a strange shadow darken Apollo's sunlit face for that instant --- something between mild distaste and the interest a giant might take in a woodlouse that had smeared slime on the sole of his foot.

"He invoked my name a couple of times the other day," Apollo clearly meant that same Eurytus. "Thought I wouldn't hear, probably."

"That was foolish of him," the Trickster smirked.

"Foolish," the Sun-God agreed, patting the quiver that hung at his side, from which a muted radiance issued. "I don't like being invoked in vain. That little basileus ought to be taught a lesson --- a pupil, after all... a former one. Pity the Family wouldn't understand it, though --- Dad's ordered no omens, no storms over blasphemers, not in Thebes!"

"Then we won't!" Hermeias picked up eagerly. "What do we need a storm for? No use to us at all, thunder, downpours, lightning bolts and the like... but as for a small, if significant, spot of help to one of the rivals, and a small, if vexing, hindrance to the other --- Dad said nothing at all about that! Dad is great, he's got no time for trifles!.."

"My thought exactly," --- remarkable, what an unpleasant smirk could appear on a face as handsome as Apollo's. "The little basileus has gotten a bit too high an opinion of himself... We won't give the Theban the victory, mind you, he hasn't earned it, but even a draw will stick in Eurytus's throat like a bone. Shall we do it, Trickster?"

"We'll do it, Archer. Only like this: Iphitus is mine, Myrtilus is yours. Dirty tricks are more my department... 'Phoebus Musagetes, silver-bowed Apollo, weaving vile schemes against Eurytus's offspring Iphitus' --- no, that doesn't have the right ring! But this now: 'And to Apollo the glorious archer of Thebes called out; and Phoebus heard him' --- now that's an entirely different matter!"

"You're a chatterbox, Dummy," Apollo waved a hand. "Now who could you have taken after?"

"After myself," Hermeias answered carelessly, watching as two doves, flapping loudly, soared up into the cloudless sky --- and two arrows shot after the birds almost at the same instant. One dove dropped like a stone --- the longer arrow had neatly sliced off its head; but the other, thrashing convulsively, fell into the thick of the delighted spectators.

Someone's hands flung the bird back onto the field; the dove's wings scraped the ground once more, and then it went still --- Myrtilus's arrow had pierced it clean through, but the dove had proved unusually tenacious for its kind.

The judges on the field exchanged glances, and one of them set about hanging two gilded cups from a wooden post on long, sturdy threads. Even the most skillful archers usually faltered at this task --- the cup would be set swinging, and the archer had to sever the thread no more than a cubit from the cup.

Arrows with forked heads were handed out.

Two slaves set the cups swinging at the same moment and leapt apart in opposite directions.

Whether a stray gleam of sunlight caught Iphitus in the eye, or his foot slipped at the wrong moment on grass that had just been dry and had suddenly turned wet and slick (now why would that be?!); or perhaps it was simply the mounting strain of the day making itself felt --- in short, his hand, which had never once known a miss, faltered, the bow wavered slightly, the string rang out in protest... and the arrow did sever the thread, but too far from the cup, which fell into the grass.

A cubit? More than that?..

And in that same instant Myrtilus the Theban's arrow leapt forward --- while in the elderly archer's eyes little sparks of astonishment still burned, still would not fade, as though it had not been he who had just drawn the bow, taken aim, loosed the string that gave its short, sharp cry...

Myrtilus gave a shiver, hastily invoked Apollo the Far-Shooter, and glanced back over his shoulder --- as though expecting to see someone there.

The stands were in an uproar, watching the cups that had fallen into the dusty grass, watching the slaves scoop them up and dash headlong toward Creon, watching the basileus of Thebes fussily measuring the broken threads, which proved equal in length...

Beside Creon, Eurytus of Oechalia sat silent and scowling. He knew, he could feel it --- he had just been put in his place.

At the very exit, the old dwarf-woman Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, stood silent.

A ringing silence replaced the crowd's roar.

Waiting.

"A draw!" Creon proclaimed solemnly.

And once more --- the roar of the many-voiced beast called Crowd, a monster subject neither to god nor to hero.

Except, perhaps, for a while.

...Eurytus was silent.

...Galinthias was silent.

...Young Iphitus was silent, glancing timidly at his stern father.

And Myrtilus from Thebes, the teacher of the Amphitryad brothers, was silent too.

The archer knew: today he had lost.
