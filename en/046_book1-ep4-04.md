#### 4

Having seen Alkmene all the way to the house---he had never found the will to catch up with her or call out---Amphitryon pulled the gate shut behind his wife and stayed out in the street himself, sitting down on the bare ground beside the gate, his back against the rough stone of the wall.

He thought, in passing, that he must look now like a beggar waiting for alms.

To Amphitryon's left came the sound of running feet, but to turn his head and look at whoever was tearing through the streets of Thebes at this ungodly hour was beyond him. Amphitryon only noted, in some remote corner of his mind, that the man ran badly---stumbling, wheezing, forcing air through inflamed lungs with a whistling rasp; and then the footsteps died away... but the wheezing stayed.

"Quick! Quick, lawagetas! Arm the household! They'll be here any moment! Come on, quick!..."

"Don't shout," Amphitryon asked indifferently without moving. "You'll wake the whole house."

"What's wrong with you, lawagetas?! Get up!"

"I haven't been lawagetas in a long time. Forget it."

"To me you're lawagetas... get up, you dead hog!"

Amphitryon didn't even notice himself being jerked to his feet. The man flopped in his hands like a fish thrown up on the shore, baring his teeth in a strange, joyful grimace that made his face resemble... resemble... a caved-in bridge of the nose, hair grey and coarse as pine needles, tiny, close-set eyes, a bristling great beard...

"Nasal, is that you?!" Amphitryon unclenched his fingers.

"Praise Zeus!" Telemos the Nasal wheezed happily, rubbing his neck, where Amphitryon's grip had left red marks, like a burned-in brand. "Praise Zeus! I thought that was it---lawagetas once, and gone up in smoke! But no, still crackling... and what fingers you have, my lord, I must say, those aren't fingers at all, those are the tongs of Hephaistos!..."

"Get to the point!" Amphitryon barked, forgetting that only a moment ago his own inaction had driven Nasal to rudeness.

"I wanted to stop them, lawagetas---but how do you stop them?! They trampled Philides flat, just like that, and they'd have crushed me too---so I made a run for it... straight to you! And they're coming, lawagetas, they're coming, they'll be here soon---I'm not what I was, I'm old now, I can't run fast! There's a stitch in my side..."

"Who's 'they'?!"

"People! People, lawagetas! Thebans. They're saying---Iphikles must be killed... offered up as a sacrifice! Or else misfortune will fall on Thebes, the gods will turn away!... Old Galinthias whispered that a sign had come to her: Iphikles was the one who finished off Linos, and Alkeides is unblemished, Alkeides is pure, a hero equal to the gods! I tried to shut her up---but she twisted free, and the people came at me and Philides... ah, Philides, Philides, why did you have to go and... I couldn't keep you safe..."

From two sides at once the crowd began pouring into the street. The people came surprisingly quietly, without shouting, without noise---they came frighteningly, calmly, purposefully, knowing why they walked, and what they were walking into. Like a river in flood, they washed over the empty space, foaming with scattered cries, rolling forward wave after wave; many carried sticks and stones in their hands. It seemed this movement could not be stopped, that any moment now the tiny island by the house gate, where Amphitryon and Telemos stood frozen, would also be swallowed by the human whirlpool---and then everything would happen of itself, everything that had to happen; everything that, until this very minute, had still lain in the lap of the gods.

For now, though, the little island held---the gate, the fence, and a scrap of ground around Amphitryon and Nasal, who kept glancing warily from side to side.

The river, spilling out of its banks in the middle of Thebes at this unseasonable hour, splashed with a burst of shouts, and the first wave touched the unyielding shore, part of it rolling back, part of it staying on the island's edge, within a spear-thrust of the two no-longer-young men blocking the gate.

"So, Perseid?" grunted a squat, broad-shouldered craftsman, scratching a bare and surprisingly hairless chest. "Fathered a bastard? Ha, your whole line... all the same---your grandfather, you, your little son! Strangers or your own, doesn't matter---kill and never notice! And you, Nasal, you'll get yours too, mark my words..."

Behind the craftsman some seven men shifted their feet---whispering among themselves, pointing fingers on the sly; two kept touching the hilts of knives at their belts, and the one at the far left held a spear out before him, its old blade notched and dull.

"The gods are angry." The craftsman was no talker and picked his words with difficulty, plainly astonished at Amphitryon's slowness to understand. "Angry, the gods are... why'd Iphikles have to go and kill poor Linos? Hand over the whelp, Perseid---and we'll leave, I swear it by whoever you like, we'll leave... isn't Alkeides enough for you? A future hero, growing up in your family too, so he's as good as yours... So will you hand over Iphikles yourself, or what?"

Behind Amphitryon, the gate creaked open.

"I locked them in the house," Alkmene said to her husband as though there were no one else in the street but the two of them. "They were trying to force their way out here... I did right, didn't I, Amphitryon?"

"You did right," he answered, without turning. "Right. Now go back in. And close the gate. Bar it."

"I'm not going anywhere." Alkmene rubbed her cheek lightly against her husband's shoulder, and the touch seared Amphitryon like red-hot iron, and the craftsman, meeting his glare, flinched and stepped back. "I'm not going anywhere. I bore them both---so let them take me first... The slaves are afraid, they're waiting to see---but I'm not afraid of anything anymore. Let them take me for the sacrifice. Forgive me, Amphitryon, I wasn't listening at the door, but Telemos was shouting so loud..."

"Telemos, take her away!" Amphitryon ordered. "By force if you have to---just take her! Now!"

"Come, my lady, come now..." Nasal mumbled dully, and, suddenly scooping Alkmene up in his arms, vanished with her behind the gate.

The bar clanged shut.

"Go," Amphitryon said quietly, addressing the crowd, which for him just now was embodied entirely in this craftsman standing opposite, blinking dully with his colorless lashes; a man who had come for his, Amphitryon's, son.

For one of his sons.

"A sacrifice!" a familiar shriek rang out over the crowd, but there was no time to recall whose it was. "Sacrifice Iphikles, killer of innocent Linos, the brother of Orpheus! Let there be raised to Alkeides an altar, and there let the impious villain fall to the double-edged bronze as an offering made to the hero Alkeides, the offspring of Zeus! A sacrifice!..."

And the island around Amphitryon shrank by half.

Something was happening behind him, past the gate, in the house or in the yard, something was stirring there, compelling him to turn, to look, to find out---but he could not turn, because any careless movement could be read by capricious fate as weakness, as uncertainty, as possible consent; if anything still held the crowd back, it was this forty-five-year-old man at the gate, this greying man with the bronze gaze, whom hardly anyone remembered anymore as Amphitryon the Exile, or as the lawagetas Amphitryon who had crushed the Taphian pirates. Ten-odd years of a quiet, unremarkable life---time enough, and more, to be forgotten; to most he was simply the husband of Alkmene, who had borne Zeus a future hero.

He could not turn.

The crowd will soon realize that killing him, Amphitryon, is far simpler than hesitating, than making up their minds; but every moment won is a moment of life for the living.

Perhaps he can yet live to see the dawn.

The craftsman shifted his weight, then, for no reason at all, stared over Amphitryon's head, as if Hermes the Kyllenian himself had appeared on the fence in his winged sandals.

The craftsman's cronies raised their heads too, breathing hard with excitement.

"Well, would you look at that!" burst involuntarily from the one with the spear. "Would you look... which one's for the altar, then?"

[Mustn't turn around,]{.thoughts} Amphitryon repeated to himself like an incantation, [mustn't... mustn't!]{.thoughts}

Bare heels struck the ground to his left and right; and something heavy landed beside him, its blunt, hard edge catching him painfully on the ankle.

"Take it, father!" Something familiar was pressed into his right palm, and Amphitryon did not at once understand that it was the hilt of a sword.

His palm understood it far sooner, closing around the weapon.

"Hold on, father!" A shield strap looped around his left forearm, and the familiar weight forced his arm to bend and thrust forward.

The craftsman backed away, nearly knocking his slack-jawed friend off his feet; a look of bewilderment came into his eyes, the look of a man realizing that it was his own belly the sword would enter first, and not knowing what to do now---run or attack?

"Your mother locked you in, you scamps!" Amphitryon snapped, positioning himself so the rim of his shield covered Alkeides, who was straining to push forward.

"Hah!" the twins retorted scornfully, leaning on their javelins. "Call that a door! We'll fix it later..."

A stone thrown from the back ranks of the crowd flew past Iphikles's ear and struck the fence. A woman's scream---"What are you doing?! We don't even know which one---"---was choked off, drowned in the hostile roar, and that same familiar shriek soared up over the crowd like a startled bird: "Strike! Strike, Thebans! Zeus will know which one---"; a second stone rang menacingly off the bronze boss of the shield and dropped at Alkeides's feet. The crowd stirred into motion: many of the women, the old men, and the simply cowardly tried to squeeze their way somewhere farther from the thick of it, unwilling to turn from onlookers into participants in a possible massacre; the most zealous shouted and waved whatever they held, but did not yet press forward---waiting for first blood.

"Hyah!" The craftsman, coming to his senses, snatched the spear from his friend and swung it back to throw.

Amphitryon crouched slightly, turning his shield to track the clumsy swing, praying to the gods only that his sons would not move from where they stood, that foolish, hot-blooded youth would not throw them onto that old notched spear; he held the arm with the sword far out to the right, trying to block Iphikles's path---and a wild, absurd thought flashed like lightning at the very edge of his mind, a thought of what Kastor would have done to a careless pupil for holding his arm out like that...

An arrow whistled sharply overhead---it seemed to Amphitryon that its shaft all but ruffled his hair---and buried itself halfway up the shaft in the craftsman's bulging eye, its narrow, predatory point jutting from the back of his skull.

The force of the blow spun the man---already dead, but not yet fallen---sideways, and Amphitryon had a clear view of that long arrow, running slantwise through the head.

A second arrow struck, its fletching trembling, beneath the collarbone of one of the seven lovers of sacrifices, tearing a cry from him and the knife from his hand; the rest at once made to flee, and a third arrow thudded with a crunch into someone's buttock, drawing a wild howl from the victim and a storm of laughter from the twins.

Adding to the panic, from somewhere off to the side came a piercing war cry, accompanied by a bass roar---unlike Kastor, Autolykos had never liked, nor really known how, to shout anything coherent before a brawl---and the Thebans standing at that end of the street bolted headlong, heedless of where they went...

The river of the crowd ebbed back from the gate, hastily draining away toward the far end of the street.

Only then did Amphitryon turn around.

On the roof of his house stood a lanky archer, waving his hand at Amphitryon and the children.

Behind Iphitos's back, the sun was rising.

"...And why didn't you come round from the other side, eh?!" Kastor Dioskouros was reproaching a contentedly grinning Autolykos, some time later. "I told you to, and you dug in your heels like a mule..."

"You did, you did," Autolykos agreed, gathering the fully armed Lakonian into a bear hug and lifting him clear off the ground. "But where would they have run to then, you thickhead, if we'd come at them from both sides at once, with Iphitos settled up on the roof with two quivers? Where, I ask you?!"

"That's exactly what I'm saying, that they'd have had nowhere to run!" Bloodthirsty Kastor shook his head. "You never do listen to me..."

And gloomy Iphitos of Oichalia went on examining his broken arrow and saying nothing.
