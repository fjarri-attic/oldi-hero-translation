#### 4

Having seen Alcmene all the way to the house --- he had never found the will to catch up with her or call out --- Amphitryon pulled the gate shut behind his wife and stayed out in the street himself, sitting straight down on the ground beside the gate, his back against the rough stone of the wall.

He thought, in passing, that he must look now like a beggar waiting for alms.

To Amphitryon's left came the sound of running feet, but to turn his head and look at whoever was tearing through the streets of Thebes at this ungodly hour was beyond him. Amphitryon only noted, in some remote corner of his mind, that the man ran badly --- stumbling, wheezing, driving air through inflamed lungs with a whistle; and then the footsteps died away... but the wheezing stayed.

"Quick! Quick, lawagetas! Arm the household! They'll be here any moment! Quick, I say!.."

"Don't shout," Amphitryon asked, indifferent, without moving. "You'll wake the whole house."

"What's wrong with you, lawagetas?! Get up!"

"I haven't been lawagetas in a long time. Forget it."

"To me you're lawagetas... get up, you pig carrion!"

Amphitryon didn't even notice himself being jerked to his feet. The man flopped in his hands like a fish thrown up on the shore, baring his teeth in a strange, joyful grimace that made his face resemble... resemble... a caved-in bridge of the nose, hair grey and coarse as pine needles, tiny, close-set eyes, a bristling great beard...

"Nasal, is that you?!" Amphitryon unclenched his fingers.

"Praise Zeus!" Thelem the Nasal wheezed happily, rubbing his neck, where red marks --- like a burned-in brand --- were left by Amphitryon's grip. "Praise Zeus! I thought that was it --- lawagetas once, and gone up in smoke! But no, still crackling... and what fingers you have, my lord, I must say, those aren't fingers at all, those are the tongs of Hephaestus!.."

"Get to the point!" Amphitryon barked, forgetting that his own idleness a moment ago had been what pushed Nasal into rudeness.

"I wanted to stop them, lawagetas --- but how do you stop them?! They trampled Philid flat, just like that, and they'd have crushed me too --- so I made a run for it... straight to you! And they're coming, lawagetas, they're coming, they'll be here soon --- I'm not what I was, I'm old now, I can't run fast! There's a stitch in my side..."

"Who --- they?!"

"People! People, lawagetas! Thebans. They're saying --- Iphicles must be killed... offered up as a sacrifice! Or else misfortune will fall on Thebes, the gods will turn away!.. Old Galinthias whispered that a sign had come to her: Iphicles was the one who finished Linus, and Alcides is unblemished, Alcides is pure, a hero equal to the gods! I tried to stop her mouth --- but she twisted free, and the people came at me and Philid... ah, Philid, Philid, why did you have to go and... I couldn't keep you safe..."

* * *

From two sides at once the crowd began pouring into the street. The people walked strangely quietly, without shouting, without noise --- they walked in a way that was frightening, calm, purposeful, knowing why they walked, and what they were walking into. Like a river in flood, they washed over the empty space, foaming with scattered cries, rolling forward wave after wave; many carried sticks and stones in their hands. It seemed this movement could not be stopped, that any moment now the tiny island by the house gate, where Amphitryon and Thelem stood frozen, would also be swallowed by the human whirlpool --- and then everything would happen of itself, everything that had to happen; everything that, until this very minute, had still lain in the lap of the gods.

For now, though, the little island held --- the gate, the fence, and a scrap of ground around Amphitryon and Nasal, who kept glancing warily from side to side.

The river, spilling out of its banks in the middle of Thebes at this unseasonable hour, splashed with a burst of shouts, and the first wave touched the unyielding shore, part of it rolling back, part of it staying on the island's edge, a spear's throw from the two no-longer-young men blocking the gate.

"So, Perseid?" grunted a squat, broad-shouldered craftsman, scratching a bare and surprisingly hairless chest. "Fathered a bastard? Ha, your whole line... all the same --- your grandfather, you, your little son! Strangers or your own, doesn't matter --- kill and never notice! And you, Nasal, you'll get yours too, mark my words..."

Behind the craftsman some seven men shifted their feet --- whispering among themselves, pointing fingers on the sly; two kept touching the hilts of knives at their belts, and the one at the far left held a spear out before him, its old blade notched and dull.

"The gods are angry," the craftsman was no talker and picked his words with difficulty, plainly astonished at Amphitryon's slowness to understand. "Angry, the gods are... why'd Iphicles have to go and kill poor Linus? Hand over the whelp, Perseid --- and we'll leave, I swear it by whoever you like, we'll leave... isn't Alcides enough for you? A future hero, growing up in your family too, so he's as good as yours... So will you hand over Iphicles yourself, or what?"

Behind Amphitryon, the gate creaked open.

"I locked them in the house," Alcmene said to her husband as though there were no one else in the street but the two of them. "They were trying to force their way out here... I did right, didn't I, Amphitryon?"

"You did right," he answered, without turning. "Right. Now go back in. And close the gate. Bar it."

"I'm not going anywhere," Alcmene rubbed her cheek lightly against her husband's shoulder, and the touch seared Amphitryon like red-hot iron, and the craftsman, catching his eye, flinched and stepped back. "I'm not going anywhere. I bore them both --- so let them take me first... The slaves are afraid, they're waiting to see --- but I'm not afraid of anything anymore. Let them take me for the sacrifice. Forgive me, Amphitryon, I wasn't listening at the door, but Thelem was shouting so loud..."

"Thelem, take her away!" Amphitryon ordered. "By force if you have to --- just take her! Now!"

"Come, my lady, come now..." Nasal mumbled dully, and, suddenly scooping Alcmene up in his arms, vanished with her behind the gate.

The bar clanged shut.

"Go," Amphitryon said quietly, addressing the crowd, which for him just now was embodied entirely in this craftsman standing opposite, blinking dully with his colorless lashes; a man who had come for his, Amphitryon's, son.

For one of his sons.

"A sacrifice!" a familiar shriek rang out over the crowd, but there was no time to recall whose it was. "Sacrifice Iphicles, killer of guiltless Linus, brother to Orpheus! Let an altar rise to Alcides --- and on it let the impious wretch be given to the double-edged bronze, an offering to Alcides the hero, own get of Zeus! A sacrifice!.."

And the island around Amphitryon shrank by half.

Something was happening behind him, past the gate, in the house or in the yard, something was stirring there, compelling him to turn, to look, to find out --- but he could not turn, because any careless movement could be read by capricious fate as weakness, as uncertainty, as possible consent; if anything still held the crowd back, it was this forty-five-year-old man at the gate, this greying man with the bronze gaze, whom hardly anyone remembered anymore as Amphitryon the Exile, or as the lawagetas Amphitryon who had crushed the Taphian pirates. Ten-odd years of a quiet, unremarkable life --- time enough, and more, to be forgotten; to most he was simply Alcmene's husband, the woman who had borne Zeus a future hero.

He could not turn.

The crowd would soon realize that killing him, Amphitryon, was far simpler than hesitating, than making up their minds; but every moment won was a moment of life for the living.

Perhaps he might yet live to see the dawn.

The craftsman shifted his weight, then, for no reason at all, stared over Amphitryon's head, as if Hermeias Cyllenios himself had appeared on the fence in his winged sandals.

The craftsman's cronies raised their heads too, snorting with excitement.

"Well, would you look at that!" burst involuntarily from the one with the spear. "Would you look... which one's for the altar, then?"

"Mustn't turn around," Amphitryon repeated to himself like an incantation, "mustn't... mustn't!"

Bare heels struck the ground to his left and right; and something heavy landed beside him, its blunt, hard edge catching him painfully on the ankle.

"Take it, father!" something familiar was pressed into his right palm, and Amphitryon did not at once understand that it was the hilt of a sword.

His palm understood it far sooner, closing around the weapon.

"Hold on, father!" a shield strap looped around his left forearm, and the familiar weight forced his arm to bend and thrust forward.

The craftsman backed away, nearly knocking his slack-jawed friend off his feet; a look of bewilderment came into his eyes, the look of a man realizing that it was his own belly the sword would enter first, and not knowing what to do now --- run or attack?

"Your mother locked you in, you scamps!" Amphitryon snapped, positioning himself so the rim of his shield covered Alcides, who was straining to push forward.

"As if!" the twins retorted scornfully, leaning on their javelins. "Call that a door! We'll fix it later..."

A stone thrown from the back ranks of the crowd flew past Iphicles's ear and struck the fence. A woman's scream --- "What are you doing?! We don't even know which one---" --- was choked off, drowned in the hostile roar, and that same familiar shriek soared up over the crowd like a startled bird: "Strike! Strike, Thebans! Zeus will know which one---"; a second stone rang menacingly off the bronze boss of the shield and dropped at Alcides's feet. The crowd stirred into motion: many of the women, the old men, and the simply cowardly tried to push their way off somewhere farther from the center of events, unwilling to turn from onlookers into participants in a possible massacre; the most zealous shouted and waved whatever they held, but did not yet press forward --- waiting for first blood.

"Hah!" the craftsman, coming back to himself, snatched the spear from his friend and swung it back to throw.

Amphitryon crouched slightly, turning his shield to track the clumsy swing, praying to the gods only that his sons would not move from where they stood, that foolish, hot-blooded youth would not throw them onto that old notched spear; he held the arm with the sword far out to the right, trying to block Iphicles's path --- and a wild, absurd thought flashed like lightning at the very edge of his mind, a thought of what Castor would have done to a careless pupil for holding his arm out like that...

An arrow whistled sharply overhead --- it seemed to Amphitryon that its shaft all but ruffled his hair --- and buried itself to the middle in the craftsman's bulging eye, its narrow, predatory point jutting from the back of his skull.

The force of the blow spun the man --- already dead, but not yet fallen --- sideways, and Amphitryon had a clear view of that long arrow, slashed crosswise through the head.

A second arrow struck, its fletching trembling, beneath the collarbone of one of the seven lovers of sacrifice, tearing a cry from him and the knife from his hand; the rest at once made to flee, and a third arrow thudded with a crunch into someone's buttock, drawing a wild howl from the victim and a storm of laughter from the twins.

Adding to the panic, from somewhere off to the side came a piercing war cry, accompanied by a bass roar --- unlike Castor, Autolycus had never cared for, nor learned, sensible shouting before a brawl --- and the Thebans standing at that end of the street bolted headlong, heedless of where they went...

The river of the crowd ebbed back from the gate, hastily draining away toward the far end of the street.

Only then did Amphitryon turn around.

On the roof of his house stood a lanky archer, waving his hand at Amphitryon and the children.

Behind Iphitus's back, the sun was rising.

* * *

"...And why didn't you come round from the other side, eh?!" Castor Dioscurus was reproaching a rather smugly grinning Autolycus, some time later. "I told you so, and you dug in your heels like a mule..."

"You did, you did," Autolycus agreed, gathering the fully armed Laconian into a bear hug and lifting him clear off the ground. "But where would they have run to then, you thick skull, if we'd come at them from both sides at once, with Iphitus settled up on the roof with two quivers? Where, I ask you?!"

"That's exactly what I'm saying, that they'd have had nowhere to run!" Castor shook his head, bloodthirsty. "You never do listen to me..."

And gloomy Iphitus of Oechalia went on examining his broken arrow and saying nothing.
