#### 13

"A brother, I want a brother!" Hermeias squealed, plainly becoming his old self again---a frivolous trickster, a deceptively frivolous god, an unpredictable, noisy schemer. "A twin brother! To hold me back from unreasonable acts! Like Iphikles holds Alkeides back during his fits!"

"Why didn't he hold his brother back today, then?" asked Cheiron, clearly not expecting an answer.

But an answer came.

"Because that snake Dummy wanted to kill us!" a sleepy child's voice rang out from the far corner of the cave. "That's why! There! Get out of here, Dummy! Didn't you get enough of the rock back then?! Want more? Take that!..."

And a flushed Iphikles hurled at his enemy the first thing that came to hand---an apple.

The boy's aim was good enough, but the juicy golden fruit, for some unfathomable reason, hung frozen in the air, a cubit short of the head of Dummy, who was bent over the tub.

Hermeias turned lazily, reached out his hand, plucked the apple straight out of the air, and bit off nearly half of it with a crunch.

"How'd you do that?" Iphikles asked, puzzled, forgetting his grievance for a moment.

"Like this!" The Trickster smirked and crunched up the rest of the apple.

"Well, there he sits, stuffing his face with apples." Alkeides, also awake now, elbowed his brother in the side. "Like it's nothing. What'd you want to kill us for, you glutton?!"

"Yes, what for?" Iphikles joined in, rubbing his eyes.

"And why did you go for me first?" Hermeias answered, matching the twins' tone, and for clarity jabbed a finger at Alkeides.

"Me?!" Alkeides was genuinely astonished. "Why are you lying? You started it..."

"No, you really did attack him." Honest Iphikles did not back his brother up. "You had a fit. You headbutted him right in the stomach! Dummy doubled right over..."

"Don't remember," Alkeides grumbled sullenly. "Pity!"

"Fine, Alkeides went first." Iphikles waved his hand impatiently. "But you gave it right back to him too! So why go and kill anybody for it?! And me---why did you want to kill me?"

"You?" Hermeias was surprised now, too, and sat down on the edge of the tub. "I wouldn't have laid a finger on you! Alkeides---yes... well, I lost my temper---it hurt too much! I got angry..."

"Yes, you wanted to kill Alkeides." Iphikles, funny and disheveled, was thinking hard about something, but the thought kept slipping away, and the boy struggled for words. "Of course, Alkeides... and then you started killing Alkeides---and me! That is..."

He got thoroughly tangled up, fell silent---and then suddenly his face lit up. It seemed Iphikles had finally managed to catch the thought he needed and put it into more or less coherent words.

"That is, it made no difference to me which of us you'd kill!" he blurted out. "Alkeides or me---either way it's *me*! And I very much wanted to kill you first... well, I wanted to for both of us, for myself and for Alkeides!"

Iphikles beamed, remembering how he alone had wanted to kill the villain Dummy for the two of them.

"And didn't I crack you good with that rock!" he finished, more peaceably now.

Cheiron, unseen by Hermeias, gave Iphikles a thumbs-up---as if to say: very good indeed!

"Shame I didn't see it," Alkeides sniffled, gradually calming down too.

"But you---you broke off a club like that!" Hermeias hurried to console him. "If you'd landed that on me, I'd have dropped straight into Hades!"

"What club?" Alkeides perked up with interest.

"About like that one, there." Hermeias leaned out of the cave, looked around, and, beckoning Alkeides over, pointed him toward a clump of hazel growing nearby. "See the outermost trunk?"

Alkeides looked doubtfully at the hazel, then at Dummy---plainly suspecting the other of lying---and turned his gaze back to the trunk in question.

"You broke it," Iphikles confirmed. "I saw it myself."

"Don't remember," Alkeides muttered once again, clambered out of the cave, and tried to break a trunk, bracing his knee against it and yanking at it with both hands as hard as he could.

Naturally, the tree had no intention of breaking.

"You're lying," Alkeides declared confidently. "All of you are lying: Dummy, and you, Iphikles, and this... horse-uncle."

Then he looked around in surprise and, just like that, added:

"Where are we, anyway? Uh, I meant to ask earlier... and fell asleep."

"At my place. On Pelion," said Cheiron, who had been silent until then.

"On Pelion?" The boy's eyes went round. "So then you're... the wise centaur Cheiron?! We rode on your back..."

"Can't speak to the 'wise' part"---the centaur smiled---"but Cheiron is indeed my name."

"But... how did we get here? It's a long way from Thebes..."

"Dummy dragged us here," Iphikles grumbled.

"I don't remember any of it!" Alkeides was genuinely upset. "How did he drag us here, anyway? By the scruff of the neck? Did we walk for a month?!"

"Not a month," Iphikles sighed. "'Hold on to my belt,' he says... we took three steps---and here we were."

"No way! Dummy, how'd you manage that? And that thing with the apple---"

"Do you two even know who your friend here really is?" Cheiron cut in, putting on an air of mock sternness.

"We know!" the twins answered in chorus. "Dummy! A liar and..."

"And a traitor," Iphikles couldn't help adding, though there was no longer any of his old anger in his voice.

"That's right. And he's also a god," the centaur said quietly, but in a way that made everyone go still and fall silent at once. "The god Hermes, one of the Family... er... one of the Olympians. And by the way, boys, he's your kinsman. Same as me. A distant one, mind you."

"There have never been any Dummies in our family!" Alkeides shouted hotly, not yet having fully taken in what Cheiron had told them.

"No horses, either," Iphikles added quietly.

"And who was your great-grandfather, heroes?"

"Perseus!" the brothers answered proudly, as one.

"And who was Perseus's father?"

"Zeus the Thunderer," the twins said, a little more quietly. "Kronid."

"Well, I'm a Kronid too! And Zeus is my half-brother, and, incidentally, the father of this one here..." Cheiron pointed at Hermeias, who had straightened up importantly ("This one?!" Iphikles gasped, disbelieving, while Alkeides simply stuck his tongue out at Dummy). "So regardless of whom you consider your father, heroes---"

"Our dad is Amphitryon!"

"Fine, Amphitryon, I'm not arguing." The centaur gave a barely perceptible smirk. "But either way, we're kin. Whether you like it or not."

The twins exchanged glances.

"Well, you---you're all right, I suppose." Alkeides graciously permitted Cheiron to count as their kinsman. "But this one here..."

"No kin of ours picks on the little ones!" Iphikles finished for his brother, with finality. "And anyway---what kind of Hermes is he?! A slob and a swindler! And that... what's it called? Well, that..."

Hermeias nearly jumped when Iphikles finally recalled the word he needed---one he'd once overheard from a drunken Autolykos and later repeated in front of his mother, for which he'd been soundly whipped by Amphitryon.

"Trickster, show them." Cheiron, barely holding back laughter, called Hermeias the Trickster for the first time.

And the twins, staring wide-eyed at Dummy, saw.

They saw the dirty stains vanish of their own accord from the once-elegant chiton of Hermeias the Dummy, saw the crumpled fabric dry out and smooth itself, quickly regaining its pristine look; and now not even the most scrupulous witness would have found a single hole in it.

They saw the ever-present, hard-wearing Talaria[\*](#fn-30){.noteref} strap themselves onto Dummy's feet of their own accord, their little wings trembling and blurring together into hazy half-circles; saw their friend-and-enemy, laughing, rise a cubit above the floor and, flinging his arms wide, sail out of the cave. Outside, Hermeias traced a few intricate figures through the air, and when he came down, two snakes hissed in the Trickster's hand, coiling around the caduceus rod that had appeared from nowhere. It was the well-known attribute of the god Hermes, patron of athletes, travelers, merchants, and thieves.

Hermeias absently flicked the snakes on the nose---and they vanished along with the rod.

"Enough?" the Trickster inquired, winking at the centaur. "Or should I fly some more?"

"And here we've been bringing offerings to you," Iphikles said, taking offense for no clear reason.

"And he picks on the little ones!" Alkeides added.

Then some thought crossed his mind, and the boy's eyes took on a mischievous gleam.

"Once we get home, I'm going to smash your altar!" he promised Hermeias, who was quite taken aback. "Or better yet..."

"Better yet, we'll dump manure on it!" Iphikles chimed in. "And set it on fire... Let him eat that!"

A great deal more might have been promised to Hermeias just then---but the twins were cut short by Cheiron's laughter, which had finally broken loose, irrepressible and not unlike a horse's whinny. The centaur roared with laughter, his whole mighty body shaking, clutching his belly (his human one, since his hands couldn't reach the horse's).

The twins fell silent mid-word, staring in bewilderment at the laughing Cheiron; Hermeias couldn't hold out either and snorted with laughter; before a minute had passed, the brothers had joined him too---and soon the astonished nymphs and satyrs of Pelion could behold all four of them, quite literally doubled over with laughter at the mouth of Cheiron's cave: the wise centaur, the youthful god, and two mortal boys---the future heroes of Hellas.
