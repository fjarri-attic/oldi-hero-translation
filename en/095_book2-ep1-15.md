#### 15

He drove the chariot north.

The wheels clatter.

Delphi, soon.

Soon.

He drove the chariot, spurring on the snorting horses, while behind him rumor flew in a thousand-winged, clamorous flock.

"He killed his teacher and friend?!" the Messenians were horrified.

"No doubt he didn't want to give back the herds he'd stolen from Eurytus!" the Eleians reckoned.

"What herds?!" the Argives were indignant. "What are you talking about?! This is the great Heracles, Slayer of Monsters! He was wronged unjustly on Euboea!"

"Monsters are monsters," the stubborn Eleians wouldn't back down, keeping a closer eye on their own herds, "a wrong is a wrong, but a herd, if you'll excuse me, is a herd! One doesn't cancel out the other. No doubt he lured poor Iphitus onto the wall --- take a look, he says, aren't those your horses grazing down there? --- and then shoved him off! Easy as that!"

"Jealous Hera, what do you punish him for?" whispered Arcadian and Laconian girls, dreaming of Heracles on hot nights.

"A madman," they shrugged in Achaea.

"A hero!" responded in Boeotia.

"The greatest..." Both.

Strong-walled Troy chuckled to itself, loud enough for the whole Aegean Sea to hear.

Oechalia and Pylos kept silent.

Except, no --- Pylos was no longer silent. And Wanax Neleus of Pylos was lying left and right, claiming that, on his way home from Euboea, he had met Heracles, who had supposedly begged him, pious Neleus, to cleanse the involuntary killer of miasma --- but Neleus, a veritable wellspring of piety and caution, had refused Heracles the cleansing, citing his long friendship with Eurytus, the dead man's father.

What's that you say?

Ah yes, of course --- with the late father of the dead man... now it's clear why salt shipped to the mainland from the salt-works of Euboea has grown so dear!..

And at the head of a hundred-mouthed horde, Heracles burst into sacred Delphi.

"Wash your hands in the Castalian Spring!" the priests said sternly, blocking the hero's path as he strode along the paved road past the rocky eastern wall. "And offer praise to radiant Apollo!"

"The nymph Castalia turned into the Castalian Spring, fleeing your god's advances," was the answer. "I will not wash my hands in the tears of the hapless one! Out of my way!"

"Put on a laurel wreath!" the priestesses ordered sternly, standing before Heracles at the temple entrance.

"The nymph Daphne became a laurel rather than yield to lustful Phoebus," was the answer. "I will not wear a wreath made from the hapless one's hair! Stand aside!"

"There is no cleansing for you!" the enraged Pythia proclaimed, and the mist over the rocky chasm trembled ominously. "There is none, and there will be none!"

"Apollo killed young Hyacinthus, son of basileus Amyclas," was the answer. "Who cleansed your god of miasma, woman?!"

"In this temple, madman, no one will prophesy for you!"

"I am my own temple and my own oracle," was the answer. "Leave, woman, and do not stand between me and the god!"

And Heracles blasphemously seized the golden tripod on which the Pythia sat while prophesying.

"Apollo! Where are you, Olympian?!" --- this roar would haunt the Pythia, who fled in terror from the innermost sanctuary of the temple, for a long time to come. "Show yourself, and answer Heracles!"

The answer was a fiery arrow, sent from the other side of the chasm.

The tripod traced a glittering arc, earthly gold met heavenly gold, flame met flame, and --- nothing but sparks scattering through the temple.

* * *

...No one will ever know the truth of how mad Heracles and furious Apollo grappled with one another; mortal and immortal. Only this will be whispered in Delphi, repeated from Epirus to Attica, and echoed across the Peloponnese: that strength met strength, forcing Zeus the Cloud-Gatherer to hurl his thunderbolt to part the wrestlers and keep his son from perishing... but which of his sons --- that will not be whispered in Delphi, will not be repeated from Epirus to Attica, and the prudent Peloponnesian echo will hold its tongue.

And an old priestess --- the one who had once allowed a nameless holy fool to remain within the sacred precinct --- will catch, in passing, weary Heracles's words as he stops by a chariot that has just driven in through the gate:

"He is not to blame. Hermeias never told him the whole truth... no, Phoebus is not to blame. He didn't even know that his favorite, Admetus[^57] of Pherae, was one of the Possessed."

[^57]: Admetus, basileus of Pherae, an Argonaut --- Apollo's favorite, whom the god had saved from death more than once. Seven years before the events described, Admetus had asked his friends, his parents, and his household to die in his place (that is, to be an atoning sacrifice). The only one who agreed was Admetus's wife Alcestis --- whom Heracles later wrested away from Thanatos, the god of death. It was afterward hushed up why Apollo's favorite, for all his virtues, had no intention of dying himself, offering that honor to others instead.

And the young charioteer, his eyes sunken and older than his years, will nod, while the man standing beside him will jump down and go over to Heracles.

The old priestess blinked, worked her withered lips, shifting her gaze from one brother to the other, and shuffled off --- never hearing Iphicles murmur quietly:

"No one is to blame; and everyone is. It isn't cleansing you need, Alcides --- it's saving. Saving you from yourself."

* * *

At that same hour, on Olympus, the word of golden-bowed Apollo rang out:

"I will go to Phlegrae only together with the great Heracles, the best and the most hapless of mortals; or not at all."

And the Kronids exchanged embarrassed glances.

* * *

"Do you know how?" Heracles asked.

"I hope I do," was the answer.
