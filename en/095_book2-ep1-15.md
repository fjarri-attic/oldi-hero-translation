#### 15

He drove the chariot north.

The wheels thunder.

Delphi, soon.

Soon.

He drove the chariot, spurring on the snorting horses, while behind him rumor flew in a thousand-winged, clamorous flock.

"He killed his teacher and friend?!" the Messenians cried in horror.

"No doubt he didn't want to give back the herds he'd stolen from Eurytos!" the Eleians reckoned.

"What herds?!" the Argives protested. "What are you talking about?! This is the great Herakles, Slayer of Monsters! He was unfairly insulted on Euboia!"

"Monsters or no monsters," insisted the stubborn Eleians, keeping a closer eye on their own herds, "insults or no insults---herds, begging your pardon, are still herds! One doesn't cancel out the other. No doubt he lured poor Iphitos onto the wall---have a look, says he, aren't those your horses grazing down there?---and then shoved him off! Easy as that!"

"Jealous Hera, why do you punish him so?" whispered Arkadian and Lakonian girls, dreaming of Herakles on hot nights.

"A madman." They shrugged in Achaia.

"A hero!" they responded in Boiotia.

"The greatest..." From both alike.

Strong-walled Troy chuckled for the whole Aegean Sea to hear.

Oichalia and Pylos kept silent.

Then again, no---Pylos was silent no longer. And wanax Neleus of Pylos was lying to anyone who would listen, claiming that, on his way home from Euboia, he had met Herakles, who had supposedly begged him, pious Neleus, to purify the unwitting killer of miasma---but Neleus, a veritable wellspring of piety and caution, had refused Herakles the purification, citing his long friendship with Eurytos, the dead man's father.

What's that you say?

Ah yes, of course---with the late father of the dead man... now it's clear why salt shipped to the mainland from the saltworks of Euboia has gotten so expensive!...

And at the head of hundred-mouthed hordes, Herakles burst into sacred Delphi.

"Wash your hands in the Kastalian Spring!" the priests said severely, blocking the hero's path as he strode along the paved road past the rocky eastern wall. "And offer praise to radiant Apollo!"

"The nymph Kastalia turned into the Kastalian Spring, fleeing your god's advances," was the answer. "I will not wash my hands in the tears of the hapless one! Out of my way!"

"Put on a laurel wreath!" the priestesses ordered sternly, standing before Herakles at the temple entrance.

"The nymph Daphne became a laurel rather than yield to lustful Phoibos," was the answer. "I will not wear a wreath made from the hair of the hapless one! Stand aside!"

"There is no purification for you!" the enraged Pythia proclaimed, and the mist over the rocky chasm trembled ominously. "There is none, and there will be none!"

"Apollo killed young Hyakinthos, son of basileus Amyklas," was the answer. "Who purified your god of miasma, woman?!"

"In this temple, madman, no one will prophesy for you!"

"I am my own temple and my own oracle," was the answer. "Leave, woman, and do not stand between me and the god!"

And Herakles blasphemously seized the golden tripod on which the Pythia sat while prophesying.

"Apollo! Where are you, Olympian?!"---this roar would long haunt the Pythia, who fled in terror from the innermost sanctuary of the temple. "Show yourself, and answer Herakles!"

The answer was a fiery arrow, sent from the other side of the chasm.

The tripod traced a glittering arc, earthly gold met heavenly gold, flame met flame, and---nothing but sparks across the temple.

...No one will ever know the truth of how mad Herakles and furious Apollo grappled with one another; mortal and immortal. Only this will be whispered in Delphi, repeated from Epirus to Attike, and echoed across the Peloponnese: that strength met strength, forcing Zeus the Cloud-Gatherer to hurl his thunderbolt to part the wrestlers and keep his son from perishing... but which of his sons---that will not be whispered in Delphi, will not be repeated from Epirus to Attike, and the prudent Peloponnesian echo will hold its tongue.

And an old priestess---the one who had once allowed a nameless holy fool to remain within the sacred precinct---will catch, in passing, weary Herakles's words as he stops by a chariot that has just driven in through the gate:

"He is not to blame. Hermeias never told him the whole truth... no, Phoibos is not to blame. He didn't even know that his favorite, Admetos of Pherai[\*](#fn-57){.noteref}, was one of the Possessed."

And the young charioteer, his eyes sunken and older than his years, will nod, while the man standing beside him will jump down and go over to Herakles.

The old priestess blinked, worked her withered lips, shifting her gaze from one brother to the other, and shuffled off---never hearing Iphikles breathe softly:

"No one is to blame; and everyone is. You don't need purifying, Alkeides---you need saving. Saving you from you."

At that same hour, on Olympos, the word of golden-bowed Apollo rang out:

"I will go to Phlegra only together with the great Herakles, the best and the most hapless of mortals; or not at all."

And the Kronids exchanged embarrassed glances.

"Do you know how?" Herakles asked.

"I hope I do," was the answer.
