#### 7

...Red-haired Chalkodon, son of red-haired Antisthenes, had always considered himself a hero. So what if he was a goatherd? At least he was the chief one. So what if he wasn't a god's son? Not even a grandson. But somewhere around the sixth or seventh generation back, there had certainly been gods in his line. If not Poseidon, then Apollo; if not Apollo, then Zeus the Bull---and if there was a wench that one failed to favor, it was only through forgetfulness. But then, was it really a matter of ancestry? A hero is a hero anywhere, even in Ethiopia, and on the island of Kos all the more so.

And anyway: redheads---they're all heroes.

So when, in the dead of night, shepherds reported to Chalkodon that amid the roar of a raging storm an unknown ship was approaching the western shore, Chalkodon's soul was untroubled by hesitation.

[Pirates!]{.thoughts} decided the valiant goatherd, who had long thirsted for a taste of blood. [Vile plunderers! Who said there's nothing worth taking on Kos?!---I know better! And I know the gods are on our side! Great Zeus himself has scattered the night's gloom with lightning, so that my men might spot the treacherous enemy in time! Forward, Koans! Forward, fellow citizens! Let us not shame the cliffs of our fatherland!]{.thoughts}

And he did not shame them.

Having sent a messenger to the local basileus Eurypylos---who considered himself a son of Poseidon (with just as much right as Chalkodon)---the warlike goatherd ordered the whole brotherhood of shepherds to arm themselves, after which he took up an advantageous position on the steep coastal cliffs. Having laid in a sufficient store of stones for throwing---javelins and arrows were in far shorter supply than he would have liked---Chalkodon caught his breath and, when the next flash of lightning came, peered out at the raging sea.

The fifty-oared pirate ship was heading straight for a small cove ringed by the very cliffs where the shepherd-warriors lay in wait.

For a moment the impatient hero thought he glimpsed the silhouette of a second ship not far off, but Chalkodon instantly forgot the fleeting vision, for the pirate ship was already grinding its keel against the sand as it ran ashore.

[They could at least have run themselves onto the rocks,]{.thoughts} the defender of Kos thought with some regret, weighing a hefty stone in his palm and taking aim at the head of a huge, half-naked plunderer who was first to leap ashore.

If someone had told red-haired Chalkodon, son of red-haired Antisthenes, at that moment that the stone in his hand was the stumbling block for a cunning plan devised by Momos Eubouleus[\*](#fn-69){.noteref} and personally approved by Zeus---our hero would have been very surprised.

And he would have been right.

* * *

Lichas was the first to grasp what was happening when Alkeides went down on the shoreline sand like a felled tree. A blade of fire split the sky; the youth glanced up and, through the slanting streaks of the driving rain, made out human silhouettes atop the cliffs, from which stones and javelins were flying.

"Shields!" Lichas bellowed at the top of his lungs, leaping to the fallen Alkeides and trying to cover him with his own shield. "Get your shields up! Archers, shoot at the clifftops!"

Lichas himself had no bow, since he was a poor shot, and his skill with throwing knives or his beloved grappling hook on a rope meant nothing just now.

"Wait!" The youth grabbed the leg of a passing warrior whose shield looked large enough. "Help me cover him!"

He tacked on those last words in haste, since the warrior who'd gone sprawling beside Lichas was already preparing to voice everything he thought of such an invitation, and to back it up with action.

His chance partner, though, proved quick on the uptake, and between their two shields they managed to cover themselves and Alkeides, stretched out on the sand, reasonably well. Only then did Lichas bend over his idol and press an ear to his chest.

"Alive!" the youth breathed out with inexpressible relief.

"And who's this?" the warrior asked belatedly; by his accent, an Argive, one of the men of Oikles, who himself had stayed aboard.

"My best friend," Lichas whispered in his ear, rightly deciding not to elaborate, to avoid panic.

The shore was already strewn with the bodies of the wounded and the dead---shields did little good against stones raining down on every head from every side---the thud of impacts blended with the deafening peals of thunder, cries and groans drowned in the dragon-like hiss of waves smashing against the rocks, and the cold rain lashed sea, earth, and the scrambling men alike with the merciless whip of a slave-driver.

[I wonder, will it be like this in Hades, or worse?]{.thoughts} Lichas thought, watching well-armed Koans come pouring out from behind the western cliffs, spears leveled---the guard of the basileus Eurypylos, arrived at last---and, joining the shepherds leaping down from above, make for the conquerors of Troy now caught in the trap.

* * *

[It's time,]{.thoughts} Zeus decided, opening a Dromos.

The storm was by now raging over nearly the whole of Hellas; priests and Pythias in the temples, their eyes rolled back in the ecstasy of revelation, proclaimed that terrible events were unfolding on Olympos; the proclamation passed from mouth to mouth, and had surely reached those it was actually meant for---in short, everything was going as it should.

And the lord of gods and men strode briskly into the cocoon of the Dromos as it pulsed with silver light.

On the other side, a piercing wind at once struck the Olympian full in the face, the very rain he himself had called down drenched him from head to foot, and a few javelins whistled past close by---though the behavior of the disrespectful elements, and likewise the weapons of mortals, held no interest for Zeus.

He needed his own weapon.

He needed Herakles.

Illuminating the scene with a bolt of lightning that forked strangely across the sky, Zeus saw, in its eerie blue-white glare, a mighty body stretched out right at the line of the surf, and a slight, youthful warrior fighting off the advancing enemies with the last of his strength, keeping them from reaching the now-helpless Herakles. Another soldier lay two paces from the Thunderer, between the god and his goal, his face driven absurdly into the wet sand, the broken shaft of a spear jutting from the dead man's back, pinning him to the inhospitable shore.

Cursing the absurd mischances threatening to wreck a plan so carefully thought through, the Thunderer shook his fist at no one in particular and pressed on through the storm.

* * *

...Lichas had never thought himself a great warrior, and in other circumstances would long since have preferred to save himself by flight, or to die quietly and without fuss. But now had come the night when debts are paid, when life grabs you by the scruff of the neck and flings you into the fire without asking, forcing you to dodge, by some miracle, a death-dealing spear thrust and hurl yourself forward, to hack, to stab, to knock men off their feet---only to dodge the bronze sting again and go on living, when it seems impossible. His Argive partner had died almost at once, having managed to cut down one of the islanders first, and Lichas was left alone... no, of course other warriors who had leapt from the ship were fighting nearby too, but here, on this tiny scrap of foreign soil, Lichas had his own battle, one-on-one with the whole world, because behind him lay the unconscious Herakles, who had once saved a ten-year-old waif from Diomedes's man-eating mares, and tonight the waif was paying his debts, with no right to flee or to die.

The Koans shied away, not daring to close with the nimble pirate, with the ferocious demon whose eyes blazed with madness, who seemed able to be in several places at once, forever slipping the blows aimed at him without even needing to parry them.

"Alastor[\*](#fn-70){.noteref}!" shouted Eurypylos's soldiers, missing yet again. "The evil eye!"

Lichas, meanwhile, darted around Herakles on the very last of his strength, salt sweat running down his face mixed with streams of rain, clouding his eyes, though it was hard enough to make anything out in that pitch darkness as it was; the sky split open directly overhead---the youth had no time to realize this was simply another flash of lightning, only one that lasted oddly long---and beside Lichas there suddenly appeared an elderly but still powerfully built man, with shaggy brows and a beard gone thoroughly gray.

The stranger swept out a sinewy arm; a hurricane gust flung aside several flying spears like dry reeds; a ringing emptiness formed around them, the way it sometimes does at the very heart of a storm, and Lichas realized that a god stood before him.

More than that, the youth even knew which one; and this god had just saved his life.

"You fought well, boy." The god laid a heavy palm on Lichas's shoulder, and it somehow did not occur to the other that he ought to fall to his knees and offer praise. "Thank you---for him."

The god gave the youth an encouraging nod and, lifting the stunned Alkeides easily into his arms, stepped into the darkness and vanished into a funnel that flared up with silver fire.

Lichas did not know that no one but him had seen any of it.

He just stood there, sword lowered, the god's words echoing in his head: "You fought well, boy. Thank you---for him."

And a strong, calloused palm on his shoulder.

Alkeides was safe now.

It was all right to die.

And Lichas laughed in the faces of the stunned Koans, stepping straight into their spears---but just then a hand, no less strong than the god's own, seized him by the scruff of the neck and hauled him back.

"Where's Alkeides?!" shouted Iphikles, who had appeared out of nowhere, and Lichas first made out beside him Iolaos's bestial snarl, and only then, behind their backs, the silhouette of a second ship that had just put in, behind which the outlines of a third were already emerging from the rain-blurred gloom.

"They knocked him out. With a stone. And then... then Zeus showed up, told me I'd fought well, and carried Alkeides off."

Lichas understood that no one would believe him.

Especially the "fought well" part.

But there was no disbelief in Iphikles's eyes.

"How did they leave?" Iolaos cut in, glancing around and signaling commands to the warriors running up from the ships. "Where to?!"

"I don't know! They went into this... this glowing... well, it looked like a funnel!"

"A Dromos..." Iphikles muttered, now fully convinced that Lichas wasn't lying. "Well then, boy, you really did fight well! And now---shall we teach these mangy goatherds how proper hosts ought to greet their honored guests?!"

"Let's teach them!" Lichas whooped happily, and---who knew where he found the strength!---dashed off after Iphikles.

Beside him, sinking ankle-deep into the sand, Telamon's Myrmidons and the gray-haired veterans of Tiryns were already running; in a flash of lightning Lichas thought he glimpsed some tall figure atop the cliff, one that looked female, but helmeted and spear in hand---though there was no time to dwell on it, since he had to keep up with the club-swinging Iphikles, clad in the thoroughly battered, sodden lionskin he'd never gotten around to returning to his brother; and it was then that inspiration descended on Lichas.

"Herakles is with us!" A piercing cry rose over the shore. "Forward, heroes! Herakles is with us!"

"Herakles is with us!" a dozen throats took up at once, and with that cry a wave such as had never been seen, foaming with the points of swords and spears, surged onto the wavering island of Kos.

Expecting neither such an onslaught nor the reinforcements arriving to back the "pirates," the islanders began to fall back. Some managed to scramble back up onto the cliffs, but most were pinned against the rocks and forced to give battle. They fought blind, by ear, getting their bearings only in the brief flashes of lightning---but when Iphikles's club crushed with a dull crunch the skull of a graying, bearded man in battered ancestral armor, one of the Koans fighting nearby could not help but cry out:

"Eurypylos... they've killed the basileus!"

"Herakles has killed basileus Eurypylos!" Lichas at once chimed in, as always finding himself in just the right place---and the outcome of the night's battle was decided.

Swearing that they hadn't known they were dealing with Herakles himself (which, strictly speaking, was the plain truth), the Koans began to surrender, begging for mercy.

And the first to throw down his spear was red-haired Chalkodon, son of red-haired Antisthenes---deciding to save his boasting for later, once Herakles and his men had left the island.

The chief goatherd knew full well that glory is no use to a dead man.

Chalkodon might not have been a real hero, but he was most certainly not a fool.
