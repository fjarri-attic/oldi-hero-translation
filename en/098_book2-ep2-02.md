#### 2

And the Calydonian Hunt turned into such a monumental pan-Achaean disgrace that afterward many well-known men paid rhapsodes solely to keep the singers' mouths shut.

At first, though, the roster of hunters looked so imposing that the very thought of failure seemed sacrilegious. The Calydonian Boar, most people agreed, ought to have died of pride, since the flower of Hellas had come for its hide --- a gathering to match which had assembled only once before, sixteen years earlier, when the twenty-five-oared *Argo* set sail for Colchis after the Golden Fleece. And now, too, those of the Argonauts still living had shaken off the years as one and arrived in Calydon --- even the Dioscuri, Castor and Polydeuces; even the Apharetidae, Idas and Lynceus the Sharp-eyed; even the wretched wanderer Jason, who had lost wife, children, and homeland; even Theseus the Exile, whom the grateful Athenians had not long ago driven out, replacing him with a Mycenaean placeman, the demagogue Menestheus; even Amphiaraus the Seer, basileus of Argos --- though it would seem that if anyone could foresee the sorry outcome of this botched hunt, it should have been the prophetic Amphiaraus himself...

Divine Orpheus alone did not come --- still troubling the Pierian meadows with the laments of his golden-stringed kithara, never having learned to bear the loss of his beloved wife, Eurydice.

As for the young men --- ah, that youth, tormented by its thirst for feats and glory! --- they simply poured into Aetolia in herds, eager to take part and distinguish themselves. So the beat turned out crowded and clamorous, and it began with a scandal (some of the more zealous hunters decided it was unseemly for men to perform feats of arms alongside a woman, even if that woman was the virgin Atalanta the Huntress); then the boar, which had turned up unnoticed by anyone, trampled three of the troublemakers to death and fled, and the javelin Peleus flung after the beast lodged, by some inexplicable means, in the liver of Peleus's own father-in-law, dispatching the man into the gloom of Hades --- and some later claimed that poor Peleus had done it on purpose; more than that, that he had come to Calydon for no other reason.

When at last the tusked beast, grunting one final time, gave up the ghost, so many claimants stepped forward for its hide that had the pelt been divided among the heroes, each would have gotten a bristly scrap the size of a palm. By closed vote (ostracism, it's called --- invented in Athens) they singled out the three chief boar-slayers: Meleager the Invulnerable, son of the hunt's organizer, the Calydonian basileus; Atalanta the Huntress, soon to be Meleager's mistress, who traded her virginity for glory; and Amphiaraus the Seer --- who, being prophetic, would know best.

Harder still was agreeing which of the three had struck where, since there were, once again, three principal wounds on the boar --- in the back, in the eye, and in the groin. The virgin Atalanta blushed and wanted no part of the groin (enviable a target though it was!); Amphiaraus laid claim to the eye, and only the eye; and Meleager was indifferent at first, then he too took a fancy to the eye... the argument reached a deadlock, until Meleager glanced sidelong at Atalanta, who was making ready to leave, waved his hand, and settled for the groin, yielding the back to the girl.

The dead were buried, the pork was eaten, prayers were offered up, every drop of wine in Calydon was drunk, and everyone went their separate ways, pretending not to have heard someone's parting remark: "Pity Heracles wasn't here..."

A few men lingered a day or two, though: Peleus, mourning his father-in-law, and his brother Telamon (both, incidentally, old allies of Heracles from way back, from the campaigns in Thrace and against the Amazons), Amphiaraus the Seer, and Theseus, who had nowhere to return to.

Oh, and Heracles's brother Iphicles Amphitryad, with his son Iolaus, left last of all --- but not before a long, detailed conversation with the hunters who had stayed behind... about who knows what.

And it was this very "who knows what" that led to various minor events scattered across many corners of Hellas, though no one ever bothered to piece them together into a single picture.

First, hapless Peleus made his way safely to Iolcus --- the northeasternmost harbor of Hellas, from which the coast of Asia Minor was but a stone's throw away --- to be cleansed of miasma. There, once cleansed by the basileus Acastus the Argonaut, Peleus acquired, with funds from some unclear source, nine fifty-oared ships; for a tenth, the funds ran short.

His brother, boisterous Telamon the Strongman, second in strength only to Heracles, before long gathered a band of some three hundred Myrmidons --- and, missing Peleus, set off for Iolcus.

No one, mind you, thought to wonder why Telamon, trailed by ill repute the way a tail trails a rat, should have needed three hundred escorts in the first place.

Around the same time, two hundred Ionians --- professional soldiers of Theseus's, dissatisfied with the new Athenian order --- left Attica; from Argos, Amphiaraus the Seer sent his own son Oicles off somewhere with a detachment of roughly the same size; and a week later the garrison of Tiryns departed under Iolaus's command.

All roads, one way or another, led to Thessaly; to Iolcus.

When all this was related in passing to Tiresias --- the Theban oracle, who, nearing his hundredth year, had not risen from his kline these last years, though his mind had lost none of its old sharpness --- Tiresias spoke words that struck his household as raving.

"Woe to you, strong-walled Troy!" said old Tiresias.
