#### 8

And Cithaeron greeted its guests with a clamor of birdsong, the rustle of beech crowns, the untroubled peace of a summer forest, and copious libations to go with mutton roasted over coals.

For three days running now, Amphitryon and a mellowed Thelem had been trying to muster the resolve to leave the hospitable shepherds' camp, but resolve never came in the morning, the days were too hot, and by evening two or three amphorae (sometimes four or five) of Dionysus's heady gifts were already cooling in the stream, two or three lambs (sometimes four or five) were bleating pitifully as they were led to the knife, and two or three young shepherdesses (more often four or five) were heading down to the stream with inviting winks, to wash their day-weary members further downstream and discover that the members in question weren't quite so weary after all --- especially since the guests had proven remarkably spry for their age, as more than one shepherdess had already had occasion to confirm.

The next morning, the resolve failed to come again.

Meanwhile the rising generation, in the persons of Alcides and Iphicles, was getting acquainted with the young shepherds, who on the very first day had resolved to give the twins a proper "Pan's Initiation" --- which included riding a ram, getting dunked in the ice-cold spring, close acquaintance with a shepherd's staff, and any number of other things the youth of Cithaeron found highly entertaining.

"They're picking on the little ones!" the twins scowled darkly, unamused by the local sense of humor, and the initiation did not take place. Or rather, it did take place, just not quite as planned.

The racing ram shrieked at the top of its lungs, jammed into the thick of the scuffle; the spring took into its scalding embrace not at all the people originally intended for it; sturdy shepherd's staffs broke against heads and backs no less sturdy --- and as a result, peace and mutual understanding were established, and the young men set off in a friendly herd to spy on the bathing shepherdesses.

The girls hadn't been hiding especially hard to begin with, but spying was so much more interesting.

...Yet everything in this world has its limit --- excepting the immortal gods, may they keep us and never withdraw their favor! --- and the day came when Amphitryon and Thelem, with no small regret, left hospitable Cithaeron and set off for home, to Thebes.

And Iphitus of Oechalia at once remembered not only that he was his father's heir (Basileus Eurytus had ridden to Thebes, to the palaestra, four times over the past five years, and each time had come away pleased both with Iphitus and with the twins' progress, hiding a jealous little spark that would flare up now and then beneath his heavy brows), but also that he, Iphitus the archer, was now sole teacher to not one but two whole pupils!

The brothers had relaxed too soon, having tasted a bit of heady freedom! The grey-bearded shepherds could only marvel and chuckle into their shaggy beards, watching how, at Iphitus's command, the twins tore around the flocks like a whirlwind right alongside the sheepdogs, leapt back and forth across the stream with a hefty stone gripped in each hand, thrashed one another with an ash spear-shaft, and traded blows in fistfights with long-armed Iphitus for good measure!

As for the bow and arrows --- that's sacred, that is... See the pinecone on that pine? Which pinecone, you ask? There, that branch sagging off to the side, two cones at the tip, and a third a bit to the left... you don't see it? Feet wider than your shoulders, knees bent, back straight, a pebble in each hand --- now stand there and look until you do see it!

A true archer doesn't look, yet sees; doesn't see, yet senses; touches the target with his heart, hears the arrow's flight with his gut; he himself shoots, himself flies, himself cuts the air, himself quivers in the target and steps back out of the quiver as the next arrow...

Hey, don't hunch those backs, you lazy louts!

It was just then that the oleander thicket trembled at the far end of the clearing, where Iphitus of Oechalia was busy tormenting the twins; branches snapped, scattering flecks of sunlight, and the bushes burst apart with a hoarse roar --- a terrible, ravenous roar, neither beast's nor man's, but the kind with which, one imagines, a hungry Cerberus disturbs the silence of Hades.

Iphitus spun half around and drove an arrow toward the sound, into the tangle of fragrant branches, while a second feathered messenger was slower, nocking itself against the string with an offended little bump... there was a roar --- and then none, as though it had never been, silence all around, and only a faint whimpering from the oleander bushes, faint, barely audible, but --- by the gods! --- quite articulate.

"Ow, mommy mine!... ow, you idiot... ow, where do they even breed people who can't take a joke?... Ow, my dear mommy... my ear, oh, my poor ear!... ow, idiot..."

And then the forest burst out laughing, shook, and delivered itself of a many-voiced neighing mixed with ringing girlish laughter, stamping and crackling, whoops tangled up with squeals, clapping hands, deep-voiced hooting --- and Iphitus the archer stared wide-eyed at the crowd spilling out onto the clearing from every direction.

Goat-legged, goat-horned, shaggy, tailed, noisy, embracing merry girls covered in nothing but their own hair and someone else's furry paw at the waist, breathing wine, wild garlic, and the heat of a hale set of lungs... in a word --- satyrs.

As a city dweller, Iphitus rarely had occasion to cross paths with the free tribes descended from the Titans, be they the tree-folk Lapiths, centaurs, or these very satyrs --- and never in such an unimaginable quantity had he seen them before. Then again, recalling his dignity as a teacher and glancing back at the twins, Iphitus found himself nearly more astonished than by the sudden appearance of satyrs and girls: the brothers regarded the newcomers with indifference, without any particular interest, having neatly set their stones down at their feet and fallen into a quiet conversation between themselves.

"Forget them!" a scrap of a sentence Alcides tossed to his brother reached Iphitus's ears. "I'm not going off carousing with them! Those goats'll get drunk again and start pestering us; and if not them, then those sweaty bassarids... forget the lot of them! Better if we shoot some more with the bow..."

"What bassarids?" Iphitus asked, taken aback. "Who are they?"

"Just those girls," Iphicles explained to the Oechalian, scratching a cheek freshly bitten by a midge.

"Nymphs, or what?"

"They're not nymphs. Nymphs are quiet, they mostly sing in chorus, especially the napaeae --- the valley ones... or the limnades, the meadow ones. But these are bassarids... the ones in Dionysus-Bacchus's retinue!"

"Bacchantes!" Iphitus guessed. "Maenads!"

"That's just what we call them," Alcides cut in. "They call themselves bassarids, companions of Bassareus. Dionysus is Bassareus because he wears a certain kind of garment called a bassaris... except we don't actually know what that garment looks like, because we've never once seen Dionysus on Pelion! The satyrs there are quiet, though --- they're afraid of Chiron..."

And he broke off, clapping a hand over his own mouth.

But Iphitus had no time to dwell on the twins' words, for a quite young satyr sidled out of the oleander bushes, his wrinkled face radiating hurt and gloom. In one hand the satyr held Iphitus's arrow; the other he pressed, palm flat, to his ear, and thick blood dripped from beneath the palm, splattering heavily onto the grass and the satyr's fur-covered shoulder.

The wounded satyr walked up to Iphitus and stared up at the tall archer, blinking rapidly with moist, bulging eyes.

"Ohh, you beanpole!" he drawled mournfully. "Who shoots like that?! You're supposed to shoot the way everyone does --- and miss... otherwise you'll go and kill someone before you know it!"

"Then why were you roaring like that?" Iphitus felt a twinge of conscience. "I thought you were a wild beast..."

"Thought, did you... Tantalus thought too, and look where it got him --- Hades!" The wounded satyr, apparently having quite given up on him, waved a hand at Iphitus (the one still clutching the arrow) and turned to the twins. "Hey, lads, where'd you dig up this freak?!"

"You're the freak!" the twins leapt to the archer's defense. "He's no freak, he's our teacher Iphitus. You know how he shoots a bow?!"

"I know," the satyr grimaced. "Old man Silenus is going to show up any minute now and ask, 'What did you do with your ear, satyrisk Phoroneus?' --- and what am I supposed to tell Silenus, huh?"

"He's the youngest of his line," Alcides whispered to Iphitus, anticipating the next question. "That's why he's a satyrisk, not a satyr..."

Iphicles, meanwhile, walked right up to unfortunate Phoroneus and, muttering, "Stop whining, you're not a baby," made the satyrisk take his hand away from his injured ear.

The ear was long, hairy, and pointed --- or rather, it had been pointed until quite recently, because its entire upper portion, as it seemed to Iphitus, had been all but sliced clean off by the arrowhead and now dangled by a thin strip of skin.

Iphicles looked around, plucked a few leaves off some nondescript little plant, and started chewing them. Then he carefully pressed the dangling tip of the ear back into place and sealed the cut with his makeshift chewed-up paste.

"Work the earlobe," Alcides prompted his brother. "Remember, Chiron showed us?"

Iphicles nodded and set to work, intently kneading Phoroneus's earlobe between his thumb and forefinger. The satyrisk grunted and groaned but stood still; and even the company of his boisterous kinfolk had quieted down a little, watching Iphicles's work with sympathy.

"It'll heal up like on a centaur," Iphicles said finally, letting go of the ear and giving Phoroneus a slap on his furry rump. "Don't go fooling around next time... or else Iphitus'll shoot off something worse than an ear for you! Got it?"

The satyrs roared with laughter, and Phoroneus arranged something like a grateful smile on his face and, muttering under his breath, "Wouldn't put it past you...," hastened to retreat into the crowd of his kinfolk, where two plump bassarids at once set about comforting him.

The rest of the girls, twirling ivy-wound thyrsus staffs in their hands and voluptuously nibbling at the pinecone-crowned tips, openly looked Iphitus up and down, and the Oechalian felt, with horror, how under those looks his manly dignity was growing more and more dignified by the moment.

Before he could gather his wits, he found himself right in the thick of the crush, a gourd cup of unusually strong and fragrant wine in one hand, and in the other a dried fish already scaled, staring up at Iphitus in astonishment with eyes gone white from salt; the older satyrs teased the young satyrisks, the bassarids fussed over Iphitus, praising his skill with the bow, as well as the length of his arms, his legs, and his arrows; some full-bosomed, plump woman was already waving the arrow taken from Phoroneus about, having found time to wind ivy around its shaft and skewer a fat, resin-oozing pinecone onto its point... in a moment of clarity, Iphitus looked around for the twins --- and discovered the brothers dashing off with cries of "Grandpa Silenus!" toward an old, completely grey-haired satyr who had just emerged from the bushes, smacking his everted lips affectionately as he gathered the twins into his embrace and rewarded each with a friendly buffet.

Behind the old satyr, two more figures appeared from the bushes: a curly-haired youth in a short linen chiton, and someone else... also apparently a satyr, but bigger, calmer, and holding somewhat apart, keeping to himself, not mixing into the unfolding revelry, the way oil does not mix with water, floating instead upon its surface.

A strange pair --- misshapen strength and beautiful effeteness, an almost animal virility and an almost feminine languidness; a whim smiling with an unnaturally crimson mouth, and the gnarled, potent soul of the forest, planted firmly on two sharp-hoofed feet.

Dionysus and Pan.

It was as though someone had whispered a prompt inside Iphitus's mind, and he, who had offered sacrifices to both of them more than once, felt for the first time the true nearness of divinity, of the kind where one might reach out a hand, touch, and say to oneself: "Yes --- it's him!"

Then again, Iphitus almost immediately felt ashamed of the solemnity that had overtaken him, discovering that no one but himself had paid the newcomers any particular attention, the way a merrymaking gang of guests barely notices the temporary disappearance, and then the return, of the host.

Well, he'd shown up, so what --- nothing to it.

And curly-haired Dionysus himself lost no time worming into the crowd, pinching the nearest bassarid's thigh, winking at another, snatching a piece of cheese from some satyrisk and instantly gobbling up his prize, swallowing it almost whole --- before Iphitus could even blink, Dionysus was already standing right beside him.

"O Dionysus of the wondrous curls," Iphitus recited automatically, the familiar words of prayer, "accept my offering..."

And he splashed some wine from his gourd cup onto the ground at the god's feet.

"What are you, an idiot?" Dionysus inquired, no longer smiling. "Why are you spilling the wine?"

"B-but it's an o-offering..." Iphitus's breath caught with shyness, and he couldn't manage to say anything more.

"And he shot Phoroneus's ear off," the bassarid whose thigh had just been honored with a divine pinch immediately tattled to Dionysus.

"Well, that's the proper thing to do," Dionysus approved. "Good lad... but there's no call to go spilling wine regardless! Spill it at home if you like, but I'm right here, and I won't have good drink wasted. Want to make an offering --- go right ahead! Pour it in here!"

And he opened his mouth, which turned out to be immoderately wide.

Utterly dumbfounded, Iphitus poured from his cup into that fearsome maw, the Adam's apple on Dionysus's throat bobbed --- and Iphitus abruptly discovered that he was, in fact, still alive, that his cup was empty, and that Dionysus was roaring with laughter, having already forgotten about him and pelting crimson-with-shame Phoroneus with pinecones.

The tattling bassarid pressed herself close against her idol, whispering something fervently right into his ear, pointing first at Iphitus, then at the twins.

"A-ha, little kinsman!" the merry god flung his last pinecone at Alcides, who was sitting on the grass to grey-bearded Silenus's left, with Iphicles on the right. "Why're you doubling up on me, brother?... Hinting that I'm drunk, are you? No, I'm not drunk, no more than usual, and certainly no more than you, my dear mortal little kinsman, doubling up on me even though this is the first time I've ever laid eyes on him! Want to drink with a god, brother? Think you're sober?... No, you're drunk, same as the whole world..."

"Leave off, Bassareus!" the old satyr tried to calm Dionysus, but dangerous little flames had already flared up in the god's blue-black eyes --- and the crowd of Dionysus's companions broke into excited chatter, anticipating another spot of fun from their curly-headed leader.

Pan, standing apart, shifted from hoof to hoof, as though weighing whether he ought to intervene --- but it was already too late, regardless of what decision the goat-legged son of Hermes (at least, son of Hermes according to common belief) and promiscuous Dryope might have reached.

Too late --- because Dionysus had already pressed his crimson mouth to a wineskin obligingly handed to him, his cheeks swelling up in ruddy bulges, and then the wineskin went flying off to one side, and the merry god, to everyone's surprise, spat a great mouthful of wine right into the face of Alcides, who'd jumped to his feet. The boy coughed, sputtered, and started rubbing his eyes with his fists, while Dionysus flourished a thyrsus that had appeared in his hand --- the same as the others carried, but gleaming as though made of polished metal --- and rapped Alcides on the crown of the head with it.

There came a light, dry ring, as though the blow had struck a copper kettle.

Iphicles --- ruffled, hunched up, every bit a fledgling --- lunged to defend his brother, but stopped himself at the last moment, seeing that Alcides himself was not in the least offended by what had happened, standing there with a bewildered smile, while curly-haired Dionysus-Bassareus, in mock terror, shielded himself with the thyrsus and hopped backward away from the twins.

"What's the matter with you?!" was all Iphicles managed to shout before the satyrs' unanimous guffaw made him fall silent.

Some strange sense of being split in two took hold of the boy, keeping him from doing anything at all besides standing there like a post, dumbly snorting as he stared at the laughing god; somewhere deep inside a wrenching emptiness had opened up, and it was not right away that furious Iphicles realized he simply wasn't feeling Alcides's familiar support, his attentive presence, the way he always did --- except... except during fits of madness!

But there were no signs of an oncoming fit either --- and on that score Iphicles could not possibly be mistaken!

Iphicles turned his head to the left and discovered Alcides, giggling foolishly, trying to step forward, except each time he bent the wrong knee from the one he'd meant to bend --- so that he had to grab hold of Silenus's neck and sway there for a while, keeping his balance.

"Well, so what if I am!" Alcides suddenly announced, flushed an unnatural red, licking his slightly swollen lips again and again. "And who cares! Because!.. And a good thrashing --- that's always welcome! Like a hero, equal to the gods... and flatten the clods!"

Iphicles was overcome with unbearable shame.

He squeezed his eyes shut and missed the most interesting part: the satyrs' applause (though that much he heard), the bassarids' dance around a mockingly bowing Dionysus, the sudden thickening of the air at the edge of the clearing near a juniper bush that spread its prickly fingers in every direction --- and, finally, the appearance, out of the shimmering haze, of an opening Dromos...

"Dummes!" hiccupping, wildly overjoyed Alcides bellowed, and plopped himself down backward onto the knees of a grunting Silenus. "Hermy! Without you we've been like without our own ears!.."

It took Hermeias no more than a quick glance around and a sniff of the air to size up what was going on --- and that assessment, one may safely assume, was not a flattering one.

Even the little wings on the Trickster's sandals fluttered indignantly, lifting their owner a forearm's length above the ground.

"You moron!" Hermeias was simply beside himself, and Dionysus's retinue hastily bunched together, trying not to end up between the two quarreling divinities, the two sons of Zeus the Olympian: the elder, Hermeias, born from the womb of Maia Pleiades, and the younger, Dionysus, carried by Semele, the foolish daughter of wise Cadmus the Thebes-Builder. "Jester, a vine down your throat! Merrymaker! Drunkard! Got Erebus whistling through your skull, do you?! Drowned your eyes clean down to your heels!.."

"And who do you think you are?!" Dionysus snapped back, clearly flustered but unable to back down in front of his retinue. "Some teacher you are! Go teach your thieves, I can manage on my own, without advisors!"

"Without advisors?! Who was it handed you, a snot-nosed brat, over to the Nysaean nymphs to raise?! Who kept an eye on you?! Nursed a viper in his bosom, that's what! I'm going to fly straight to dad right now and tell him you're getting his heroes drunk!"

"Heroes!" Alcides, who had been listening to the spat with great attention, gave a second happy hiccup and jabbed a finger skyward in an admonishing way, nearly poking Silenus's eye out. "Getting drunk... a-hoy, I see the mountains, see the peaks of Cithaeron, a-hoy, cut the throat, let the blood pour in a stream!.."

No one was surprised that drunk Alcides had suddenly broken into song in a hoarse voice, eyes rolled back, nodding his head rhythmically in time; only old Silenus glanced uneasily at silent Pan, while Iphicles, coming back to himself, crouched down beside his brother and tried to bring Alcides to his senses, slapping him on the cheeks.

"Fly, fly, little tattletale!" Dionysus, it seemed, was starting to work himself up in earnest. "Flap those little wings! You were a Dummy and a Dummy you'll stay! Go on, go tell on me! --- or have your feathers gotten damp?! I could always sprinkle a bit more!"

"Why, you..." Hermeias was, at that moment, truly terrible to behold, and the hissing snakes of his caduceus were already poised to grapple with the living ivy of Dionysus's thyrsus, but the unexpected intervention of horned Pan, whom everyone had forgotten about, changed the situation in an instant.

"A-hoy," Alcides was mumbling meanwhile, shaking his head, "cut the throat, a-hoy, tear the veins, let the flood gush over my body, staining my own skin with purple!"

Pan bent his shaggy legs slightly, hunched his shoulders, knitted his shaggy brows, then clapped his hands softly, gave a low whistle, and stamped a hoof...

A dark, sickening numbness settled over the sun-drenched clearing; the bassarids' shrieking died away, the carousing satyrs pulled their heads down between their solid shoulders, Iphitus the archer let out a barely audible groan, glancing about in fright, even the gods took a step back from one another and flinched, as though fighting down nausea rising to their throats... it seemed as if --- should a branch so much as sway now, should a rotten bough crack beneath a careless foot, should some foolish bird whistle in the crown of an ash tree --- everyone who only a moment ago had been taking part in the Cithaeronian Bacchanal, or whatever one was meant to call the goings-on, would go bolting blindly off, careless of the way, leaving shreds of cloth, fur, and flesh on the thorns behind them --- gods, satyrs, people... every last one of them.

In a word --- panic.

But the branch did not sway, the bird held its tongue, the rotten bough remained unharmed, and little by little the world returned to its former state.

"That's more like it," Pan smirked, scratching the base of his left horn with a clawed finger. "Quarrel all you like, but I won't have fighting in my woods."

"A-hoy," Alcides went on regardless, a thread of sticky spittle crawling from the corner of his mouth down to his chin, "cut the throat, a-hoy, tear the veins; as I lived, so I died, lived a dog, died a prey..."

Hermeias, coming back to himself as though only now truly seeing the boy, rushed over to him, shook him, pulled back his eyelids, and looked into his rolled-back eyes. "He'll sober up, it's nothing much..." Dionysus started to say, but the Trickster only waved him off and looked questioningly at Iphicles, who was holding his brother's limply dangling arm.

"No," Iphicles answered the unspoken question. "It's not a fit. It just looks a lot like one. His head's spinning, I can feel that, and there's stuff flashing in front of his eyes... faces, all kinds of them, I can't quite make them out... and it smells of hides too. A really bad smell... no, not the slippery ones, those smell of mold, not hides!.."

"Just you wait, Bassareus!" someone hissed venomously --- Hermeias, or perhaps the snakes on his rod --- and the Trickster was gone.

Only glassy threads drifted through the hot air, and the satyrs and bassarids exchanged admiring glances --- no one could race along the Dromoi faster than Hermeias Psychopompos.

"Off to complain, is he," Dionysus muttered, with a certain bravado that in fact concealed his fright. "Hey, Pan, if that flier actually drags dad over here --- back me up that I meant no harm! Just joking around, tell him!... And it got out of hand."

What sullen Pan intended to answer remained forever a mystery --- because almost at once the Dromos opened again, letting Hermeias back into the clearing; and right behind the Trickster...

"It's the end of the wor-ld!" gasped the satyrisk Phoroneus, forgetting all propriety, backed up against an ancient beech. "Ch-Chiron... left Pelion! That's it, poor Phoroneus has drunk himself into it!"

"A-hoy, I see the mountains, lived a dog, died..."

One glance cast toward singing Alcides was enough for the centaur.

"Your doing, Bromius?" Chiron asked quietly of a crimson-faced Dionysus.

"Well," was all the merry god answered, who hated it terribly when he was called Bromius --- the Roarer.

Or, more plainly, the Blusterer.

"Not 'well' --- yours," the centaur concluded, heading toward the twins; and a miracle occurred: Dionysus held his tongue.

Iphicles watched Chiron approach with unconcealed hope, and the centaur gently smoothed the boy's hair, sensitive fingers parting the stiff curls, before bending his forelegs and, folding his fetlocks beneath himself, settling down beside Alcides --- the way trained Peneia ponies sink down in a clownish bow.

Only it's unlikely anyone would have thought to laugh at the sight of a kneeling centaur.

"Water!" Chiron commanded. "As much as you can, and as cold as you can! Who knows where the nearest stream is?!"

One of the satyrs tore off like a whirlwind, snatching up a half-empty wineskin, and dashed straight through the underbrush, smashing through the tangle of branches as well as any wounded boar.

"He's drunk," Chiron said quietly, not lifting his palm from Alcides's forehead (the boy had stopped singing and was now mumbling something unintelligible), speaking only to Hermeias, who had come to stand beside him, and to anxious Iphicles. "He's badly drunk... it's practically madness! Ah, I'm a fool --- he mustn't drink, not him! A foolish old bay, that's what I am... I should have warned Dionysus! Iphicles, please, for my sake --- concentrate! What is he seeing right now? What?! This is very important..."

"I'll try." Iphicles closed his eyes, then for some reason grimaced, screwing up his nose comically, as though bracing for a blow, while whoever meant to deliver it kept dawdling; and Hermeias suddenly understood what an inhuman effort it cost the boy to do this, voluntarily, to try to peer over the edge of madness, instead of screaming and bolting away from the monsters of a clouded mind.

"A-hoy, I see the mountains, see the peaks of Cithaeron... oh, everything's spinning! A-hoy, cut the throat..."

Iphicles sang --- his face going pale, jaw muscles rolling stubbornly, manfully, along his cheekbones --- sang in the same hoarse, cracked voice that drunk Alcides had been singing in only moments before, sang while clenching his fists tighter and tighter, as if wishing to turn them into bone hooves; a spasm wrenched the muscles of his compactly built body, turning the boy for a moment into a small Atlas hoisting the sky onto his shoulders for the first time --- except this sky was one that thirteen-year-old Iphicles Amphitryad had hoisted onto himself far from the first time, a sky of the gods of madness, one sky shared between two, a sky behind whose shield (perhaps!) hid the otherworldly and inscrutable One, baring its teeth with an entire universe.

That last thought belonged to Hermeias.

Alcides suddenly went limp, clutching his stomach, and was sick right onto the grass at his brother's feet, and the Trickster, watching Alcides retch up black bile, suddenly understood that at that moment the brothers looked more alike than ever --- unthinkably, impossibly alike... one turned to stone in an almost divine effort, the other writhing in an almost monstrous torment; Iphicles and Alcides, Olympus and Tartarus, two sacrifices at one altar, two wounds on a single body, unfortunate boys conceived at the crossing point of too many designs, hopes, and lofty purposes.

"Life and death," Hermeias still had time to think, "what do they mean for us, if we call ourselves gods and claim mastery over the one and invulnerability to the other; and what do they mean for them?! Perhaps it would be truer to call mortals the Living; perhaps we're all lying to one another --- we, with our immortality, they, with their death?!"

"A-hoy, I see the mountains," Iphicles suddenly broke off. "I see!... I see mountains! It's somewhere close by, this is probably Cithaeron... people, people all around!... and it reeks of hides. Only I can't see well, and this stupid song!... a-hoy, tear the veins, let the blood beat out in a stream... no, I won't sing! Chiron, Hermeias, it's not us, not me and Alcides singing, it's the one who's watching, and there are people in hides all around... yes, hides, and one more in a cloak, an old, patched one, and there's a knife in his hand... a-hoy, I see the mountains..."

"Hermeias, it's a sacrifice!" Chiron breathed out, and purple veins swelled on the centaur's neck, as though inside him an unborn scream were dying. "Somewhere close by they're offering up a human sacrifice! Not to Alcides, no! --- but Dionysus has clouded his mind with drink, and his soul has been a hostage to Tartarus since the day he was born!... Alcides is a sacrifice! And he feels himself to be the sacrifice --- the man who's about to be killed! If only we knew who he is, this man..."

"A brigand," Hermeias said curtly.

"Why a brigand?"

"It's a brigand's song, Chiron... they sing it in battle, or before an execution. Don't forget --- I am, after all, a god of thieves."

"Hermeias, the rite must be stopped! I don't know what'll happen to Alcides when the sacrifice is killed... ah, if only we were on Pelion!"

"We're not on Pelion," the Trickster gripped his caduceus tight with both hands, "and a brigand's no thief... not quite a thief... but I'll try! I'm already searching, Chiron, only I can barely hear anything, almost nothing at all... Iphicles, dear boy, is there a road anywhere nearby?! Any road at all!"

"A-hoy, cut the throat, let the blood... There is! There's a road, Dummy! Across the meadow, to the right... there's a herma! I see a herma! Ouch!... that one, in the cloak, is kicking with his feet... it hurts!... And someone's running from the road... that's it, I can't anymore!"

"Rejoice, Chiron!" Hermeias bellowed at the top of his lungs, taking to the air. "Rejoice! Found it! It's on the southern slopes! Only the herma's a long way off, better through the woods --- there's a clearing right nearby there... Pan, my boy, my forest marvel, hop over here, I'll explain! We need to..."

"No need," Pan cut in, having come up noiselessly, his tail twitching with agitation. "I can already feel it... looks like they're offering the sacrifice to me. There, sure enough --- they've started calling out! Some sort of sheep plague they've got over there, is it? Stinking shepherds! And then they wonder why I don't hear them!... Couldn't just offer some berries, or a lamb..."

"Or a wineskin," Dionysus put in, but the centaur merely glanced sideways at him, and the merry god --- who had, admittedly, lost a fair share of his merriment by now --- fell silent.

"There's no direct Dromos from this clearing to there," Pan said, surveying his surroundings like a proprietor, "which means we'll need to go down along the stream first, then left toward the old apple tree... yes, there's no shorter way. Follow me!"

The obliging satyr who came running back onto the clearing a minute later, hauling a wineskin full of water, was very nearly crushed and trampled underfoot by the rushing crowd, but managed to leap aside in time and got off with a few light bruises and a tuft or two of torn-out fur.

"Just try helping them after that!" he tossed out resentfully, shifted his grip on the wineskin, and hurried off after his kinfolk.
