#### 13

From around the bend came the straggling carts, laden with plunder from the three captured cities. The lead cart was driven by a proud Lichas in a brand-new sky-blue chiton --- and Iolaus, nodding farewell to Heracles, rose in his chariot and lashed the horses.

He had long since sent his own share of the plunder on ahead, under the reliable guard of fifty veterans grown old on campaign, who wished to settle down and spend the rest of their lives in some quiet place.

Tiryns --- being so close to Mycenae --- had never been a quiet place.

Iolaus had kept with him only a closed bronze helmet, much like the helmet of Ares, and --- the chief treasure! --- a blade of a rare and insanely costly metal: iron. That blade alone was worth half the goods Heracles's companions were carrying in the carts.

Iolaus lashed the neighing horses once more, and his chariot, jolting over a rut, turned east.

He was leaving for Phylace alone.

Laodamia, as though sensing something, came rushing out to meet him when Iolaus pulled up his chariot at her uncle's house.

They met on the very spot where they had said goodbye half a year before --- and, embracing the radiant girl, Iolaus admitted to himself that deep down he had feared meeting the past: a rumpled kline, a weary woman, and a sated god just departed, whom he, Iolaus, had missed by a mere instant.

No.

This was an entirely different beginning.

One that promised not a great future, but the hope of peace.

Then, holding hands like children, they went into the house to meet Laodamia's uncle, the basileus of Phylace, who was smiling broadly (the carts that had arrived the day before yesterday made that smile broader still), while the servants, somewhat dazed by the changes in their drab lives, clamored all around them --- but neither Iolaus nor Laodamia noticed.

The wedding was held a week later.

Life went on as it would.

Iolaus dabbled a little in trade, drove dull-witted and timid bandits away from the Phylacian herds, kept an eye on the Tirynthians as they trained the basileus's few soldiers in the craft of war --- but he did all this with a certain laziness, simply to fill his free time somehow, of which he now had plenty.

In the evenings he would sit for hours with his wife in a secluded arbor at the very far end of the basileus's garden, and Laodamia would tell him about her father, about her childhood --- while Iolaus told her stories from his own life that were more like fairy tales, and in these fairy tales there was always a happy ending.

"But the rhapsodes sing it differently!" Laodamia would laugh.

"Never mind those rhapsodes!" Iolaus would laugh back. "They lie, the idlers..."

At such moments he was almost happy.

He even began to forget his own name, because his wife now called him Protesilaus, "Iolaus the First" ("The first and only," she liked to add) --- after her, the servants took up this new name too, and Iolaus soon grew so used to it that Heracles's driver Iolaus the Mad, and the former lawagetas Amphitryon the Exile along with him, receded ever further into the past...

Into the place where his son was trying to go --- the greatest and unhappiest of mortals, god-equal Heracles.

Iolaus got away to visit Heracles only once, and even then not for long. The great hero, who had defeated the Aetolian river god Achelous[^83] in a wrestling contest ("Quite right, it was high time somebody broke their horns off," Iolaus approved on hearing of it), had married Deianira, sister of the late Meleager, and now led a quiet, measured life. Only he lived not here and not now, having withdrawn almost entirely into the past. His young wife soon resigned herself to this, all the more so since her husband behaved decently, his fame guarded Calydon better than any army, in bed Heracles was beyond all praise, fathered children diligently (and not only by his wife), and as for oddities --- well, who doesn't have them?!

[^83]: In single combat for the right to become Deianira's husband, Heracles defeated Achelous, who had taken the form of a bull, breaking off one of his horns.

When they met, Heracles stubbornly called Iolaus father, but those around paid it no attention --- Calydon had already grown used to Heracles's behavior.

After staying about a week, Iolaus returned home.

So a year went by.

And after it, a second.

Unexpectedly for everyone, an envoy arrived in Phylace from Thebes, bringing Iolaus a message from the Theban nobility. They invited their famous countryman to take part in the festivities marking the anniversary of the victory over the Argives, who had once besieged the city and fled in disgrace from beneath its walls.

The invitation hinted transparently that the Thebans would be very glad if Iolaus could persuade his uncle Heracles to come as well; but even should the great hero decline, Iolaus himself would be given a warm welcome.
