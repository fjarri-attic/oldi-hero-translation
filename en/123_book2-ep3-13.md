#### 13

From around the bend came the straggling carts, laden with plunder from the three captured cities. The lead cart was driven by a proud Lichas in a brand-new sky-blue chiton---and Iolaos, nodding farewell to Herakles, rose in his chariot and lashed the horses.

He had long since sent his own share of the plunder on ahead, under the reliable guard of fifty veterans grown old on campaign, who wished to settle down and spend the rest of their lives in some quiet place.

Tiryns---being so close to Mycenae---had never been a quiet place.

Iolaos had kept with him only a closed bronze helmet, much like the helmet of Ares, and---the chief treasure!---a blade of a rare and insanely costly metal: iron. That blade alone was worth half the goods Herakles's companions were carrying in the carts.

Iolaos lashed the neighing horses once more, and his chariot, jolting over a rut, turned east.

He was leaving for Phylake alone.

* * *

Laodameia, as though sensing something, came rushing out to meet him when Iolaos pulled up his chariot at her uncle's house.

They met on the very spot where they had said goodbye half a year before---and, embracing the radiant girl, Iolaos admitted to himself that deep down he had feared meeting the past: a rumpled kline, a weary woman, and a sated god just departed, whom he, Iolaos, had missed by a mere instant.

No.

This was an entirely different beginning.

One that promised not a great future, but the hope of peace.

Then, holding hands like children, they went into the house to meet Laodameia's uncle, the basileus of Phylake, who was smiling broadly (the carts that had arrived the day before yesterday made that smile broader still), while the servants, somewhat dazed by the changes in their drab lives, clamored all around them---but neither Iolaos nor Laodameia noticed.

The wedding was held a week later.

* * *

Life went on as it would.

Iolaos dabbled a little in trade, drove dull-witted and timid bandits away from the Phylacian herds, kept an eye on the Tirynthians as they trained the basileus's few soldiers in the craft of war---but he did all this with a certain laziness, simply to fill his free time somehow, of which he now had plenty.

In the evenings he would sit for hours with his wife in a secluded arbor at the very far end of the basileus's garden, and Laodameia would tell him about her father, about her childhood---while Iolaos told her stories from his own life that were more like fairy tales, and in these fairy tales there was always a happy ending.

"But the rhapsodes sing it differently!" Laodameia would laugh.

"Never mind those rhapsodes!" Iolaos would laugh back. "They lie, the idlers..."

At such moments he was almost happy.

He even began to forget his own name, because his wife now called him Protesilaos, "Iolaos the First" ("The first and only," she liked to add)---after her, the servants took up this new name too, and Iolaos soon grew so used to it that Herakles's driver Iolaos the Wild, and the former lawagetas Amphitryon the Exile along with him, receded ever further into the past...

Into the place where his son was trying to go---the greatest and unhappiest of mortals, god-equal Herakles.

Iolaos got away to visit Herakles only once, and even then not for long. The great hero, who had defeated the Aitolian river god Acheloos[^83] in a wrestling contest ("Quite right, it was high time somebody broke their horns off," Iolaos approved on hearing of it), had married Deianeira, sister of the late Meleagros, and now led a quiet, measured life. Only he lived not here and not now, having withdrawn almost entirely into the past. His young wife soon resigned herself to this, all the more so since her husband behaved decently, his fame guarded Kalydon better than any army, in bed Herakles was beyond all praise, fathered children diligently (and not only by his wife), and as for oddities---well, who doesn't have them?!

[^83]: In single combat for the right to become Deianeira's husband, Herakles defeated Acheloos, who had taken the form of a bull, breaking off one of his horns.

When they met, Herakles stubbornly called Iolaos father, but those around paid it no attention---Kalydon had already grown used to Herakles's behavior.

After staying about a week, Iolaos returned home.

So a year went by.

And after it, a second.

Unexpectedly for everyone, an envoy arrived in Phylake from Thebes, bringing Iolaos a message from the Theban nobility. They invited their famous countryman to take part in the festivities marking the anniversary of the victory over the Argives, who had once besieged the city and fled in disgrace from beneath its walls.

The invitation hinted transparently that the Thebans would be very glad if Iolaos could persuade his uncle Herakles to come as well; but even should the great hero decline, Iolaos himself would be given a warm welcome.
