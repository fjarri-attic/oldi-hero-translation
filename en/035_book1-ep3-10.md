#### 10

"Where are we?" Iphicles asked, frightened, squinting against the sun that struck him full in the eyes. "Where is this, Dummy?"

"Pelion," Hermeias answered curtly, offering no further explanation.

"It smells of juniper," --- Iphicles, it seemed, had accepted his friend the Dummy's word at once and without question, and was now trying to master his shock. "And grass, too... Alcides, can you hear me? We're on Pelion... you'd better shout, Alcides, or fight me, only don't lie there like that, like something dead..."

"Shut up!" Hermeias cut the boy off.

Iphicles obediently fell silent, swallowing the vile lump that had treacherously lodged in his throat, and trying not to blink away the tear hanging on his lashes --- but it slipped free all the same, a drop of salty dew falling onto a blade of grass, which bent beneath this unheard-of weight.

Hermeias did not so much as turn to him, as though Amphitryon's son no longer existed in the world. Carefully laying Alcides's senseless body on the ground, the Trickster made for a stand of maquis shrubs; they suddenly swayed, parting...

And Chiron stepped out to meet Hermeias.

Behind the Trickster's back Iphicles gasped, barely audible --- forgivable in a mortal boy, but shameful in a grown god --- yet Hermeias himself felt the urge to step back when he saw the centaur's unwontedly stern face. Chiron's shaggy brows drew together over the bridge of his nose, like storm-heavy clouds looming over a mountain ridge; his sharply cut mouth had hardened; heat lightning flickered scorchingly in his slanted eyes; under the silken hide of his horse's flank and his human torso the boulders of muscle rolled faintly; his four slender legs, fine-fetlocked, seemed rooted into the earth...

Drawn up to his full height, Chiron stood more than a head taller than the Trickster --- to Iphicles he must have looked a giant outright --- and it cost Hermeias no small effort to remember that he, Hermeias Psychopomp, was one of the Family, and had, in truth, nothing to fear.

He had simply forgotten how to be afraid, and now the sensation struck him with its novelty.

"Well," Hermeias mumbled foolishly, unable to tear his gaze from Chiron's face. "Well, Chiron... here we are. You did say --- if it's with them, you said, then it's fine without permission..."

The centaur did not answer. He was staring fixedly past the Trickster at the motionless Alcides, and as though in answer to that gaze, Alcides stirred, groaning hoarsely --- but the groan turned abruptly into laughter, just as hoarse, and no less inhuman.

Hermeias flinched, wheeling sharply around --- in that wild cackle he had heard the death-cry of Myrtilus, the Theban tutor, falling upon the knife that stood point-up, fixed in the makeshift altar. The sacrifice had been made, blood had spilled on the stone, the Fallen had drawn one full breath for an instant, setting the bronze walls of Tartarus trembling; Hermeias had faltered, listening to the rumble of the underworld, and had missed the moment when Alcides hurled himself at him.

From the terrible blow of the head to his stomach the Trickster's vision went dark; he doubled over, gasping for air, and only his hands did the accustomed work, wrapping around the body of the raging boy and flinging him aside --- no, not at all the way he wanted, over himself in a steep arc, so that only the heels would flash through the air --- but roughly, almost right along the ground, barely managing at the last moment to hold on to the boy, who had become impossibly heavy.

Alcides fell on his side, but sprang up at once, throwing himself at a hazel sapling growing nearby. The young trunk, wrist-thick, cracked pitifully when Alcides seized it with both hands and drove his bare heel with all his strength against its base; a ribbon of mottled bark tore loose --- and, holding out before him a stick with a white, frayed end, the mad child advanced on Hermeias.

Knowing not what he did, innocent and deadly, a raging beast, a wretched boy, the Nameless Hero; a door that everyone was knocking at --- Tartarus, Olympus, Zeus, Amphitryon, Hermeias, Eurytus, gods, men, not-men...

A target riddled by many a cunning archer.

And for the second time that day, Hermeias remembered what it meant --- to be afraid.

...The Trickster would never, to anyone, tell of this. And Chiron, too, would keep silent about it, because both of them --- god and centaur --- had seen the same vision: a purple-golden haze, in which molten gold mingled unnaturally with black-shot purple, and within it, in that shimmering mist, stood a two-bodied being beyond good and evil, beyond truth and lies, beyond Tartarus and Olympus --- yet equally capable of being either.

Pelion shuddered.

Hermeias did not grasp at once what was happening, and by the time he did, the centaur was already rearing for the second time, his forehooves striking the earth again, wringing mercy from the mountain. The eternal right of the sons of Kronus the Timekeeper: to call out to Tartarus by striking their grandmother Gaia the Earth three times, to call out and be heard.

Only Zeus the Olympian struck with lightning bolts, Poseidon Ennosigaios with his trident, and the centaur Chiron simply with his hoof. And Hermeias understood why it had mattered so much to the Family that Chiron the Kronid stand aside during the days of the Titanomachy, taking no part in the battle. And afterward Pelion had been given to the centaur, and his lack of love for the Family had been forgiven, and by the Styx they had sworn never to raise a hand against Chiron...

The hooves struck a third time, and a horse's neigh tore from the centaur's human mouth, rolling like thunder over Pelion --- so, it must be, had great Kronus once neighed, coursing the wide earth in the guise of an azure stallion after the mare Philyra, Chiron's mother. Tartarus listened to that distant call of Chiron's, Kronus the Fallen listened to the neighing over Pelion, the mountain of his brief happiness; and when the echo repeated the centaur's voice three times over --- little Alcides let his absurd stick fall from his hands and dropped backward onto the grass.

Hermeias looked at him, then at Iphicles, frozen like a statue.

And back at Alcides again.

"I was right," the Trickster whispered, raising the caduceus rod, wound with two serpents, which until now had been invisible. "I was right. A hero stands alone. A pity it turned out this way... I will lead your soul to Hades myself, boy --- it is all I can do for you now. Well then, shall we go?"

A stone struck him on the right shoulder, nearly knocking the staff from his hand.

Hermeias hissed, clutching the bruised spot; Iphicles, sobbing, hastily hurled a second stone, missed, and wiped his tears away with his palm, smearing dirt across his face. Then the boy bit his lip and stepped forward, planting himself between his brother and the god.

"You're a snake, Dummy!" Iphicles shouted, his voice breaking. "A snake... a liar! And we, we believed you, fools that we are..."

He sniffed and crouched down, gripping a boulder the size of a head, sunk a third of the way into the earth.

"Don't be afraid, Alcides," Iphicles clenched his teeth, not noticing the blood running from his bitten lip, and rocked the stubborn boulder with a hatred as though it were the head of that snake, the Dummy, which he meant to tear off. "Just don't be afraid, all right? We're going to kill them now and get out of here... go home. Just don't be afraid, remember that you're me... and I'm not afraid! Let them be the ones afraid of us..."

His thin arms strained, the stone head rocked resentfully in its socket, disturbed worms and woodlice spraying out from beneath it in every direction. Iphicles grunted, hoisting the burden that kept slipping from his hands up to his knees, nearly dropped it, but caught the stone from below at the last moment and, unexpectedly, swung it easily onto his shoulder.

He did not see Alcides rising behind him, having come back to himself: first onto his knees, blinking in astonishment and shifting his gaze from his brother to Hermeias, who had raised the staff once more; then to his full height, snatching up the fallen stick, his eyes narrowing unkindly, asking no questions at all.

After the fit, Alcides was unsteady on his feet, but this did not make him look like a sick child --- if anything, he looked like a fist fighter stubbornly rising after a blow he'd failed to dodge.

No, Iphicles did not see any of this; the stone had simply become half as heavy, and death had turned into something distant and utterly impossible, as it should be when you are eight years old.

"Or when you are the son of Amphitryon, grandson of Alcaeus, great-grandson of Perseus, and only after that the great-great-grandson of some Zeus who sneaks around other men's bedchambers" --- the thought flashed through Hermeias's mind, and at this alien, seditious, unnatural notion, cold sweat broke out on the Trickster's brow.

Shoulder to shoulder, stooped in a way no children should be, glowering from under lowered brows --- Hermeias recognized, with horror, the fighting stance of Autolycus, his own son --- before Hermeias stood Strength. Young, unhardened, still fragile, aware of itself for the first time, Strength looked at a friend turned enemy, at the first betrayal of its life, and in the eyes of Strength, through the tears that veiled them, could be read plainly the wish to kill, to kill forever, without mercy and without regret... With that absurd stone on his shoulder, with that naive stick in his hands, before Hermeias stood the newborn Lone Cleaner, Slayer of Monsters --- and he, Hermeias Psychopomp, was in that moment the traitor, the scoundrel, the monster, the refuse that ought to be swept away or burned; and the caduceus rod wound with serpents was, at that instant, no less absurd and laughable than the filthy boulder or the hazel switch, because the twins no longer saw a friend in the Dummy --- but neither did they see a god.

Not Tartarus --- the Trickster had set himself, by his own betrayal today, against these children as against equals, like a snake cornering a polecat; and Ananke the Inevitability had stripped Hermeias of the right to decide and to choose, leaving him only the right to fight against those who had just said: "We're going to kill them now and get out of here."

Kill them and leave.

For the first time, the staff felt unbearably heavy to Hermeias.

...The Trickster nearly fell when Chiron imperiously pushed him aside and, as though nothing at all were wrong, walked toward the twins.

The centaur's tail traced an elaborate figure-eight in the air; Chiron bent down, plucked a yellow narcissus flower, and tucked it behind his own ear.

"Can you throw it as far as the ash tree?" the centaur asked Iphicles calmly, pointing with one hand at the stone on the boy's shoulder and waving the other toward an ash growing twenty paces off.

"I can throw it as far as you," Iphicles retorted with hostility, and, after a moment's thought, added: "Don't come any closer... you horse."

"Horse yourself," Chiron laughed, dancing in place. "Don't get a hernia, hero! Come on, hand over that little pebble..."

Hermeias watched, astonished, as Iphicles meekly handed the centaur his weapon, and Chiron weighed the stone in his palm --- the centaur's eyes flashed strangely as he did --- and hurled the boulder at the old ash.

"Wow!" the twins, forgetting everything else, breathed out together, as the stone crashed into the gnarled trunk and, tearing off bark, fell at the foot of the Pelian giant.

"What did you expect!" Chiron answered them in kind, teeth gleaming --- teeth an envious man would sooner have called horse-like. "Well then, lads, shall we ride to my place?"

"Ride?" Alcides echoed, distrustful, having flung his stick at the ash tree and missed. "What, are we going to harness you to a chariot?"

"Not likely!" the centaur laughed. "We'll manage well enough without a chariot..."

And he caught up first Iphicles, then Alcides, under the arms, bending and settling the brothers onto his horse's back.

"Hold on!" Chiron ordered sternly, though there was no need: Alcides clung to his brother, Iphicles gripped the centaur's right elbow, and two pairs of heels drummed cheerfully against Chiron Kronid's bay flanks.

"Ah, too bad there's no goad!" Alcides started to say, gazing wistfully at the stick he'd thrown away, but the centaur pretended not to hear, and set off at a slow amble toward the maquis shrubs, from which he had appeared not long before.

At the edge of the shrubs he paused and glanced sidelong at Hermeias, standing alone, as though noticing him for the first time.

"Shall we take this one along?" Chiron asked casually, toying with the flower behind his ear with his free hand.

"No!" the twins yelled indignantly, and Iphicles even shook his fist at the Dummy, nearly toppling off, and hurriedly grabbed hold of Chiron again.

"Quite right!" the centaur agreed, tossing his tangled mane. "We won't take him! And if he comes on his own, why, we'll..."

"Cut his throat!" Alcides suggested.

"Hang him!" Iphicles added.

"And then drown him," Chiron summed up, quite seriously. "And after all that, we'll make him build a fire and cook us some stew. Agreed?"

And, without waiting for an answer, he neighed merrily and pushed straight on through the bushes.

When the crackling, the hoofbeats, and the shouting had drawn considerably away, Hermeias, left alone, scratched the back of his head, tucked the staff into his belt, and, shrugging helplessly, set off after Chiron, cursing under his breath at the thorny branches that kept trying to lash the Dummy across the face.
