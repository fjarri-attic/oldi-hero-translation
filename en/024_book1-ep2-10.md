#### 10

Upon his return to Thebes, Amphitryon went at once to Creon to arrange for a plot of land within the city for a palaestra --- a private gymnastic school. There a pleasant surprise awaited him: Creon, looking at his friend with a sly expression, declared that if Amphitryon had no objection to the new palaestra also taking on the sons of certain noble Thebans as pupils (for a considerable fee, naturally), then he, Basileus Creon, would order construction to begin the very next day, on the broad empty lot ten minutes' walk from Amphitryon's house.

And two-thirds of the expense he would take upon himself.

Amphitryon did not at once see what his objections had to do with it, or where such generosity had come from --- surely not from friendship alone? --- but then he understood: a palaestra with teachers famous throughout all Hellas would at once add to the glory of Thebes, and to Creon personally, while the greater part of the cost would fall on the treasury regardless.

They agreed that the new palaestra would open in a year's time --- by which point the teachers Amphitryon had gathered promised to settle their current affairs and arrive in Thebes --- and parted well pleased with one another.

That evening Amphitryon withdrew with Alcmene to the megaron, listening to her account of all that had happened in his absence. Between them stood a little table on legs curved into the shape of lion's paws, and Amphitryon reached now and then for the nearest dish, took a freshly baked flatbread, dipped it into a bowl of thick yellow honey, and ate it with pointed relish.

He rarely ate sweets, but his wife baked the flatbreads with her own hands, and choosing the best honey at the market was Alcmene's particular pride; besides, she loved very much to watch Amphitryon eat.

It filled her with calm and certainty.

...Amphitryon held out his free hand, and Alcmene, sitting beside him on a low stool, happily pressed her cheek against his palm.

"And Tephia gave birth ten days ago," she said, after a moment's thought, meaning one of her trusted Phoenician slave women. "A girl. Healthy, and loud-voiced. I asked Tephia --- from whom, then? --- and she just laughed and wouldn't answer. Though what was there to answer, when the girl is the very image of Philid the watchman! Only eyelashes are all Tephia's, so long... And here I'd been wondering why Philid kept hanging about our house!"

"Poor girl," Amphitryon murmured, calling to mind with some effort Philid the watchman's overgrown mug. "What did she do to anger the gods? Should I free your Tephia, then --- let Philid marry her..."

Alcmene broke off a piece of the flatbread, turned it in her fingers, but did not eat it.

"No need," she said with a small laugh. "I already told Tephia --- do you want your freedom? And she cried out at once! Why are you driving me away, mistress, I'll be lost without you, better give me the lash if I'm at fault... I could hardly calm her down. And crying sours the milk, besides. Well, that's all, I think. Nothing else happened. And how are things with the palaestra?"

"Splendid," Amphitryon licked the honey from his mustache and grimaced imperceptibly. "Creon turned generous --- must have had a vision, no doubt! Two-thirds of the expense he's taking on himself. A stadium, a pool, a covered gymnasium... let Mycenae be envious!"

"Let them," Alcmene agreed, knowing full well that Creon's vision had been his own wife Nausicaa, with whom Alcmene had already spoken more than once on the matter of the palaestra. "And when will you be finished?"

"Not before a year from now. Next autumn, at the earliest."

They fell silent, each thinking their own thoughts.

Amphitryon was wondering whether to tell his wife about the gaunt ragged man he had seen in Autolycus's courtyard, who had stirred up old and troubling memories in him; and whether to speak, too, of the sacrifice made in honor of their young son (o Zeus, I remember, I remember --- your son, truly your son!...), or whether it was better not to trouble Alcmene needlessly.

Alcmene, meanwhile, was wondering whether to tell her husband about Alcides's strange fits, which over the past half year seemed to have grown less frequent, and about which the wise woman Galinthias had said that with age they would stop altogether; or whether there was no need to trouble her husband further, burdened as he already was with so many cares.

"No, no need," they both decided, and smiled at one another.

"Next autumn," Amphitryon repeated. "And then, half a year after that, in spring, I'll send the boys to the palaestra. As they say, the bones are mine, the flesh is the teachers'. Let them teach..."

At first Alcmene did not understand.

"Spring?" she asked. "But boys aren't sent to the palaestra before they're twelve!"

"That's right," Amphitryon nodded. "At twelve."

"But by then they'll have only just turned six!"

"Yes. Six, and a bit."

"But..."

It was not the hardships of study that Alcmene thought of first, nor the sternness of the teachers --- no, another thought beat like a trapped bird in her mother's mind.

"But the older boys will bully them!"

Amphitryon rose and walked to the wall where the rack of his spears stood.

"They will," he answered quietly, looking at the bronze spearheads. "They certainly will. I very much hope they do."

And a grim shadow fell across his face.
