#### 13

"Receive your guest, wise Autolykos," Iolaos said quietly, standing on the threshold of the megaron into which a silent, stocky servant had shown him, seeing nothing yet after the bright sunlight outside.

"And why don't you say 'rejoice' to me, Amphitryon, my friend?" an old man's mocking voice came from beside the cold hearth. "Or do you suppose I'm too decrepit to rejoice?"

His eyes gradually grew used to the gloom, and Iolaos finally made out the sick man's kline, and beside it, on a sturdy beechwood stool, sat... Hermeias.

"He knows," the youthful god said quietly. "I told him. Just before you came in."

And Iolaos understood that Autolykos was dying.

It wasn't even that his old friend had long since crossed the sixty-year mark, that his head had gone bald, that wrinkles had cut deep furrows into his face, and that the former wrestler's body, always heavy, had grown outright fat and unwieldy.

That wasn't it.

"Forgive me for not getting up." Autolykos gave his familiar smirk. "My father here says it's bad for you to get up before dying... and undignified besides. And what's this Thanatos to me, anyway, when our boy roughed him up?! I'll meet the Iron-Hearted, threaten him with Herakles---and who knows, I might come back..."

"You will." The young father sat at his dying son's bedside. "You will. It's all been arranged with the Sovereign."

Iolaos ground his teeth without meaning to.

He remembered his own return all too well.

And he knew that several years earlier a son had been born to Autolykos's sharp-tongued daughter Antikleia and to Laertes, basileus of the island of Ithaka. Laertes had named the boy Odysseus, meaning "The one who angers the gods," because those whom the gods would destroy, they first make mad---in short, the boy had been born feeble-minded.

Iolaos needed no explanation of what that might mean for the dying Autolykos.

"Will we meet again?" Iolaos asked, not expecting an answer.

"No," Hermeias said curtly. "This time uncle Hades won't even tell me what he's planning. So there's no reason for the two of you to meet."

They fell silent.

"Iphitos the Oichalian gave me a bow the other day," Autolykos announced out of the blue. "As a ransom for the herds Herakles stole from his father. Now, of course, I know nothing about that, nothing at all---but I promised to look into it. And I will. If I don't die first."

[You old rogue,]{.thoughts} Iolaos thought with satisfaction, smirking as he sat down on the empty stool. [You don't even bother hiding that the herds were your people's doing! No, Trickster, you spoke too hastily there... the late Amphitryon and the late Autolykos, never to meet?! If I don't know him by Iphitos's bow, I'll know him by his character... Are people like us really born that often?!]{.thoughts}

"So the herds will go back to Eurytos?" he asked cheerfully.

"Hardly," Hermeias snorted. "A dear big brother of mine---Apollie---paid me a visit recently... kept asking about Eurytos. Well, I told him a thing or two: about Myrtilos the Theban, and so forth... So I doubt there'll be anyone left to give the herds back to. Or---there's already no one."

"But Eurytos is one of the Possessed!" Iolaos sprang up.

"That's right. So let him clear off to Erebos for a heart-to-heart with uncle Hades."

"But Eurytos's shade won't go to Hades willingly! Don't you remember how Galinthias and the other Possessed passed on?! Or is Apollo a Psychopompos too, a Soul-Guide like you, Hermeias?!"

"Tartaros into Erebos's murk!" Hermeias's face changed. "Why didn't I think of that myself!"

But the Trickster never got the chance to dash for the door.

In the half-darkness of the megaron, barely audible, came the words:

"I, Apollo Thyraios..."
