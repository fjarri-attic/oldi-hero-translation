#### 13

"Receive your guest, wise Autolycus," Iolaus said quietly, standing on the threshold of the megaron where a silent, stocky servant had led him, seeing nothing yet after the bright sunlight outside.

"And why don't you say 'rejoice' to me, Amphitryon, my friend?" a mocking old man's voice came from the cold hearth. "Or do you suppose I'm too decrepit to rejoice?"

His eyes gradually grew used to the gloom, and Iolaus finally made out the kline where the sick man lay, beside which, on a sturdy beechwood stool, sat... Hermeias.

"He knows," the youth-god said quietly. "I told him. Just before you came in."

And Iolaus understood that Autolycus was dying.

It wasn't even that his old friend had long since crossed the sixty-year mark, that his head had gone bald, that wrinkles had cut deep furrows into his face, and that the once-heavy body of the former wrestler had gone outright fat and unwieldy.

That wasn't it.

"Forgive me for not getting up," Autolycus smirked, familiar as ever. "My father here says it's bad for you to get up before dying... and undignified besides. And who is he, anyway, this Thanatos, if our boy roughed him up?! I'll meet the Iron-Hearted one, threaten him with Heracles --- and who knows, I might come back..."

"You will." The young father sat over his dying son. "You will. It's all been arranged with the Lord."

Iolaus ground his teeth without meaning to.

He remembered his own return all too well.

And he knew that a few years ago a son had been born to Autolycus's sharp-tongued daughter Anticlea and to Laertes, basileus of the island of Ithaca. Laertes had named the boy Odysseus, meaning "The one who angers the gods," because those whom the gods would destroy, they first make mad --- in short, the boy had been born feeble-minded.

Iolaus needed no explanation of what that might mean for the dying Autolycus.

"Will we meet again?" Iolaus asked, not expecting an answer.

"No," Hermeias cut him off. "This time uncle Hades won't even tell me what he's planning. So there's no reason for the two of you to meet."

They fell silent.

"Iphitus the Oechalian gave me a bow the other day, out of nowhere," Autolycus announced. "As a ransom for the herds Heracles supposedly stole from his father. Now, of course, I know nothing about that, nothing at all --- but I promised to look into it. And I will. If I don't die first."

"You old rogue," Iolaus thought with satisfaction, smirking as he sat down on the empty stool. "You don't even bother hiding that the herds were your people's doing! No, Trickster, this time you've overreached... to keep the late Amphitryon and the late Autolycus from meeting?! If I don't know you by Iphitus's bow, I'll know you by your character... Are people like us really born that often?!"

"So the herds will go back to Eurytus?" he asked cheerfully.

"Hardly," Hermeias snorted. "My dear big brother paid me a visit recently, Apollie... very curious about Eurytus, he was. Well, I told him a thing or two: about Myrtilus the Theban, and so forth... So I don't think there'll be anyone left to give the herds back to. Or --- there already isn't."

"But Eurytus is one of the Possessed!" Iolaus sprang up.

"That's right. So let him march off to Erebus for a heart-to-heart with uncle Hades."

"But Eurytus's shade won't go to Hades willingly! Don't you remember how Galinthias went, and the other Possessed?! Or is Apollo a Psychopompos, a Soul-Guide, same as you, Hermeias?!"

"Tartarus into Erebus's murk!" Hermeias's face changed. "How did I not think of that myself!"

But the Trickster had no time to dash for the exit.

In the half-darkness of the megaron, barely audible, came the words:

"I, Apollo Thyraios..."
