#### 6

Five men, none of them young, sat facing Iolaus, who had paused for a moment on the threshold of a rectangular, somewhat elongated megaron, its open forehall reached by a colonnade leading in from the palace courtyard.

Five lords.

An old acquaintance, Eurytus the marksman, basileus of Oechalia and the unofficial master of all Euboea; snow-white hair, caught at the brow by a band, falling thickly onto his shoulders, the tanned skin of his face gathered into a multitude of creases, bony hands lying heavy on the arms of his chair, the footstool cast aside as needless --- even seated, Eurytus of Oechalia seems taller than all the rest, so that the basileus's sharp knees jut up indecently high, as though a grown man had, by mistake, sat himself down in a child's little chair.

That would have been funny, had it concerned anyone else.

Another acquaintance, though of a more recent time --- the miser Augeas, fertile Eleia clutched fast in his grasping little fist; a flabby, squat, balding man with an unhealthy complexion, whose soul was hardly cleaner than his famous stables. More than once the Eleian basileus had tried to prove his descent now from Helios, now from Poseidon --- but all the Peloponnese remembered too well the brigand exploits of Augeas's true father, the dissolute Phorbas the Lapith, who together with his fellows had robbed pilgrims on the roads of Phocis and once even set fire to the temple of Delphi, for which he was wounded by an arrow of Apollo.

Or, to put it more plainly --- fell gravely ill, but somehow lived.

Beside the lopsided Augeas sat the Spartan Hippocoon, who had seized power by force from his own brothers, Icarius and Tyndareus, driving them out of Sparta. Iolaus was seeing Hippocoon for the first time, but the basileus's haughty, petrified bearing, his lean, muscular torso, the scalding ice of his gaze --- all of it betrayed at once a native of Laconia, a man easier to kill than to persuade.

Though killing him, too, is no easy matter.

Laomedon the Trojan stood by the hearth, squinting absently into the heart of the fiery blossom. Crimson flickers wandered unsteadily across his pale, well-groomed face (Laomedon's beard grew poorly --- or rather, did not grow at all), and Iolaus suddenly, sharply recalled that same crimson murk which the living are ill-advised to remember --- the gloom of Hades, the heat-lightning of the Isles of the Blessed... Now the Trojan rubbed his hands together, turned, and nodded amiably to Iolaus --- no, that was not how he had looked, once, when he drove his daughter's savior from Troy, trusting to the strength of his walls, and to the hope that Heracles was in a hurry to return to Mycenae after the campaign against the Amazons.

"Not here," Iolaus reminded himself.

And quite at the edge, on a low stool, half-hidden by the back of Eurytus's chair and by one of the four columns ringing the megaron's hearth, perched Neleus, wanax of merchant Pylos.

Gray on gray.

A shadow, not a man.

Look, and you won't notice him.

Without rising, Eurytus took up from a small table --- its curved legs, shaped like lion's paws, unpleasantly reminded Iolaus of things past --- two shimmering, chased kraters of wine, and raised his hands aloft, unmistakably intending one of the cups for his guest.

"Enter, O most worthy Iolaus, and join us with an open heart, pouring a libation in honor of god-equal Heracles, Slayer of Monsters!" the basileus of Oechalia proclaimed, in a voice too resonant for an old man's.

"Clever," Iolaus judged, approaching the table and, with a bow, taking the offered krater. "He hasn't introduced anyone present: flattered them all at once --- who doesn't know you, my lords?.. and me too --- walk in freely, as an equal among equals; and Heracles --- see, despite the regrettable misunderstanding, we're pouring libations, singing his praises... So why is it you didn't send for Alcides, then, and sent for me instead?!"

The wine scorched his throat in a spiced stream, the cup emptied --- and Iolaus set it back down on the table.

Unobtrusively, he looked around.

"Was the road here from Tiryns a good one?" --- even friendly words, in Augeas the Eleian's mouth, somehow came out sounding greasy. "Sit, sit, Iolaus, there's a chair free right there..."

"Good," Iolaus answered curtly, sitting down. "Praise the gods."

The lords exchanged glances.

"And the heroes," Eurytus added quietly, and his fingers, with a dry crack, closed tightly around the armrests.

"Now there's someone who deserves a low bow for our roads," Augeas smoothed his sweaty bald patch with a palm, "and that's the heroes. And first of all --- god-equal Heracles, best of men. Wiped out the monsters root and branch, brought the brigands to heel, centaurs and Lapiths --- squashed flat... the ones still left are petty vermin, all snapping at one another, no time for us! Trade to your heart's content! Good neighbors, I to you, you to me, they to us..."

"And everyone --- to Mycenae," the blunt Hippocoon cut in suddenly, stamping his coarsened palm on his knee for emphasis.

On his stool, the shadow of Neleus of Pylos stirred --- had he meant to say something? No, he held his tongue --- while the crimson flickers on the Trojan's face gathered themselves into a smile.

As if to say: not all of it --- to Mycenae.

So bear that in mind, most honored ones.

"Here we're all among friends," Eurytus leaned forward sharply and fairly fastened his gaze onto Iolaus's face. "And we speak as friends do, without beating around the bush."

"Go on, then, look all you like, archer," Iolaus smirked to himself (spreading, just in case, into a vain grimace --- well, of course, the great men had counted him among their own!). "You won't see much that I don't choose to show you... And still, what is it you want from me, rulers? To sing along in praise of Heracles?.. As a sixth voice?"

Apparently the basileus of Oechalia was satisfied with his inspection --- for he leaned back again against the chair's back and toyed with his long, dry fingers in the air.

As though catching a fledgling.

"Why has your uncle Heracles, now that his service is over, not taken the Mycenaean throne in Eurystheus's place?" Eurytus the archer asked, gently, almost tenderly. "Everyone from Euboea to Pylos has been waiting for it... will we live to see it?"

And again Laomedon smiled.

From Euboea to Pylos --- while we Trojans lie a good deal farther east...

"What am I to them?" Iolaus reached for the krater, as though he'd forgotten the cup was empty. "Something like Lichas?.. Little wit, plenty of scars, pride beyond his years; is it my right of succession you mean to buy, my lords? If I told you what that right of mine is really worth --- the wine would likely stick crosswise in your throats!"

"Hardly." He took up the round-bellied jug, painted with stylized octopuses, tilted it, and a crimson stream poured into the krater. "You lot know Heracles mostly from the fables of blind rhapsodes, while I've been at his heels since the cradle... A hero? Yes, a hero. Great? Yes, great. The gre-a-test! But first and foremost, he is the son of Zeus!" --- for greater conviction, Iolaus jabbed his cup at the ceiling, splashing wine over himself. "The son of Zeus! Whatever the heavenly father commands, that he does! Will you say that's wrong?"

"That's right!" Augeas agreed hastily; the rest kept silent.

"And I say that's right, too! But sometimes --- it's a pity." Iolaus drained the krater in one gulp, noting in passing the absence of servants in the megaron, and of any food on the table. Well then, let's consider the deed done --- the wine had gone to the young hero's head, loosening his tongue; and since everyone here was among friends, there was no need to fear strange ears.

Was this what you were after, lords?

"A pity! --- but right. We don't serve Eurystheus! We're the hands of the Thunderer, his earthly thunderbolts! We're --- I and Heracles... I mean, Heracles and I. And our service, our honorable and most perilous service..."

"--- is over," Eurytus finished calmly. "Twelve years, to the day. If Zeus is to be believed, Heracles's immortality is assured. But surely he'll go on living here too, on earth --- or am I wrong? Why shouldn't Heracles, the god-equal hero, son of Zeus, live a while longer --- and as wanax of Mycenae, no less?! And then withdraw to Olympus, leaving as wanax of Mycenae --- oh, let's say, Iolaus, his nephew and friend?"

"And what about Zeus?" Iolaus asked blankly, methodically filling every cup he could reach with wine.

Then he heaved himself out of his chair and, swaying, carried the kraters round to those present.

"And what about Zeus?!" he proclaimed once more for the whole megaron to hear, and raised the cup toward the soot-blackened ceiling beams as though it were a sacred rhyton[^49], draining it to the bottom an instant later.

[^49]: A sacred vessel for pouring libations to the gods.

The rest, willing or not, had no choice but to follow his example.

"And... what about Zeus, exactly?" Augeas inquired, his voice gone slightly hoarse, shivering as if from a chill.

"What do you mean, what?! Who, thirty-six years ago, proclaimed: 'Let the newborn be wanax of Mycenae and lord over all the Perseids!' He proclaimed it, Zeus, Dias the Father... about Eurystheus, mind you! So that's how it must be!"

"But Zeus meant Heracles, your own uncle, that's who!" Augeas flapped his plump little hands at Iolaus. "And Hera hurried along the labor of Nicippe, Eurystheus's mother, and that's how the little mistake came about! Who better than us to set it right?"

"Nothing will come of it," Iolaus cut him off, slamming his fist on the table for good measure. "Uncle Heracles will not sit in Mycenae!"

"But why not?!"

"Because I said so. And besides --- what business has he, a born hero, playing ruler? And then there are the fits of madness --- you've heard about those, I take it? Well, be glad you've only heard and never seen. I know my uncle well enough --- once it comes over him, all you can do is run for cover!"

"Well, all right," Eurytus summed up. "Heracles won't sit in Mycenae. But what about his brother --- and your father? Don't drink just yet, Iolaus, think it over, don't rush your answer... wine is a poor counselor."

Iolaus swayed heavily.

Knocked over his chair.

Made a guttural sound --- and stepped toward the door.

"A hero," Hippocoon's voice splashed quietly after him. "The gilded youth. And I did warn you, Eurytus..."

Hippocoon suddenly fell silent.

With a firm, even stride Iolaus walked to the door, and stood for a moment on the threshold of the megaron.

Turned around.

With a hard look, he swept his eyes over those gathered, as though seeing them for the first time.

"Rejoice, my lords!" Iolaus tossed out quietly. "Let's say I've only just now walked in. Let's say I never heard any of what you've said --- but thought of it all myself, on the way here. Well then, shall we start over?"

Only now did Laomedon the Trojan burst out laughing.

"You're a horse whisperer, Hippocoon," he called through his laughter to the Spartan, "fit to judge horses, not men! This fellow --- remember how he handled the whole lot of them, out in the courtyard? 'Halt! You heard me!' Walk back in, Iolaus Iphiclid, Heracles's driver! A driver, after all, is one who drives --- isn't he?"

"No," Iolaus answered seriously. "A driver is one whom fortune drives."

And he moved away from the door --- back.

Anew.
