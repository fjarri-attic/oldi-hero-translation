#### 6

Five men, none of them young, sat facing Iolaos, who had paused for a moment on the threshold of a rectangular, somewhat elongated megaron, its open forehall reached by a colonnade leading in from the palace courtyard.

Five lords.

An old acquaintance, Eurytos the marksman, basileus of Oichalia and the unofficial master of all Euboia; snow-white hair, caught at the brow by a band, falling thickly onto his shoulders, the tanned skin of his face gathered into a multitude of creases, bony hands lying heavy on the arms of his chair, the footstool cast aside as needless---even seated, Eurytos of Oichalia seems taller than all the rest, so that the basileus's sharp knees jut up indecently high, as though a grown man had, by mistake, sat himself down in a child's little chair.

That would be funny, if it concerned anyone else.

Another acquaintance, though a more recent one---the miser Augeias, fertile Elis clutched fast in his grasping little fist; a flabby, squat, balding man with an unhealthy complexion, whose soul was hardly cleaner than his famous stables. More than once the Eleian basileus had tried to prove his descent now from Helios, now from Poseidon---but all the Peloponnese remembered too well the brigand exploits of Augeias's true father, the dissolute Phorbas the Lapith, who with his cronies had robbed pilgrims on the roads of Phokis and once even set fire to the temple at Delphi, for which he was wounded by an arrow of Apollo.

Or, to put it more plainly---he fell gravely ill, but for some reason survived.

Beside the slouching Augeias sat the Spartan Hippokoon, who had seized power by force from his own brothers, Ikarios and Tyndareos, driving them out of Sparta. Iolaos was seeing Hippokoon for the first time, but the basileus's haughty, stony bearing, his lean, muscular torso, the scalding ice of his gaze---all of it betrayed at once a native of Lakonia, a man easier to kill than to persuade.

Though killing him, too, is no easy matter.

Laomedon the Trojan stood by the hearth, squinting absently into the heart of the fiery blossom. Crimson flickers wandered unsteadily across his pale, well-groomed face (Laomedon's beard grew poorly---or rather, did not grow at all), and Iolaos suddenly, sharply recalled that same crimson murk which the living are ill-advised to remember---the gloom of Hades, the heat-lightning of the Isles of the Blessed... Now the Trojan rubbed his hands together, turned, and nodded amiably to Iolaos---no, that was not how he had looked at him back then, when he drove his daughter's savior from Troy, trusting to the strength of his walls, and to the hope that Herakles was in a hurry to return to Mycenae after the campaign against the Amazons.

[Not here,]{.thoughts} Iolaos reminded himself.

And off at the very end, on a low stool, half-hidden by the back of Eurytos's chair and by one of the four columns ringing the megaron's hearth, perched Neleus, wanax of merchant Pylos.

Gray on gray.

A shadow, not a man.

Look, and you won't notice him.

Without rising, Eurytos took up from a small table---its curved legs ending in lion's paws unpleasantly reminded Iolaos of things past---two shimmering, chased kraters of wine, and raised his hands aloft, unmistakably intending one of the cups for his guest.

"Enter, O most worthy Iolaos, and join us with an open heart, pouring a libation in honor of god-equal Herakles, Slayer of Monsters!" the basileus of Oichalia proclaimed, in a voice too resonant for an old man's.

[Clever,]{.thoughts} Iolaos judged, approaching the table and, with a bow, taking the offered krater. [He didn't introduce anyone: that flatters them---who doesn't know you, my lords?... and me---walk right in, an equal among equals; and Herakles---see, despite the regrettable misunderstanding, we're pouring libations, singing his praises... So why didn't you send for Alkeides, then, instead of me?!]{.thoughts}

The wine scorched his throat in a spiced stream, the cup emptied---and Iolaos set it back down on the table.

Unobtrusively, he looked around.

"Was the road here from Tiryns a good one?" Even friendly words, in Augeias the Eleian's mouth, somehow came out sounding greasy. "Sit, sit, Iolaos, there's a chair free right there..."

"It was," Iolaos answered curtly, sitting down. "Praise the gods."

The lords exchanged glances.

"And the heroes," Eurytos added quietly, and his fingers, with a dry crack, closed tightly around the armrests.

"Now there's someone who deserves a low bow for our roads"---Augeias smoothed his sweaty bald patch with a palm---"and that's the heroes. And first of all---god-equal Herakles, best of men. Wiped out the monsters root and branch, brought the brigands to heel, centaurs and Lapiths---squashed flat... the ones still left are petty vermin, all snapping at one another, no time for us! Trade to your heart's content! Good neighbors: I give to you, you to me, they to us..."

"And everyone---to Mycenae," the blunt Hippokoon cut in suddenly, slapping his knee with a calloused palm for emphasis.

On his stool, the shadow of Neleus of Pylos stirred---had he meant to say something? No, he held his tongue---while the crimson flickers on the Trojan's face gathered themselves into a smile.

As if to say: not everyone---to Mycenae.

So bear that in mind, most honored ones.

"We're all friends here." Eurytos leaned forward sharply and all but sank his gaze into Iolaos's face. "And we speak as friends do, without beating around the bush."

[Go on, then, look all you like, archer,]{.thoughts} Iolaos smirked to himself (letting his face spread, just in case, into a smug grin---why, the great men had counted him as one of their own!). [You won't see much that I don't choose to show you... And still, what is it you want from me, rulers? To sing along in praise of Herakles?... As a sixth voice?]{.thoughts}

Apparently the basileus of Oichalia was satisfied with his inspection---for he settled back in his chair again and played his long, dry fingers in the air.

As though catching a fledgling.

"Why has your uncle Herakles, now that his service is over, not taken the Mycenaean throne in Eurystheus's place?" Eurytos the archer asked, gently, almost tenderly. "Everyone from Euboia to Pylos has been waiting for it... will we live to see it?"

And again Laomedon smiled.

From Euboia to Pylos---while we Trojans lie a good deal farther east...

[What am I to them?]{.thoughts} Iolaos reached for the krater, as though he'd forgotten the cup was empty. [Something like Lichas?... Little wit, plenty of scars, pride beyond his years; so you mean to bait me with a right of succession, my lords? If I told you what that right of mine is really worth---you'd choke on your wine!]{.thoughts}

"Hardly." He took up the round-bellied jug, painted with stylized octopuses, tilted it, and a crimson stream poured into the krater. "You know Herakles mostly from the tales of blind rhapsodes, while I've been at his heels since the cradle... A hero? Yes, a hero. Great? Yes, great. The great-est! But first and foremost, he is the son of Zeus!" For added emphasis, Iolaos jabbed his cup at the ceiling, splashing wine over himself. "The son of Zeus! Whatever the heavenly father commands, that he does! Will you say that's wrong?"

"No, it's right!" Augeias agreed hastily; the rest kept silent.

"And I say it's right, too! But sometimes---it's a pity." Iolaos drained the krater in one gulp, noting in passing the absence of servants in the megaron, and of any food on the table. Well then, let's call it done---the wine has gone to the young hero's head and loosened his tongue; and since everyone here is among friends, there's no need to fear prying ears.

Was this what you were after, lords?

"A pity!---but right. We don't serve Eurystheus! We're the hands of the Thunderer, his earthly thunderbolts! We're---I and Herakles... I mean, Herakles and I. And our service, our honorable and most perilous service---"

"---is over," Eurytos finished calmly. "Twelve years, to the day. If Zeus is to be believed, Herakles's immortality is assured. But surely he'll go on living here too, on earth---or am I wrong? Why shouldn't Herakles, the god-equal hero, son of Zeus, live a while longer---and as wanax of Mycenae, no less?! And then withdraw to Olympos, leaving as wanax of Mycenae---oh, let's say, Iolaos, his nephew and friend?"

"And what about Zeus?" Iolaos asked blankly, methodically filling with wine every cup he could reach.

Then he heaved himself out of his chair and, swaying, carried the kraters around to those present.

"And what about Zeus?!" he proclaimed once more for the whole megaron to hear, and raised the cup toward the soot-blackened ceiling beams as though it were a sacred rhyton[\*](#fn-49){.noteref}, draining it to the bottom an instant later.

The rest, willing or not, had no choice but to follow his example.

"And... what about Zeus, exactly?" Augeias inquired, his voice gone slightly hoarse, shivering as if from a chill.

"What do you mean, what?! Who, thirty-six years ago, proclaimed: 'Let the newborn be wanax of Mycenae and lord over all the Perseids!' He proclaimed it, Zeus, Dias the father... about Eurystheus, mind you! So that's how it must be!"

"But Zeus meant Herakles, your own uncle, that's who!" Augeias flapped his plump little hands at Iolaos. "And Hera hurried along the labor of Nikippe, Eurystheus's mother, and that's how the little mistake came about! Who better than us to set it right?"

"Nothing will come of it," Iolaos cut him off, slamming his fist on the table for good measure. "Uncle Herakles will not take the throne in Mycenae!"

"But why not?!"

"Because I said so. And besides---what business has he, a born hero, playing ruler? And then there are the fits of madness---you've heard about those, I take it? Well, be glad you've only heard and never seen. I know my uncle well enough---once it comes over him, all you can do is run for cover!"

"Well, all right," Eurytos summed up. "Herakles won't take the throne in Mycenae. But what about his brother---and your father? Don't drink just yet, Iolaos, think it over, don't rush your answer... wine is a poor counselor."

Iolaos swayed heavily.

Knocked over his chair.

Made a guttural sound---and stepped toward the door.

"A hero," Hippokoon's voice splashed quietly after him. "Gilded youth. I did warn you, Eurytos..."

Hippokoon suddenly fell silent.

With a firm, even stride Iolaos walked to the door, and stood for a moment on the threshold of the megaron.

Turned around.

Swept a hard gaze over those gathered, as though seeing them for the first time.

"Rejoice, my lords!" Iolaos threw out quietly. "Let's say I've only just now walked in. Let's say I never heard any of what you've said---but thought of it all myself, on the way here. Well then, shall we start over?"

Only now did Laomedon the Trojan burst out laughing.

"A horse-breeder, that's what you are, Hippokoon," he called through his laughter to the Spartan, "fit to judge horses, not men! This fellow---remember how he handled the whole lot of them, out in the courtyard? 'Halt! You heard me!' Walk back in, Iolaos Iphiklid, Herakles's driver! A driver, after all, is one who drives---isn't he?"

"No," Iolaos answered seriously. "A driver is one whom fortune drives."

And he set off from the door---back the way he had come.

Starting over.
