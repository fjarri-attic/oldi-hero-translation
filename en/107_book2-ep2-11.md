#### 11

Scared half to death, the people of Kos pressed close to their native cliffs and, backing away from the raging Iolaus, cursed under their breath the hereditary madness of the Perseids.

The oarsmen from the surviving ships looked a little more composed --- evidently ashamed to be caught behaving like backwater Koans --- but even they tried to keep their distance from Heracles's driver, whose head had been turned by the heat, by fury, by exhaustion, or by all three at once.

And indeed: Iolaus, like a predator caught in a hunter's net, paced back and forth by a tall crack in the rock face, sniffing the air, every so often wheeling sharply around and snarling with rage --- as though he hoped to catch someone hiding behind his back, and could never quite seize the right moment.

"S-scum!" Iolaus rasped, baring his teeth in a terrible snarl, and in that instant his face turned so hopelessly old that the unwilling onlookers flinched and silently called upon the gods --- whichever ones each of them preferred.

"Dr-r-romos! I can smell you --- you're here! Open up, filth!.. Open up! No you don't --- I'll break you open!.."

To Lichas, who stood closest of all, it truly began to seem that there was something by the crack, whose edges were overgrown with mold and whitish-green lichen: the air began to tremble like glass, a shifting haze wove threads that gleamed black... but the moment the boy looked more closely, it all vanished.

Just a cliff, and nothing more; just a crack, and nothing more.

Stone, mold, lichen.

The island of Kos.

"They took my boys," Iolaus suddenly whispered, wearily, scooping up full handfuls of grainy sand and pouring it over his disheveled head. "Took them! Both of them! Like horses... for the races... the races..."

His gaze suddenly cleared, filled with bronze resolve, swept over the gathered crowd, and fixed on Lichas.

The boy felt distinctly as though he were being grabbed by the front of his tunic, unable to do the one thing he wanted most of all --- run.

"The chariot!" Iolaus ordered.

Lichas knew at once what he meant: back in Troy, Iolaus hadn't been able to resist taking as plunder a chariot found in one of the palace storerooms --- a light thing, not a war chariot at all, built specially for racing, resembling a seashell mounted on wheels, with thin side rails curving together toward the center.

Waving to the nearest oarsmen, Lichas ran for the ship --- and before long the chariot, together with a full set of harness (after so much time around Iolaus, the boy's foresight knew no bounds), had been brought ashore.

"Horses!"

"What's got into you, Iolie?!" Lichas babbled anxiously. "Cool off, have a drink of water... where would horses come from? We didn't take any horses in Troy... loading the hoofed brutes on board --- no way, Iolie, it's just not possible!"

"I said --- horses! Lichas, explain to the locals: if a decent pair of horses can't be found on their thrice-blessed Kos, I'll harness whoever's handy!"

Lichas explained.

He tried very hard --- rightly suspecting whom the rabid Iolaus would harness first.

It turned out that a more or less decent pair of horses did exist on the island --- brought there once, for no clear reason, by the late basileus of the Koans, Eurypylus --- but the animals were to be sacrificed that very day to their former master, on his funeral pyre.

"I'll sacrifice them myself," Iolaus said quietly, though to many it seemed it would have been better if he'd shouted. "Myself! At once! To Eurypylus... to whoever you like! Well?!"

The Koans needed no second urging.

Less than an hour passed before the short, shaggy little horses were brought and harnessed.

Iolaus leapt into the chariot and began picking up speed almost from the water's edge, driving straight for the cliff.

The wheels sank in the sand, the horses strained themselves hoarse with frantic neighing, but neither the oarsmen from the ships nor the people of Kos could shake the feeling that the mad chariot with its mad driver was flying, hurtling, tearing along at such a speed that the eye had no time to take in the whole picture, catching only fragments: the horses' bared teeth, the driver rearing up, the whip cracking through the air, a whirlwind of sand at the wheel...

The chariot crashed into the crack in the rock and vanished.

Completely.

No wreckage, no crash of impact... nothing.

Just a cliff, and nothing more.

Stone, mold, lichen.

The island of Kos.

Only faint threads drifted through the air, like the remnants of a torn spiderweb; only a stuttering echo rippled out of the crack, as though hooves were striking stone, farther and farther away... farther... then silence.

"Is he a god?" one of the Koans asked Lichas, stunned.

"Worse," Lichas answered.
