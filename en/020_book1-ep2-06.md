#### 6

"...Mama! Come look! Ma-a-a-ma!..."

That is little Alkeides shouting, three and a half years old, erecting out of wet sand some structure as grandiose as it is nonsensical. He shouts ringingly and a touch crossly, because his mama is in no hurry to come; curly hair falls over his bulging forehead, his whole body from head to toe is smeared with mud, like that of a wrestler in the palaestra[\*](#fn-20){.noteref} after a long bout, and he bites his lower lip in exactly the same way Amphitryon does when absorbed in something.

Or perhaps it isn't Alkeides at all, but his brother Iphikles.

"Do-oggy! Eat, doggy, eat..."

That is little Iphikles muttering, three and a half years old. He is poking a finger into the mouth of a puppy recently born to their hunting bitch Procris, still half-blind and entirely unable to bark or understand that it is being played with. The puppy sucks at the finger, and Iphikles bursts into peals of laughter, then scoops up a handful of mud with his free hand, smears himself with it, and bites his lower lip in exactly the same way Amphitryon does when absorbed in something.

Or perhaps it isn't Iphikles at all, but his brother Alkeides.

Anything is possible...

Which forever irritates the nurses and amuses the household, all trying to work out---who is who? Whose belly aches---Alkeides's or Iphikles's? In the end both their bellies ache, and both refuse to drink the bitter medicinal draught, spitting and howling loud enough for the whole house to hear. Who tore mama's favorite peplos, the one aunt Nausikaa gave her---Iphikles or Alkeides? Both of them tore it, and none of the nurses can fathom how such sturdy-looking cloth could be torn so thoroughly to shreds after being left unattended for barely a minute!

They tried tying a braided cord to Alkeides's wrist---but that helped only up until the memorable business with the snakes. After that the cord invariably slipped off and got lost, or migrated to Iphikles's wrist entirely, or else both brothers took to proudly sporting matching cords, leaving the nurses thoroughly baffled.

So in the end both of them drank the vile medicine, and clothes were sewn for the two of them at once (however the restless pair managed to wear them out so quickly!), and swats to the backside were dealt out equally; and hardly any nurse would ever pat one scamp's head without immediately tousling the other's hair too---Zeus forbid she should shortchange one of them on affection!

Only Alkmene could tell the twins apart unerringly---but then again, she was their mother.

And every nurse, every slave or freedman, every free man who set foot in Amphitryon's house---in short, everyone took it for granted that little Alkeides received just a touch more of his mother's love. Just the tiniest drop, a crumb, a trifle...

Naturally: both were her own, her flesh and blood, but the elder was *His* son!

The nurses nodded knowingly, the slaves exchanged glances, the guests smiled---and Alkmene grew somber, catching the looks of the wise, god-fearing Thebans upon her. The daughter of the Mycenaean ruler Elektryon was afraid to admit even to herself that though she would give both her hands for her sons, it was the right hand that Alkmene would give for Iphikles, for the younger one, for the son of that grim, taciturn warrior whom she had chosen once and forever, whose chariot had thundered along the edge of a precipice, and in that thunder one name alone could be heard: "Alcme-ene"; and "Alcme-ene" the curved knife had hissed in a dark alley, slitting a rapist's throat; and her own name she read in Amphitryon's eyes every single night.

And her left hand, without a moment's hesitation, she would give for Alkeides, whom Alkmene had not once called Zeus's son aloud.

Both hands alike, like twins---yet not quite...

That is why Alkmene never once mistook one for the other, looking at her children.

"...Mama! Come! Ma-ama!..."

Alkmene knows it is Alkeides shouting.

"Do-oggy! Eat, doggy..."

Alkmene knows it is Iphikles muttering.

She sits on a little bench by the entrance to the gynaikeion, her hands occupied as usual with sewing, and her round, pretty face expresses nothing but calm and contentment. She has become very good at pretending lately---Alkmene, wife of Amphitryon.

Another patty of mud slaps down onto the work of art, the handiwork of the great architect Alkeides; the half-blind puppy crawls away from the pensive Iphikles (the finger he has just been feeding the puppy with is now in Iphikles's own mouth, and he is wholly absorbed in sucking it) and, waddling its hindquarters comically, draws near to the fortress of wet sand.

And little Alkeides gazes off into the distance, over the top of his structure, and deep in his eyes a black flame is slowly kindling; and young Iphikles seems to be listening to something, as though the rumble of fire in his brother's eye sockets had reached him too, making him frown in a way no child should.

The puppy crawls on, wagging its hindquarters... nuzzling its foolish little muzzle into the warm, muddy thigh that smells of home, of comfort, of peace...

They screamed almost simultaneously---Alkeides and Iphikles---only in Alkeides's cry rang out a strange triumph and the rumble of ancient depths, his scream flowing like lava breaking free and burning everything in its path; while in Iphikles's cry mingled a child's fright in a darkened room and the horror of an adult who has come face to face with the unknowable.

Alkeides screamed like a priest over a sacrifice laid out on the altar; Iphikles---like the sacrifice under the knife.

Then Alkeides's fingers, with a strength no child should have, clenched around the puppy, hoisting it up and at once slamming its back against the ground, and again, and again...

"Give him back! Do-oggy!..."

With scarcely less force Iphikles seized his brother by the arms, and both boys collapsed onto the fortress of wet sand, thrashing about in the mud, crushing beneath them the puppy, which whimpered so faintly it could barely be heard---and when the nurses came running and pulled the children apart, more than one of them found herself wondering: what kind of might did the young son of Zeus possess, if three grown women could barely hold him down---him, a child of three and a half?

Beside them Iphikles wept inconsolably, wiping his eyes with mud-caked palms and crying only the harder from the stinging under his eyelids; and over him stood the aged Eurytheia, the only one to think: [Three grown women could barely hold the thrashing, hysterical Alkeides down, but until we came running it was Iphikles holding him back---this one... or not?... Zeus the Cloud-Gatherer, which of them is which?!]{.thoughts}

At Eurytheia's feet lay the dead puppy, its spine broken; an innocent victim of an incomprehensible incident.

Tomorrow, hunters returning to Thebes from the direction of the Kadmeia will discover, in a grove dedicated to warlike Ares Enyalios, a large stone. The stone will turn out to have been hewn by someone into the shape of an altar---hewn hastily, in a rush---and on the altar-stone dried, caked blood will be plainly visible. Beside the stone the hunters will see the ashes of a burned-out fire---fresh ashes, from only yesterday---and in the ash will lie bones, blackened on the outside.

Human bones.

Word of this will reach basileus Kreon, and he will order an inquiry, but it will lead nowhere. All the free citizens will turn out alive and well, and if someone decided to sacrifice a slave to Ares or some other deity, well, that is his own private affair, and no one else's business.

The youngest of the hunters will pipe up that the old dwarf woman Galinthias, known to the whole city, the half-mad servant of Three-Formed Hekate, had been lurking near the grove---but he will be laughed down, basileus Kreon will shrug in bewilderment, and there the matter will end.
