#### 8

For the first time in his life, a passionate desire to become a god seized Alcides.

Hermeias's crepidae sandals, the featherlight tread of river naiads, the untamable sweep of Poseidon's whirlwind steeds, the shaggy wings of Boreas, the north wind --- anything at all, if only it would carry him there, to the right bank of the Cephissus, maimed by men, rolling gnarled trunks about in shreds of raging foam! Better pain, better death beneath the feet of men gone wild, forever proving to one another that they were mortal, than this helpless fury, when you are ready to howl with helpless frustration, standing in despair before the wrecked rope crossing, so carefully rigged the evening before!

His body burned mercilessly; the greasy mud was drying, coating his skin in a brittle crust of clay. His muscles begged for mercy, cramping as they remembered the sleepless night when, beneath the mocking patter of rain, three dozen young Cithaeronian shepherds, having crossed with Alcides to the left bank, fed the Cephissus, gorged on downpours, boulders and tree trunks. At the same time seventy Thebans sent to help, led by Iphicles (some of them known to the brothers from the palaestra), were building a short, slanting causeway forty cubits from the right bank, undercut well in advance --- for it was precisely there that the Cephissus ran considerably narrower.

By dawn Alcides felt as though a herd of drunken satyrs had trampled him half to death under their hooves. The shepherds, for their part, simply dropped where they stood, glancing with superstitious dread at the youth moving on at the same unbroken pace, two coals burning inhumanly in the hollow eye-sockets of a fearsome mask of clay.

Just before dawn, Iphicles made his way across to his brother along ropes strung between two trees and told him that most of the Thebans working under his command had left. They were to join the false vanguard, bait for the warlike Orchomenians, while the men remaining on the right bank were left to mind the props still holding back the artificially made landslide.

Only after hearing out his brother, no less spent than himself, did Alcides let himself fall, and lying there ordered the shepherds to cross by the rope bridge.

Iphicles had meant to stay, to help Alcides tip the last store of boulders and logs kept on the bank into the river at the right moment --- the props, he said, could very well be pulled without him. For a while the brothers argued, until it turned out they were arguing in silence, saving their breath. And in any case the argument had long since lost all meaning, because the ropes of the crossing had snapped, letting twenty Cithaeronians through and sending the twenty-first --- who never even cried out --- into the belly of the Cephissus, that serpent swollen with rain and violence.

Nine shepherds and the Amphitryad brothers found themselves cut off from the site of the coming battle --- to swim the maddened river would have been suicide. But there was no time left to grieve or rejoice, because the Orchomenian chariots were already forming up for an irresistible charge; the prop-posts were already shrieking as they slid across the sodden clay, turning the bank into a landslide, so that weariness had to be seized by the throat, to lash the rearing Cephissus across the valley in time!

Only afterward did the nine shepherds collapse face-down onto the safe left bank, looking little different from the corpses on the far side, while a wakened helplessness dealt each brother a scorching slap and burst into a hoarse laugh at the sight of ditch-diggers where there should have been warriors.

Without a word between them, the twins bolted in opposite directions. Iphicles snatched up the bow given him by Iphitus of Oechalia, along with three arrows that happened to be at hand, and dashed off upstream, hoping to catch the attention of the Theban baggage-train and get a crossing rigged. Alcides raced downstream, scanning for anything at all --- a miracle, a gift of fate, anything that would let him get across the Cephissus.

That was when he wanted to become a god.

The rain had stopped shortly before. Nephele, goddess of clouds, drove her heavenly cattle away, Helios's sun-crown flashed through the gaps, and Alcides at first did not believe his own eyes when he saw, ahead of him, an unfamiliar warrior sitting on the rim of a war chariot drawn by a pair of black stallions.

Silver lion heads on the warrior's greaves and on the hilt of his sword bared their teeth mockingly, as if reminding Alcides of his recent exploits on Cithaeron. Even without seeing the stranger's face, hidden beneath the close horsehair-crested helmet, the youth was somehow certain that he was smiling.

"Rejoice, brother!" the warrior's clear voice suddenly rang out, unexpectedly bright, from beneath the helmet.

"Who are you?" Alcides breathed, eyes darting about in search of anything that might serve as a weapon, not trusting this strange, spotless "brother" very far.

"I am Ares, son of Zeus and Hera," the answer was simple and unambiguous. "Look at yourself, and look at me, and you will understand at once that we are brothers."

Not a shadow of mockery lay hidden in the warrior's words, as though he considered his resemblance to the exhausted Alcides, crusted over with scaly mud, something entirely self-evident.

"I am Ares Enyalius, the god of war," the warrior repeated, pointing with a hand in a chased vambrace across the river, where Thebes and Orchomenus tore at each other with their teeth. "And this is my domain. Do you like it? Do you want to go there?"

"I want to! But can you... can you do it?!"

The warrior's laughter made the ribbed visor of his helmet shudder.

"Can I, really? The old, gorged Cephissus is no obstacle for my horses! The question isn't whether I can --- the question is whether you will want to!"

"Want to --- what?"

"Whether you will want to mount as driver onto the chariot of Ares the Furious?! Think, boy!"

Everything in Alcides screamed "yes!" but, forcing the words straining to break free back down his throat, a scent slid almost imperceptibly through the air, tickling his nostrils... the smell of mildew.

"As driver? On your chariot?" Alcides echoed distrustfully, trying not to look across the Cephissus, from where the life and death of his kinsmen called out to him. Ares Enyalius was not like Hermeias, or Pan, or merry Dionysus; and utterly unlike Chiron --- but what the difference actually was, he could not manage to grasp.

Ares took his hesitation for youthful uncertainty.

"Yes! I understood at once that you were like me, back there on Cithaeron, when you, boy, danced among six Minyans with a stranger's sword in your hand, prolonging the ecstasy of pleasure like a god! Alcides, brother and driver of Ares Enyalius --- you will not have to wait for decrepit, miserly Zeus to deign, or not deign, to grant you immortality! Hero, you shall become a god yourself, by wresting what you desire from the cowardly, base Family! You will race beside me through a hundred battles, a thousand battles; soldiers will drive their spears into an enemy's liver calling out to you, maidens will conceive boys calling out to you; remembering me, the grey-haired veteran and the youth donning his cuirass for the first time will not fail to remember you as well... and at last my cursed loneliness will end! Right now you strain for the far bank the way a weary traveler longs for home --- agree, Alcides, and war will become your home forever, beloved and desired... sacrifices are made to gods, boy --- and that great, grand sacrifice which mortals will never tire of making to me shall become yours as well! Be Ares's driver!.."

Ares fell abruptly silent.

He had expected anything at all, but not an expression of grief on Alcides's gaunt, suddenly aged face --- as though Enyalius's last words had been the final cup of hemlock offered to a criminal condemned to death.

"I'm sorry for you. You're mad, just as I am." The exhausted, filthy youth looked at the warrior in gleaming armor with unfeigned compassion, and for a moment it seemed to Ares that the visor of his helmet had turned transparent. "My poor... brother --- forgive me, but I need to find another crossing."

"Fool!" Ares leapt up, making the black stallions snort. "Pup! You don't want to be my driver? Fine, then you will be my slave! For you were born a hero, after all! Grubbing in the mud, the very mud you are so eager to sink into, you will remember me, and what you refused, when I go racing past you, laughing! Go, hero --- live, make war, gnaw --- for the glory of Ares!"

"If the thing you've all been turning me into since childhood is called a 'hero' --- believe me, I don't want to be one. If not to make war means not being a hero; if not rejoicing in victory means not being your slave, then I..."

"Oh, so that's how it is!" Ares was already standing on the chariot, gripping the reins. "Very well, I won't take advantage of a child's foolishness. Stand here, then, hero who wants to be neither hero nor god! Stand here and watch across the river; and if you should change your mind --- give me a sign! Ah, fool that I am... no wonder the Family calls you Cleaners! Watch, and think!"

A sudden gust of wind ruffled the crest of Ares's helmet, and the god drove his chariot across the Cephissus, toward the place where, in the mire, his priests and his sacrifices struggled.

Alcides watched him go, not noticing Iphicles running toward his brother, having failed to find so much as a single Theban wagoner on the far bank.

"A Cleaner?" It felt as though a crust of clay had lodged in his throat, and he still could not get his trembling hands to forget the boulders, the logs, the squelching mud. "Oh well --- it's true..."
