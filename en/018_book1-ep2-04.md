#### 4

...The heat had laid siege to seven-gated Thebes.

Helios, crowned white-hot, a skilled and merciless commander, had encircled the city with the blazing host of his rays, and every attempt by Aeolus, lord of the winds, to force his way into exhausted Thebes and refresh it with so much as the breath of Zephyrus came to nothing --- for the fierce northern Boreas the Warrior, had raced off on shaggy wings to Hyperborea, not troubling himself in the least over the fate of luckless Thebes.

The house of Amphitryon had not escaped the irritated Helios's attention either --- hardly surprising, since even great heroes suffer from the heat just like the lowliest of slaves, a fact that leads to rather bleak thoughts about universal equality. Silence reigned in every chamber, a sweat-soaked, languid silence; slaves, servants, and the household's own kin sought refuge in whatever unreliable shade they could find --- and only from the western chambers came the cheerful racket of children at play.

One corner of the room had been securely fenced off with four of Amphitryon's battle shields --- though no, the central one was a ceremonial piece, its face worked with an intricate relief of Zeus swallowing his first wife, Metis; in real combat such ornamentation would have been more hindrance than help --- and there, behind the shields, the eight-month-old twins Alcides and Iphicles crawled about, babbling, cooing, and working out their complicated business.

Children of great Amphitryon and chaste Alcmene.

Or, more precisely, children of divine Zeus, great Amphitryon, and chaste Alcmene.

A cause for gossip and rumor across all of Hellas.

Across from them, seated on a low kline, the aged nurse Eurythea nodded off. Though in fairness, the head of venerable Eurythea --- whose now-withered breast had once nursed no small number of worthy Thebans --- had over the past five years grown too heavy for her thin old neck and shook almost constantly, so one oughtn't blame her for excessive drowsiness on that account alone.

All the more so since the three younger nurses had long since fallen asleep, sprawled on mats along the wall, their sound, healthy slumber leaving any outside observer in no doubt whatsoever as to its genuineness.

"Gimme!" came from behind the shields, and through the gap flashed first a pink infant back, and then that remarkable part of the anatomy which mothers love to spank, not just in seven-gated Thebes. "Gi-i-i-imme!.."

The smack of a slap, a scuffle, indignant howls... silence.

Silence.

Who could blame the sleepers, when the breath of Hypnos the Sleep --- a god as dark and inescapable as his elder brother, the pitiless Thanatos the Death --- had crept unnoticed into the hot summer air?!

So it was Eurythea, swaying in half-sleep, who alone noticed some movement on the floor --- though the old woman was slow to grasp what it meant.

"Gi-i-i-imme!" came again from the fenced-off corner.

Silence.

Two oily, gleaming ribbons slid lazily from the threshold toward the shields, pausing now and then to lift narrow, triangular heads; they flowed soundlessly, innocent and terrible both, and the aged nurse watched them, indifferent at first, then --- as understanding dawned in her mind --- with mounting terror; and the scream born in her throat swelled and stuck, choking her breath, escaping only as a faint wheeze.

In her fright it seemed to Eurythea that the snakes were far larger than they truly were, that they were spawn of Acheron, the river of the underworld realm of the dead, that their scales flashed with the dread fire of the Abyss of Whirlwinds; and for the first time in years the old woman's head stopped shaking, frozen in place. "Zeus the All-Good," Eurythea thought she was screaming, though in truth her lips only moved without a sound, "our mother Athena Tritogeneia... the children!.. Children, children, chi—"

It was entirely unclear whether the old nurse was praying at all, and if so, to whom --- Zeus, Athena, or some strange children of her own imagining... Though then again, we are all someone's children --- Zeus, son of Kronus; Athena, daughter of Zeus; and the nurse Eurythea, daughter of the freedman Minyas the Lop-eared.

The commotion behind the shields ceased for a moment.

The two snakes twined into a single knot, and two heads, tongues flicking anxiously, slipped in one swift motion through the gap between the ceremonial shield and the plain battle shield bearing the image of a blazing sun; then just as quickly darted back out.

"Gi-i-imme!.."

Two chubby hands appeared in the gap. They snatched excitedly at the air with splayed fingers, squabbling, shoving each other aside, trying to catch the toy that had gotten away... Later, when Eurythea would tell the story for the hundredth time to gasping slaves and servants, she would hold her own hands out before her, pause, work her sunken lips, shake her head no, and take hold of a nearby slave woman's elbow with her left hand. That was how she would always show it: Eurythea's hand, and the slave woman's hand. Only no one would ever understand what the old woman, gone soft in the head, was trying to say by it --- and that was a pity, because it mattered.

Both hands were right hands.

...The ceremonial shield shuddered, rocked from within; the children's hands withdrew back behind the barrier, and the snakes' heads darted after them --- and just then one of the props gave way. Something creaked, cracked, the relief showing poor Metis being swallowed slid sideways, the edges of the two shields --- the heavy ceremonial one and the lighter battle shield --- snapped together like gigantic scissors, and the knot on the floor twisted into impossible coils, swelling with taut, springing strength...

And went slack.

When the central shield crashed down --- outward, thankfully --- Eurythea found the strength to scream.

While the sleepy young nurses were still rubbing their eyes and trying to work out what was happening, a frightened Alcmene had already burst into the room. Without slowing, she rushed to the children, dropped to her knees at a run, and began feeling the little ones over.

Bit by bit it dawned on her that nothing terrible had happened, that the children were alive and well, and that she could now turn around at her leisure and vent her feelings on the negligent nurses. She drew a deep breath, filling her lungs, which made Alcmene's already fine, full bosom finer and fuller still, cast one last glance at the children --- and saw what triumphant Alcides was holding in his hands.

All that carefully gathered breath went to waste, bursting out in a terrible shriek, which the nurses immediately joined. The women screamed, old Eurythea struggled to raise herself from the kline, young Alcides turned the two dead snakes over in his hands, gripping them by their broken necks and trying to stuff one of the heads into his mouth, while around him crawled Iphicles, red with indignation, howling at the top of his lungs:

"Gimme! A-a-ah... gi-i-imme!.."

Then all at once he calmed down, pulled a snake's tail out from under the fallen shield, and set about businesslike wrapping it around his brother's leg.

It was right about then that a disheveled Amphitryon appeared in the room, stark naked but sword in hand; five or six household servants ran in behind him, and less than an hour later all of Thebes knew what had happened --- with every last Theban holding an opinion of his own on the matter.

By evening, the most famous seer in all of Hellas arrived at Amphitryon's house --- the effeminate blind man Tiresias.

He was led into the now-notorious chamber, given the snakes lying on the floor by the wall to feel, and then brought before the children, seated in the nurses' arms.

"Snakes of Hera," Tiresias announced with an air of profundity, wiping on his linen chiton the finger with which he had just touched the snakes' fangs.

"Snakes of Hera!" people whispered around him meaningfully, and the blind man had the sense not to explain that these were nothing more than harmless rat snakes, the kind any slave woman content with her masters (or her own husband!) could buy at Hera's temple, buy and let loose to live beneath the house, dedicated to the goddess of the hearth.

It was believed this fostered prosperity and peace in the household.

"The boy will grow up to be a hero!" Tiresias jabbed a finger upward, considered whether to say "a great hero" instead, and decided not to skimp.

All the more so since he had once already prophesied much the same thing to Amphitryon.

"The greatest hero of Hellas!" Tiresias amended thunderously, and felt the back of his neck go cold. That happened to him rarely, only when a wave of true foresight washed over the blind man --- and he disliked those moments, disliked and feared them both, because truth paid poorly; and also because Tiresias was afraid, to the point of cramps, of stomach-twisting pain, of the future that opened up before him.

Already at the doorway, someone timidly touched Tiresias's shoulder.

"Forgive me, my lord," old Eurythea mumbled, barely audible, "it's about the boy... you said just now --- a hero, he'll be, you said... Which boy is it then, my lord?"

"That one there," Tiresias said, pointing behind himself, and moved on, led by his slave guide.

"That one?" the old woman repeated. "Which one is 'that one'? There are two of them!.. Two boys there are, my lord!.."

But no one was listening to her anymore.
