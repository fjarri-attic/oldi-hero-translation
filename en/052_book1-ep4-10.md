#### 10

"...Dad, dad! Praise... oh, praise whoever it is---you're awake, dad! Oh, dad, you should have seen it! Gods, heroes, centaurs, satyrs, some kind of nymphs too... Apollo himself was there, and Hermes, and Dionysos, and Pan, and... and even the Thunderer himself was with them!"

Automedusa decided to mention the Thunderer just in case, for good measure.

"Sweetheart... are you all right?" Alkathoos mumbled, eyeing his excited daughter doubtfully and trying to sit up. "Did they... hit you on the head too?"

Sitting up was not going well.

"Oh, but you didn't see a thing! Gods, straight from Olympos---and right away they set about saving me! And there was a hero with them, called Iphikles! He set me free with a club---everyone just scattered!..."

At the mention of the club, Alkathoos couldn't help touching his bald patch and wincing. Then he looked at his beaming daughter---no, alas, she was no Andromeda, for all Olympos to come to her rescue!---at the empty altar, at the meadow, plowed up every which way...

"Fine, so be it," he drawled, "who knows... and what did you say the hero's name was?"

"Iphikles, dad! He's such a hero, such a hero!..."

"Iphikles?" Alkathoos decided to leave the gods in peace and latch onto a more earthly subject, especially since after the blow he'd taken, the telestas's head wasn't at its sharpest. "Iphikles, is it... no, never heard of him!"

"What do you mean, never heard of him?!" Automedusa nearly burst into tears, offended both on her own behalf and on her rescuer's. "How could you not have heard of him?! Well, Iphikles, the hero... he also has a brother, Alkeides!"

"Alkeides?" Alkathoos marveled. "Alkeides of Thebes?! Son of Zeus and Alkmene, the future Slayer of Monsters?!"

"Oh, forget about him!" The girl jerked her sharp little shoulder scornfully. "Slayer of wine, that's what he is, your Alkeides! Kept babbling all sorts of nonsense too, the drunkard!... But Iphikles---now he's truly..."

And the girl's eyes clouded over dreamily.

"Are you sure you're not confusing them?" Alkathoos inquired hopefully, glancing back at the soldiers who were starting to come to. "It was Alkeides, the future hero---already a hero!---who saved you, while his brother was drunk and talking nonsense. Think again!"

An indignant outburst was his answer.

"I am not confusing anything! Honestly, dad, how can you be so dense! Iphikles saved me, Iphikles, Iphikles, not that horrid Alkeides at all! You just lay there and saw nothing, and I---"

"And she saw nothing either," a suspiciously familiar voice said nearby.

Father and daughter turned as one---and discovered the headman, who had crept up unnoticed, his right hand bandaged in a moderately filthy rag.

"You unwashed scum!" Alkathoos choked with indignation and began groping first at his belt, then at the grass around him.

"Here it is, my lord." The headman humbly held out the telestas's sword with a bow. "And here are your soldiers' spears."

Two embarrassed village lads shuffled up awkwardly and laid the spears beside the soldiers, then hastily backed away.

Alkathoos turned the weapon over in his hands, at a loss. Hacking down the headman under the circumstances would have been somehow in poor taste.

"We had a sign, my lord," the headman said meaningfully. "A sign that human sacrifice is displeasing to the gods. So we humbly ask your forgiveness---the gods set us straight!"

"Set you straight, all right." Automedusa giggled slyly. "No wonder your hand's bandaged... and your lads' faces are all bashed up!"

Alkathoos did not need to look long to see his daughter was right.

"So who did this to you, then?" he asked the headman and the lads huddled together behind the old man, more amicably now.

"Why, the sign!" the headman offered vaguely.

"And who plowed up the meadow with hooves, from end to end?"

"The sign." The old man sighed heavily. "All its doing, curse it... I mean, bless it!"

Washing down the tough, scorched mutton with lukewarm wine, Alkathoos decided not to touch on signs and gods any further, especially since the matter was settled. Buying cattle---the very purpose of his trip from Megara, where a ravenous army of stonemasons was raising new walls---had proved impossible after the plague. But he had managed to learn from the talkative headman that the plague hadn't reached the northern pastures, so...

"You had no business tagging along," he flung at his daughter. "None at all... I get back to Megara, I'll flay the soothsayer Empedokles alive! Lying scoundrel, told me the road would be a lucky one---and just look at it... Hyah, you shaggy-legged nags, get moving!"

The unlucky road carried the Megarian telestas's chariot ever farther from Megara and ever closer to the northern slopes of Kithairon, where the restless Amphitryads, exiled from Thebes, had settled.
