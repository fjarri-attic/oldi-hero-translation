#### 3

The harbor of Oropus was living its usual, day-in-day-out unchanging life: a many-voiced bustle seethed, slave-porters glistening with sweat scurried back and forth with bundles and baskets, sailors and captains off two galleys --- which had just nearly snapped each other's long oars --- cursed each other with real fervor; merchants hurried along their negligent servants and hired dockhands, pornai whores plied their charms without shame, and petty thieves slipped this way and that with the professionally harmless look of their trade.

"A Sidonian tub," Lichas identified unerringly, looking at one of the two quarrelsome galleys. "Just listen to them bawl, the stinking seafarers! Now there's a people I can't stand..."

"I wonder, what have you got against them?" Iolaus smirked, scanning the bay from under the shade of his hand.

"Their writing, that's what! You, Iolie, taught me their filthy script --- yelling and cuffing me round the head; Alcides taught me --- a smack for every 'aleph'; Iphicles taught me too... no, I don't like Sidonians!"

Struck by such logic, Iolaus only shook his head, and they walked on along the waterfront.

A little further on, a twenty-oared merchant ship was being loaded in haste at the pier. Nearby on the shore lay a heap of mismatched bundles, sacks, and bunches of sharp-smelling roots, while dockhands scurried briskly across the gangplanks, hauling all this cargo aboard.

The one thing the dockhands gave a wary, wide berth was a cart bearing four enormous clay pithoi, each nearly as tall as a man. The dockhands could hardly be blamed: every one of these giant pithoi weighed no less than four talents, there was no rolling them up the gangplanks, and carrying the wretched things two or three men at a time was a fine way to tumble off the boards --- and no one wanted to answer for the loss of the goods.

Their owner --- a red-faced merchant built like a bear, in a chiton soaked with grease --- was beside himself: he stamped his feet and made threats, but it was all in vain.

"Do you even know, you scoundrels, who this cargo is meant for?! This wine was ordered by the basileus of Oechalia himself, the glorious Eurytus, for his festival feast! Gods, these pithoi have to be on Euboea by noon, come what may!"

"They will be," the merchant heard a confident bass voice right by his ear, and a heavy hand came down on his shoulder, pressing the fat man rather firmly toward Mother Gaia. "Come what may --- so they will be."

The merchant's first thought, as he turned, was: "Seems I did overdo it with the wine yesterday after all!... and then again, out in the sun since dawn..."

His eyes were plainly playing tricks on him. Standing before him were two utterly identical hulking men, hairy and bearded, both smirking the very same smirk, both tilting their heads toward the right shoulder at the very same moment. Even the old scars on their arms and legs looked, as far as he could tell, identical.

"Will you take us aboard?" the one on the left asked without preamble; the one on the right said nothing --- and the merchant let out a breath of relief: Bacchic frenzy and sunstroke, it turned out, had nothing to do with it after all.

"Haul the pithoi aboard, and I'll take you!" he hastened to assure the brothers (there was no doubt of that left in him now).

"Then it's a deal," nodded the one on the right. "Hey, you," he shouted to the dockhands, "come on, drag those planks over here! Those look sturdier... Come on, you layabouts, get moving!"

A crowd of genuine layabouts had already begun gathering at the pier, eager to gawk at the curious spectacle. It did not escape Lichas and Iolaus that one man, in good but dusty clothes, had stepped a little to one side and was watching, with feigned indifference, first the brothers setting up the extra gangplanks, then the knot of onlookers.

"Do you know him?" Lichas pointed Iolaus toward this odd spectator.

Iolaus only shrugged.

At last the newly hired workers set up the gangplanks to their own liking, and hopped on them a few times to test how sturdy they were.

"You'd do better jumping with the pithoi on your shoulders," someone in the crowd advised snidely.

"Mm-hm," the brothers grunted in agreement, and headed for the cart.

Those gathered on the shore smiled with malicious glee, savoring the fun to come.

And they weren't wrong to! --- the brothers tried lifting one of the pithoi, first grabbing the side handles, then wrapping their arms around its middle; then they shook their heads ruefully and, gripping the side of the cart, slowly dragged the loaded thing closer to the gangplanks.

All around, whistles and jeers went up. "There you go, proper donkey work, that!" someone in the front row called out, and barely managed to jump back in time, as the cart's wheel was just about to run over his foot; right at the gangplanks the cart lurched, catching in a rut, and the nearest pithos rolled toward the edge to the guffaws of the onlookers and the wailing of the hapless merchant...

One of the brothers slid a callused palm under the vessel's base, gripped a handle with his other hand, and simply walked off up the gangplank, holding the pithos pressed to his shaggy chest.

"You mustn't shake good wine about," he explained in passing to the merchant, who stood frozen with his mouth hanging open. "The sediment... and Dionysus takes offense."

And, reaching the middle of the boards, he hopped twice, landing with surprising softness.

The wood creaked but held; the merchant-owner creaked too, but held --- and soon the first pithos stood on deck.

By then the second brother was making his unhurried way up the gangplank as well --- though, unlike the first, he carried his pithos on his shoulder, the way slave women carry water jugs.

The merchant watched, stunned, his slack lips flapping soundlessly, like a fish pulled out of the water.

"Zeus Brotoloigos, send me workers like that!" the fat man muttered at last. "What would it cost you, Great One?"

"Dream on!" a boyish voice sang out behind him. "Your name wouldn't happen to be Eurystheus, would it?"

"No," the merchant answered automatically, and, turning, found right beside him an insolent, pimply youth grinning a gap-toothed grin straight into his face.

"And who might you be?!" the merchant, having recovered himself, rounded on the impudent boy --- but caught himself, remembering whose name the boy had just spoken. And remembering, too, just what sort of "worker" had spent twelve years in service to the Mycenaean wanax Eurystheus.

The fat man's crimson face went pale as if dusted with flour, but no one in the crowd paid it any mind.

"Why, they're off to Euboea with the suitors!" some wit shouted. "Going to court Iole! And instead of arrows at the target, they'll be flinging pithoi! Eurytus the archer won't know what hit him!"

"Now there's an idea," one of the athletes called back, still short of the gangplank. "Especially since a target like your backside, even a pithos couldn't miss."

And he gave his burden a little swing, plainly meaning to test which was harder: the pithos, or that windbag's skull.

The crowd flinched back, the merchant squeezed his eyes shut, mentally calling on all the gods at once --- he couldn't care less about the wit's skull, but the wine, the precious wine!... --- and just then a familiar boyish voice offered some advice:

"You'd better cool off, Alcides... you never know, your hand might slip, and here I am standing right next to you!"

"I'm not Alcides, I'm Iphicles," the athlete grunted over his shoulder, and set the last pithos down on deck. "You're always butting in, Lichas!... I only meant to give them a scare..."

"Lying, I bet," Lichas declared confidently, and the merchant warily cracked his eyes open. "About being Iphicles, about 'giving them a scare,' and about me always butting in."

"Want a clip round the ear?" the worker inquired, and reached for the pithos again.

"Nuh-uh!" Lichas hastily jumped back. "Now I believe you're Iphicles. Alcides never threatened to clip me round the ear!"

"He'd just up and do it!" Iolaus and the second worker, who had come up by then, finished together in chorus.

Lichas took offense and fell silent.

* * *

...By the time the baggage from Iolaus's chariot had been carried aboard and the ship had finally cast off, with the four passengers comfortably settled at the stern --- next to the steering oar and the helmsman, who was deaf to other people's conversations --- Iolaus looked the brothers straight in the eye; between the two of them, their personal property now amounted to one ash club and one heavy bow apiece, and as for the rest, it was better not to bring it up.

"Blown through everything again on the road, you good-for-nothings?" he asked sternly.

The twins, as one, as though on command, stared down at the planked deck; then, unable to bear the strained silence, shifted their eyes to the rowers' bare, gleaming torsos, as though hoping to spot the answer to the question there.

"Wine? Women?" Iolaus was implacable. "Heroic feats?"

"Dice," Iphicles muttered under his breath, tugging mercilessly at his own beard. "Some scoundrel, back in Nauplia... though he played, he played --- like a god!"

Alcides only sighed ruefully and clenched and unclenched his fists several times, as though he were strangling someone.

"I didn't... I mean, you two weren't whipped enough as children!" Iolaus snapped in exasperation, stumbling slightly midway through the sentence. "Fine, I'll teach you to play dice myself. It's downright shameful --- when it comes to breaking necks, you've got skill to spare, but a simple thing like this..."

The brothers nodded, embarrassed, and Iolaus went on grumbling for a good while --- and only as they moored at the deeply indented, bay-cut coast of Euboea did he remember that he'd meant to leave that rascal Lichas behind in the harbor of Oropus.

After which he went on grumbling for a good while more.
