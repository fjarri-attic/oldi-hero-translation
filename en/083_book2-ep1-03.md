#### 3

The harbor of Oropos was going about its usual life, unchanged from one day to the next: a many-voiced bustle seethed, slave-porters glistening with sweat scurried back and forth with bundles and baskets, sailors and captains of two galleys---which had just nearly snapped each other's long oars---were cursing furiously; merchants hurried along their negligent servants and hired dockhands, pornai whores plied their charms busily, and petty thieves slipped this way and that with the professionally harmless look of their trade.

"A Sidonian tub," Lichas pronounced with unerring certainty, looking at one of the two quarrelsome galleys. "Just listen to them bawl, the stinking seafarers! Now there's a people I can't stand..."

"And what, I wonder, have you got against them?" Iolaos smirked, scanning the bay from under the shade of his hand.

"Their writing, that's what! You, Iolie, tried to teach me their filthy script---yelling and cuffing me upside the head; Alkeides tried---a smack for every 'aleph'; Iphikles tried too... no, I don't like Sidonians!"

Struck by such logic, Iolaos only shook his head, and they walked on along the waterfront.

A little further on, a twenty-oared merchant ship was being loaded in haste at the pier. Nearby on the shore lay a heap of mismatched bundles, sacks, and bunches of sharp-smelling roots, while dockhands hurried across the gangplanks, hauling all this cargo aboard.

The one thing the dockhands warily steered clear of was a cart bearing four enormous clay pithoi, each nearly as tall as a man. The dockhands could hardly be blamed: every one of these giant pithoi weighed no less than four talents, there was no rolling them up the gangplanks, and carrying the wretched things two or three men at a time was a fine way to tumble off the boards---and no one wanted to answer for the loss of the goods.

Their owner---a red-faced merchant built like a bear, in a grease-stained chiton---was beside himself: he stamped his feet and made threats, but it was all in vain.

"Do you even know, you scoundrels, who this cargo is meant for?! This wine was ordered by the basileus of Oichalia himself, the glorious Eurytos, for his festival feast! Gods, these pithoi have to be on Euboia by noon, come what may!"

"They will be," the merchant heard a confident bass voice say right by his ear, and a heavy hand came down on his shoulder, pressing the fat man rather firmly toward Mother Gaia. "Come what may---so they will be."

The merchant's first thought, as he turned, was: [Seems I did overdo it with the wine yesterday after all!... and on top of that, out in the sun since dawn...]{.thoughts}

The merchant was plainly seeing double. Standing before him were two utterly identical hulking men, hairy and bearded, both smirking the very same smirk, both tilting their heads toward the right shoulder at the very same moment. Even the old scars on their arms and legs looked, as far as he could tell, identical.

"Will you take us aboard?" the one on the left asked without preamble; the one on the right said nothing---and the merchant let out a breath of relief: Bacchic frenzy and sunstroke, it turned out, had nothing to do with it after all.

"Haul the pithoi aboard, and I'll take you!" he hastened to assure the brothers (of that there was no longer any doubt).

"Then it's a deal." The one on the right nodded. "Hey, you," he shouted to the dockhands, "drag those planks over here, quick! Those look sturdier... Come on, you layabouts, get moving!"

A crowd of genuine layabouts had already begun gathering at the pier, eager to gawk at the curious spectacle. It did not escape Lichas and Iolaos that one man, in good but dusty clothes, had stepped a little to one side and was watching, with feigned indifference, now the brothers setting up the extra gangplanks, now the knot of onlookers.

"Do you know him?" Lichas pointed the odd spectator out to Iolaos.

Iolaos only shrugged.

At last the newly hired workers set up the gangplanks to their own liking, and hopped on them a few times to test how sturdy they were.

"You'd do better jumping with the pithoi on your shoulders," someone in the crowd advised snidely.

"Mm-hm," the brothers grunted in agreement, and headed for the cart.

Those gathered on the shore smiled with malicious glee, savoring the fun to come.

And sure enough!---the brothers tried lifting one of the pithoi, first grabbing the side handles, then wrapping their arms around its middle; then they shook their heads ruefully and, gripping its side, slowly dragged the loaded cart closer to the gangplanks.

All around, whistles and jeers went up. "That's it---just the job for donkeys!" someone in the front row called out, and barely managed to jump back in time, as the cart's wheel was just about to run over his foot; right at the gangplanks the cart lurched, catching in a rut, and the nearest pithos slid toward the edge to the guffaws of the onlookers and the wailing of the hapless merchant...

One of the brothers slipped a callused palm under the vessel's base, gripped a handle with his other hand, and simply walked off up the gangplank, holding the pithos pressed to his shaggy chest.

"Good wine shouldn't be shaken up," he explained in passing to the merchant, who stood frozen with his mouth hanging open. "The sediment... and Dionysos takes offense."

And, reaching the middle of the boards, he hopped twice, landing with surprising softness.

The wood creaked but held; the merchant-owner creaked too, but held---and soon the first pithos stood on deck.

By then the second brother was making his unhurried way up the gangplank as well---though, unlike the first, he carried his pithos on his shoulder, the way slave women carry water jugs.

The merchant watched, stunned, his slack lips flapping soundlessly, like a fish pulled out of the water.

"Zeus Brotoloigos, send me workers like that!" the fat man muttered at last. "Is that so much to ask, Great One?"

"Dream on!" a boyish voice sang out behind him. "Your name wouldn't happen to be Eurystheus, would it?"

"No," the merchant answered automatically, and, turning, found right beside him an insolent, pimply youth grinning a gap-toothed grin straight into his face.

"And who the hell are you?!" Recovering himself, the merchant rounded on the impudent boy---but stopped short, remembering whose name the boy had just spoken. And remembering, too, just what sort of "worker" had spent twelve years in service to the Mycenaean wanax Eurystheus.

The fat man's crimson face went pale as if dusted with flour, but no one in the crowd paid it any mind.

"Why, they're off to Euboia with the suitors!" some wit shouted. "Going to court Iole! And instead of arrows at the target, they'll be flinging pithoi! They'll out-throw Eurytos the archer in no time!"

"Now there's an idea," one of the athletes called back, still short of the gangplank. "Especially since even a pithos couldn't miss a target like your backside!"

And he gave his burden a little swing, plainly meaning to test which was harder: the pithos, or that windbag's skull.

The crowd flinched back, the merchant squeezed his eyes shut, mentally calling on all the gods at once---he couldn't care less about the wit's skull, but the wine, the precious wine!...---and just then a familiar boyish voice offered some advice:

"You'd better cool off, Alkeides... you never know, your hand might slip, and here I am standing right next to you!"

"I'm not Alkeides, I'm Iphikles," the athlete grunted over his shoulder, and set the last pithos down on deck. "You're always butting in, Lichas!... I only meant to give them a scare..."

"Lying, I bet," Lichas declared confidently, and the merchant warily cracked his eyes open. "About being Iphikles, about 'giving them a scare,' and about me always butting in."

"Want a smack upside the head?" the worker inquired, and reached for the pithos again.

"Nuh-uh!" Lichas hastily jumped back. "Now I believe you're Iphikles. Alkeides never threatened to smack me upside the head!"

"He'd just up and do it!" Iolaos and the second worker, who had come up by then, finished in chorus.

Lichas took offense and fell silent.

...Once the baggage from Iolaos's chariot had been carried aboard, the ship had finally cast off, and the four passengers were comfortably settled at the stern---next to the steering oar and the helmsman, who was deaf to other people's conversations---Iolaos fixed a hard stare on the brothers, whose personal property now amounted to an ash club and a heavy bow apiece; as for the rest, it was better not to bring it up.

"Blown through everything again on the road, you good-for-nothings?" he asked sternly.

The twins, as if on command, both stared down at the planked deck; then, unable to bear the strained silence, shifted their eyes to the rowers' bare, gleaming torsos, as though hoping to spot the answer to the question there.

"Wine? Women?" Iolaos was implacable. "Heroic feats?"

"Dice," Iphikles muttered under his breath, tugging mercilessly at his own beard. "Some scoundrel, back in Nauplia... though he played, he played---like a god!"

Alkeides only sighed ruefully and clenched and unclenched his fists several times, as though he were strangling someone.

"I should've whipped you---I mean, you two weren't whipped enough as children!" Iolaos snapped in exasperation, stumbling slightly midway through the sentence. "Fine, I'll teach you to play dice myself. It's downright shameful---when it comes to breaking necks, you've got skill to spare, but a simple thing like this..."

The brothers nodded, embarrassed, and Iolaos went on grumbling for a good while---and only as they were putting in at the bay-riddled coast of Euboia did he remember that he'd meant to leave that rascal Lichas behind in the harbor of Oropos.

After which he grumbled for a good while all over again.
