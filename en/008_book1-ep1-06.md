#### 6

Alcmene told her husband nothing --- but the slave women's tongues knew no bounds.

That night Amphitryon was especially tender with his wife, and afterward slept fitfully, waking again and again to touch Alcmene with his hand --- as though he feared she might vanish at any moment, no one knew where.

And in the morning he ordered two black cows led to the temple of Paean, there to be sacrificed to the healer god and the sister Pharmakides; and one more cow --- to the temple of Zeus Kronion.

Why Zeus? Just in case...

It's just that Amphitryon really disliked these cursed incidents that had been dogging his wife since that memorable night.

Ah, gods, gods... what is human life to you? In passing you exalt, in passing you trample --- and it's hard to say which is worse...

This thought tormented Amphitryon on the very day when Alcmene, accompanied by a hulking Ethiopian slave, set out for the palace of basileus Creon, which rose in the southern part of Thebes. She was going, naturally, not to Creon himself but to his wife Nausicaa --- a plump, ever-giggling woman of an utterly unregal (though very sweet) appearance. Like their husbands, Alcmene and Nausicaa had grown close almost at once, and the latter often invited her friend over, especially whenever new fabrics were brought in --- Alcmene could unerringly tell which cloth would best suit frivolous Nausicaa, who had already borne her Creon a whole flock of daughters.

There was nothing unusual about such a visit, nor about the women lingering late over gossip and fittings, but Amphitryon's heart had been ill at ease these past few days, and when dusk began to fall, he could bear it no longer. There was only one road from his house to Creon's palace (short of wandering through filthy back alleys, which Alcmene would hardly have risked), so Amphitryon made himself ready in short order and went out into the thickening dusk, meaning to meet his wife.

And soon, from around a bend, coarse laughter reached him, men's voices --- and almost at once, Alcmene's voice, angry and frightened:

"What are you doing?! Don't you understand who I am?!"

Amphitryon quickened his step and regretted not having brought a weapon.

There were six of them. And they could have been called anything under the sun, except respectable citizens. "Even if they were respectable..." Amphitryon thought bitterly, nearly stumbling over the corpse of the Ethiopian slave, his skull split open. Some twenty paces off, Alcmene was pressed against a fence, the men surrounding her, egging each other on with lewd little jokes. They were clearly in no hurry.

A little further off stood another man --- not so much tall as tremendously broad-shouldered, no longer young, with a curly, greying beard. He was wrapped in a long, aquamarine cloak, and seemed altogether uninvolved in what was happening.

Simply stood there, watching...

And gave a low whistle when Amphitryon appeared.

The men turned and stared at the newcomer.

"Wh-who's this now?" one of them muttered.

"I am Amphitryon the Exile," Amphitryon deliberately gave the old nickname by which he'd been known in Thebes, back when he'd come to summon basileus Creon against the Teleboans with nothing behind him but a young wife, a heavy spear, and a fearsome reputation. "And this is my wife..."

And at the top of his voice, in all its brazen ring that had more than once drowned out the din of battle:

"Out of the way, you bastards!"

The rapists flinched back at first, then stopped, exchanging glances, and one of them stepped forward --- a thick-necked brute with a hooked nose and small, sleepy, close-set eyes. From his left eye a scar ran down, like an earthworm, losing itself in his stubble.

Swaying, the leader moved toward Amphitryon.

A curved Thracian knife jutted from the brute's belt, but he did not draw it, simply thrusting forward his knotted hand, like a tree root, and grabbing Amphitryon by the chiton at his chest.

"A hero," the thick-necked man grunted glumly, and the worm on his cheek writhed lazily. "Look, brothers, that's a hero... more like a zero..."

And he let out a shrill howl when Amphitryon's fingers, hard as wood, struck him across the eyes: he howled and threw his hands to his face, then wheezed, gurgled, sinking to the ground, grinning horribly with a new mouth carved beneath his chin.

No one even noticed how the curved knife had migrated from the brute's belt into Amphitryon's hand, washing its former owner's throat in a bronze splash before darting aside at once, crunching into the collarbone of the quickest of them, who had swung up a short club.

The rest flinched, bustled, backed away behind the broad-shouldered owner of the aquamarine cloak --- but backed away oddly, as if not even noticing him --- and quickly dissolved into the evening gloom.

The broad-shouldered man stepped toward Amphitryon, watching him with fastidious interest --- and Amphitryon struck, short and skillful, putting into the blow all his experience and a savage pain that made his heart pound, dry and relentless.

The ground proved surprisingly hard.

Springing to his feet, Amphitryon struck again, feeling his legs refusing to obey him, his ribs crying out for mercy, his head filling with murky twilight with no hope of a coming dawn.

He struck, not understanding what he was doing.

And landed it, splitting his knuckles to blood, as though he had struck a cliff.

After which the ground shoved Amphitryon in the back of the head once more.

...The broad-shouldered man stood over him, thoughtfully holding his cheek, and in his piercing eyes contempt mingled, inexplicably, with astonishment. That, no doubt, is how a wolf looks at a mad little kid goat that has sunk its blunt teeth into the fierce beast's flank.

"Your wife has hairy thighs," the broad-shouldered man said indifferently, so indifferently that it was at first hard even to take it for an insult. "Like a satyr's. You're a woodlouse, hero... Slime."

And then Amphitryon understood --- it was over. The time had come to die. Only he could not die, because Alcmene was still pressed against the fence; he could not die, and he forced himself up onto his knees --- kneeling before the broad-shouldered man was humiliating, but somehow not very much --- then onto one knee, then...

Then, from somewhere in the darkness, from behind the broad-shouldered man, a voice rang out. A sly, almost boyish, and very familiar voice --- only the fog in his head kept Amphitryon from placing where he had heard it before.

"What, uncle, decided to play the hero too?" the voice asked. "Doesn't really suit you, though..."

"You'd better get lost, Dummy!" the broad-shouldered man rumbled. "Keep your nose out of business that isn't yours, little nephew!"

"I will," the voice agreed, almost too readily. "And I think I'll get lost too. I'll get lost very quickly. One might even say I'll fly. To dad. Who's been in a foul mood since this morning, angry as Typhon... So, uncle, shall I be going?"

The broad-shouldered "uncle" did not answer. His face twisted in a grimace of fury visible even in the dark; he exhaled sharply and, turning on his heel, strode off. His heavy footsteps seemed to shake the earth, and were a long time fading into the darkness.

For an instant Amphitryon fancied he saw before him a hazy little figure in a hooded chlamys --- and he remembered, remembered sharply and painfully, how that same voice had, not so long ago, warned him of the need to hold his tongue...

He groaned and got to his feet.

He was ashamed to look at his wife.

But Alcmene gazed at her hero husband with adoration --- her warrior, her man, who would always save her, always protect her, always, always...

She had not seen the broad-shouldered man, had not heard the strange exchange; for her, it had all ended with the thick-necked brute's death --- his last opponent had never stood over Amphitryon, had never walked away afterward, unbeaten but suddenly stooped...

None of it had happened for her --- as though someone had stolen a handful of minutes from her life.

Except that a familiar hooded chlamys had flickered in the darkness, so vividly reminiscent of the thief from before, the one who had poisoned himself on stolen meat.

...And Amphitryon avoided his wife's eyes and trudged along on buckling legs, his head hung low. For the first time in many years, he had been defeated. And now they were walking home only thanks to an unknown protector, toward whom Amphitryon felt a distinct dislike --- though he tried with all his might to feel gratitude instead.

The bitterness of defeat, alas, grew no sweeter for his thinking of the number of enemies he had felled, nor for the thought that one of them was, perhaps, not quite human.

If it were to be played over, Amphitryon knew, he would strike him again.

"O gods," Amphitryon muttered hollowly, "what for?.."

The question was foolish and naive; he understood that perfectly well, but could not help asking it.

"We must go to Delphi," Alcmene answered, her voice breaking, quietly supporting her swaying husband. "We must ask the Pythia in the sanctuary of Apollo. It's the last thing left to us."

Against his will, Amphitryon pictured Apollo wearing his own shape, in Alcmene's bed --- and understood that he did not want to go to Delphi.

Absolutely.
