#### 7

Proud Orchomenus seethes, foaming over its brim...

The return of the mutilated envoys provoked an outburst of fury among the Minyans matched, perhaps, only by the eruption of a volcano beneath whose crater the lame god Hephaestus fans his forge. The hottest and emptiest heads, along with the relatives of the injured, called for marching on Thebes at once, putting the Thebans to the sword to the last man, and razing the city and burning it, sowing the ruins with salt so that not even a thistle would grow where seven-gated Thebes had stood.

To the credit of Erginus, basileus of Orchomenus and son of Clymenus, it must be said that his head was neither hot nor empty. The shouting and the calls to arms troubled Erginus little, even though his own nephew Proclus was now called Proclus the One-Armed and shouted loudest of all.

War is inevitable, and even useful in its way. That much is true. But so serious a business demanded careful preparation --- the gods' blessing, again, and finding out what the enemy was plotting... nor was it out of the question that Thebes, like an overreaching puppy, would lose its nerve at the last moment and agree once more to whatever terms Orchomenus set, as had happened before --- only this time a hundred cows a year would not cut it!

The priest of Ares, having made an offering to warlike Enyalius, reported to Erginus that Ares blessed the valiant Orchomenians for the coming battle. "And what else is one to expect from the god of war?" the prudent Erginus shrugged to himself; all the more so since the other gods remained stubbornly silent, neither answering nor heeding the priests' prayers.

The entrails of a black ram smoked to no purpose; the flight of birds only confused the diviners --- and so the basileus, while preparing for war, was in no hurry to march.

Soon the scouts brought news from Thebes. The seer Tiresias the blind proclaimed to all and sundry that the heralds of insolent Orchomenus had insulted the Thunderer himself, and had therefore been justly punished by Alcides. Orchomenus seethed harder still; Erginus the basileus shrugged once more --- what, after all, could one expect of Tiresias, a native of Thebes and a local patriot? --- then ordered the blind man's inventions declared deceitful Theban propaganda, and went on unhurriedly preparing for war.

All the same, Erginus personally interrogated, none too gently, all the returned envoys about any blasphemous remarks. They swore up and down that Zeus had absolutely nothing to do with it, though they oddly kept looking away as they said so. So in the end Erginus let the cripples go, then took counsel with his war-commanders --- the red-bearded giant Thesticles and the grim one-eyed Pterelaos, nicknamed the Cyclops --- and decided to hold off marching until the enemy made some definite move.

The commanders reasoned soundly enough: first, that if the Thebans, after everything, went so far as to invade Orchomenian land, then even a supposedly insulted Zeus (to say nothing of the other Olympians) would deem war the only possible answer; and second, that northeast of Orchomenus, in the floodplain valley by the bend of swift-rushing Cephissus, the Minyans' heavy chariots would hold a clear advantage over the numerous Theban infantry.

Besides, why drive the horses all the way around Cithaeron, if the Thebans would come themselves to meet their death?

On the suggestion of one-eyed Pterelaos the Cyclops, a detachment of six dozen seasoned warriors was sent to Cithaeron --- to slaughter the Theban shepherds, drive off the herds, and, if possible, seize the bastard Alcides, should he still be loitering on the northern slopes.

The detachment left and did not return.

Erginus very much wanted to think it simply hadn't returned *yet*.

And a week later, the scouts reported that the Theban army had marched on Orchomenus.

Before long, having gone downstream along the Cephissus and made a small detour by the Eastern Pass (Erginus understood well enough that this was most likely where Amphitryon had picked up his cursed offspring), the Thebans made camp in the southern foothills of Cithaeron --- right at the entrance to the very valley Erginus and his lawagetai had marked out for the decisive battle.

Learning where the Theban camp lay, basileus Erginus reflected that the gods, after all, were not so ill-disposed toward Orchomenus.

Early in the morning, when the sky, wholly overcast, had only just begun to lighten in the east, the Minyans marched out of the city.

In front, along a road turned to mud by two days of rain, rolled the pride and glory of the Minyans --- six dozen heavy war chariots, their bronze plating glistening wetly. Each chariot carried three warriors: a driver with a short sword at his belt, a spearman, and a shield-bearer, whose duty it was to cover the spearman with his shield and hand him spears and javelins; all three wore helmets and double-sided leather cloak-cuirasses sewn with plates of bone or metal.

Behind them marched the infantry: seven hundred shield-bearers from noble and prosperous families able to equip a son with a full set of war-gear, and a hundred of the sturdiest young men, carrying on their shoulders two-handed, double-bladed labrys axes, whose blow always cut clean through a cuirass, and few warriors could take the full weight of so massive an axe on so long a haft upon a shield.

In the rearguard moved a militia armed with whatever came to hand --- mostly poor young men eager for battle, hoping to come by armor and more or less decent weapons right there on the field, and for now firmly believing in Orchomenus's victory and their own invulnerability.

In all, the Orchomenians numbered a little over sixteen hundred men.

On the foremost chariot everyone could behold the famous helmet of basileus Erginus (naturally, along with the basileus's head and all the other body parts), so very like the helmet of Ares Enyalius himself, as he was usually depicted.

One-eyed Pterelaos led the shield-bearers; red-bearded Thesticles, the militia.

Exactly a day later, the leading detachment of chariots rumbled into the valley, leaving on its right the bend of the Cephissus, swollen and roiling with the autumn rains. Erginus, helmet off, rose slightly and, shading his eyes with a hand, saw the Thebans descending on the far side of the valley. The enemy numbered nearly half as many as the Orchomenians, and had precious few chariots besides --- but then, it was Orchomenus, not Thebes, that was famed for the might of its war chariots, so basileus Erginus grinned and thought once more of the gods' favor.

Having advanced to the starting line, Erginus waited for the infantry to catch up. The ground in the valley had turned soft, the horses kept slipping, but the wheels still ran well enough, and the basileus decided that the rain was unlikely to hinder a chariot charge into the enemy's center. Nor did the Thebans have anywhere to retreat: on Erginus's right the muddy, rain-swollen Cephissus curved in an arc, on his left stretched a ridge of hills, and behind the Thebans began the foothills of Cithaeron.

Yes, Amphitryon the Exile had once given the Taphian pirates a good thrashing --- well then, let him try his luck against Orchomenus now!

While the infantry was still coming up, the enemy managed to take position on a low, gentle rise. The Thebans clearly had no intention of coming down from it, crowding on the spot they had chosen and shouting threats and curses at the Orchomenians. It was hard to make out much, but thanks to the heroic efforts of a few especially loud-mouthed individuals, it emerged that the Minyans, having been born into the world by thoroughly unnatural means, were invited to pursue various forms of love with their own mothers, and with she-asses afflicted with skin diseases.

Orchomenus gave as good as it got, so that the Thebans too learned a great many new things about themselves.

"They're counting on our chariots not managing the climb," Erginus took in the situation in an instant, "and they couldn't be more wrong! The slope where they've made their stand is so gentle and stony that... well, may Ares not abandon the brave!"

"Forward!" Erginus shouted the command, slapping his driver on the shoulder; both his war-commanders took up the cry behind him, and after them the rest of the warriors. In the next instant the ground shuddered, and the chariots of Orchomenus surged forward, flinging clods of mud from under hooves and wheels.

Erginus still had time to think that the ground had shuddered somewhat too hard, but paid it no mind.

And then... then it was too late.

The lead horses were already within two spear-throws of the positions of the hushed Thebans when, from the direction of the Cephissus, a torrent of muddy water came roaring into the valley, knocking horses off their feet and overturning chariots... At first Erginus decided that the river-god of the Cephissus had gone mad, but almost at once he understood that the god's sanity had nothing to do with it, and that the murky wall of water was the doing of the accursed Thebans, who had proven far more treacherous than he, the noble basileus Erginus, had believed.

A dozen teams managed to dash through the flood, which only caught them at its edge, and now they were racing at full gallop toward the enemy, not yet realizing that no one was following behind them.

Already up on the rise, the drivers began to rein in their horses in bewilderment, hearing none of the usual thunder behind them, and turning around in hopes of seeing their comrades --- but the Thebans, having instantly cut the traces of their own chariots, had led the horses back, and the Orchomenian teams smashed at full speed into the empty, unmoving carts.

The crowd of Thebans closed around the handful of unlucky men --- and it was all over.

Two chariots did manage to turn back in time, but the men of one were caught at once by well-aimed javelins. So that only the last team succeeded in returning to the newly formed bog, in which the rest of the Minyans' glory and pride now lay so firmly mired.

Still, not everything was lost by any means. The flood of water and the maddened horses had overturned about ten chariots, and another dozen had fallen prey to the enemy --- but the men on the remaining carts were still fully fit to fight, and the infantry had not been touched at all.

What was needed now was to dismount the men, break free of this loathsome mud, and, skirting the edge of the hills, strike at the flank of the exultant Thebans, who were still considerably fewer in number than the Minyans.

Forcing down his seething rage by sheer will, Erginus began giving orders and sighed with relief to see that his commanders had proven themselves beyond all praise: the infantry was already moving toward the hills, making its way onto dry ground clear of the mired chariots.

Pterelaos had only just led the shield-bearers along the ridge when, as if sprung from the earth itself, some two hundred Theban archers and slingers suddenly appeared on the hills. A hail of stones mixed with arrows followed, throwing the shield-bearers into confusion and bringing them to a halt.

But red-haired Thesticles, coming up behind, showed enviable presence of mind, instantly steering the wave of furious militia into the narrow gap between the hills, intending to circle around behind the archers and drive them down onto the spears of the waiting shield-bearers, seizing the high ground in the process.

Thesticles's calculation was sound, and his men made their way successfully through the gap between the hills, preparing to scramble up the gentler slopes on the western side; but...

"Well, so be it!" the red-haired commander had time to think, philosophically, as nearly three dozen Theban chariots, which had been hiding behind the ridge, crushed and trampled the poorly armed militia within moments. The four hundred veterans of past campaigns following behind the chariots finished the rest --- the Orchomenians rolled back into the swampy valley, under a downpour of stones, arrows, and javelins, leaving behind the hills more than two hundred dead and gravely wounded.

As he lay dying, Thesticles could console himself only with the thought that the spear which had crunched its way in under his ribs belonged to Amphitryon, and not to some common warrior.

Small comfort; but better than none at all.

The trap had sprung shut, but the beast caught in it was still very much strong. Holding the gap between the hills, the Theban veterans and the dismounted charioteers were for now withstanding the onslaught of the remaining militia and shield-bearers, thrown into the fight by Cyclops Pterelaos, whose single eye glinted fiercely from the slit of his crested helmet --- but this could not go on much longer.

Especially if the rest of the Orchomenians abandoned their chariots and joined the fighting too, and managed to take the hills.

With difficulty --- his age was telling --- Amphitryon fought his way clear of the melee, ran to a spare chariot left ready beforehand, clambered aboard, gasping, and ordered the driver to get them away.

He had to make it in time.

* * *

Catching sight of the familiar chariot racing out from behind the hills, the young Thebans (barely held in check by their commanders until now) roared with five hundred throats and rushed forward, down the gentle stony slope and into the bog, where the Orchomenians under Erginus were hastily trying to form some semblance of a rampart out of their chariots.

The bait had become the hunter.

Amphitryon did not press into the front ranks of the attackers --- his years no longer allowed it, and besides, it was not his place; nor, in any case, could he have reached them in time. Moving at an easy, measured run in the middle of the militia, he simply kept the untrained Theban youths, hastily armed and loud-mouthed, from realizing --- by his mere presence --- that before them stood nearly two hundred experienced fighters: worn down by their struggle with the elements, mired in the mud, perhaps, but still terribly dangerous. Yes, the young Thebans outnumbered the Orchomenian charioteers by nearly three to one; yes, in the sucking mire that filled the valley, the heavily armed Orchomenians had grown clumsy, which played into the hands of the light-footed, nearly unarmored youths; but still, still, still...

Well, they say Ares loves the brave.

A cloud of javelins, made ready beforehand for throwing from the chariots, rose to meet the Thebans. Many of them found their mark, and the swampy mud closed over those unlucky enough not to reach the enemy.

The losses could have been far greater had Amphitryon not, well in advance, armed the front ranks with large oval shields, which absorbed no small number of deadly stings; besides, the unrelenting barrage from the hills kept the Orchomenians from making an accurate throw.

Casting aside shields bristling with javelins, the attackers leapt with a roar onto the carts blocking their way. Falling onto the Orchomenians' spears, they died by the dozen, but more men were already clambering up behind them, armed with whatever came to hand: clubs, knives, another man's spear yanked from a comrade's belly. A general, chaotic slaughter began, in which mortals who had forgotten about death, plastered head to foot in mud and choking on it, told friend from foe only by some miracle, some special instinct, clamping a death-grip on a stranger's throat so as to fall together into the squelching muck...

There were no wounded. They were trampled into the bloody mud, which rose to the fighters' knees, and in places to their waists.

Now it would take only one small effort to overturn the battle's precarious balance, to tip the scales --- spattered with blood and horse dung --- one way or the other, but the warring sides had nothing left except mud, blood, and the enemy's throat...
