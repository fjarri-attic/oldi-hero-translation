### Third epeisodion

#### 1

Anxiety would not let go of Iolaos for a moment; it had burrowed under his skin like a tick, prodding at his heart with its hairy little legs, poisoning every minute of his sleep---and the most unpleasant part of it was that Iolaos could not find a single actual reason for his worry.

Everything was going so well that it simply could not be true.

Alkeides had forgotten about his madness; the secret of the Phlegraian Fields lay safely buried beneath the barrow of legend; the three surviving members of the Brotherhood of Salmoneus---Neleus, Augeias, and Hippokoon---were close at hand, in the Peloponnese, unable to leave their own lands, able only to wait for great Herakles to finally get around to them too, stripped as they were of overt and secret power; and even the gods, those irritable and unpredictable Olympians, were behaving with the utmost benevolence.

Apollo, flaying alive the satyr Marsyas, who had dared to challenge Mousagetes to a musical contest; Artemis, shooting the children of loose-tongued Niobe; Athena, turning Arachne the Lydian, her rival in the weaver's craft, into a hideous spider; Zeus, incinerating whole cities; Dionysos, punishing the Tyrrhenian pirates...

All that had receded into the past, and was even starting to be forgotten---the Achaians were not, by nature, a people to hold grudges.

And the stream of human blood that ran down the altars was drying up.

Perhaps that was why Iolaos had so readily answered the summons of his old acquaintance Theseus---who, never having made peace with the quarrelsome Athenians, was now living on Skyros, a small island northeast of Euboia---and, calling himself an overcautious fool, had left the Peloponnese.

He wanted to prove to himself that everything really was fine.

A week before his departure, volunteers had begun streaming into Tiryns from all over Hellas---word had gone around that any day now Herakles would invade Elis---and so many were eager to plunder tightfisted Augeias that the Argolis basin proved too small to hold them all.

The fearsome battle cry "Herakles is coming" was quite enough to make the wretched Eleians throw up their hands in despair; everyone else's hands, meanwhile, immediately began to itch for a fight.

Nearby Mycenae grew nervous almost at once, making it plain that it had no wish to tolerate the presence of so many armed men in such dangerous proximity to the Mycenaean walls. But even this flare-up was resolved in the most fortunate way---a messenger arrived from Arkadia, sent by the ruler Kepheus, a former Argonaut, offering Kepheus's own lands for quartering the army, as well as the services of the warlike Arkadians for the coming campaign.

The fate of Elis, once the seat of the rebellious Salmoneus the Madman, and of its ruler Augeias, was decided.

Now he can set off for Skyros, to visit Theseus, with a clear conscience.

But the conscience refused to be clear.

Crossing the Isthmos and then Attike, hiring a small twenty-oared galley whose owner turned out to be a passionate lover of the horse races and refused to take more than half the fare from an olympionikes[\*](#fn-77){.noteref}, Iolaos could not shake the feeling that somewhere overhead, point down, an invisible sword hung suspended, waiting only for the right moment to fall.

As everyone knows: everything comes to those who wait.

* * *

The house of Lykomedes, basileus of Skyros, who had sheltered Theseus after his exile from Athens, did not in the least resemble a palace.

Nothing much to look at, really---an estate worn down by time and by want of money.

And this estate was now sunk in mourning.

The hair of Lykomedes's servants and kinsfolk had been cropped short, their heads strewn with ashes, their garments mercilessly torn, and wailing and lamentation rose to the heavens almost without pause.

"What happened?" Breaking through the howling, hand-wringing cordon, Iolaos found Lykomedes himself in the megaron, gloomily contemplating the dying embers of the hearth.

The basileus of Skyros pulled out a tuft of his already thinning hair; thought a moment, and pulled out another.

"What happened? Where is Theseus?!"

"Alas, alas for us, god-equal Iolaos," Lykomedes drawled mournfully, sunk deep in an enormous chair, "grief has entered our house, and our life is now as empty as a punctured wineskin. Great Theseus, whose fame can be compared only to that of Herakles, best of mortals, has left us, departing into the gloom of Hades. Anguish gnaws at my heart, anguish has crept in like a serpent---"

"When did Theseus die?! How did it happen?!" Iolaos felt a sharp urge to seize his honey-tongued host by the throat and shake the truth out of him, every detail of it. "He wasn't even thirty-five yet!"

"He wasn't," Lykomedes agreed, sorrow in his voice. "But the gods do not count our years when they call us to them. Great Theseus was summoned by his divine father Poseidon, and the hero fell from a cliff into the sea before my very eyes. It happened the day before yesterday, the morning after the departure of the Athenian envoy---"

The basileus of Skyros snapped his mouth shut in haste, realizing his slip---but it was too late.

"How much did they promise you for Theseus's death?!" Little Lykomedes floundered in Iolaos's grip, his colorless, fish-like eyes bulging. "How much, you bastard?! Talk!"

The master of Skyros wheezed, foam appearing at his lips---and Iolaos, flinging the basileus back into his chair, strode toward the door.

"You have insulted me twice, insolent Iolaos," Lykomedes, recovering himself, cried after him, "no, three times: as a guest, by raising your hand against your host; as a young man, by insulting my gray head; and, finally, as a false witness, by rashly accusing an innocent man! But I am not angry with you, no, I am not angry..."

Iolaos went out, slamming the door.

At sea, leaving Skyros behind, he kept thinking of Theseus---he could not bring himself to say "the late Theseus"---as he remembered him from back on Crete: short, impetuous, quick to strike and to make friends, a born skirt-chaser and a brilliant spearman, by all accounts the darling of the whole *Argo*, on which he had sailed as a boy of sixteen...

Having taken no part in the Argonauts' voyage himself---at the time, he had been more preoccupied with old Possessed Galinthias than with any Golden Fleece---Iolaos now suddenly pictured, with total clarity, the deck of the famous ship, and young Theseus sharing an oar with Autolykos... while nearby, beside stern Tiphys the helmsman, stands Jason, leader of the Argonauts, and, listening to divine Orpheus pluck the strings of his kithara, smiles at gloomy Iphikles, vainly hoping for a smile in return.

O heavens, could it really have been sixteen whole years?!

Arriving in Attike, Iolaos learned in a harbor tavern that on the Isthmos the old *Argo*---set up there in Poseidon's honor---had collapsed for no reason at all, and its wreckage had crushed to death a vagrant asleep in its shade.

The vagrant turned out to be Jason, once leader of the Argonauts.

At around the same time, in the meadows of Pieria, the maddened bacchantes, swept up in sacred frenzy, had torn divine Orpheus to pieces.

And finally, in Sicily, the creator of the Cretan Labyrinth, the great inventor Daidalos, had been swiftly carried off by a sudden attack of fever---though he, at least, had not been an Argonaut.

These reports, this strange, wild, senseless epidemic of deaths, made the very ground sway beneath Iolaos's feet.

That night he got drunk.

Blind drunk, the way only people who hold their liquor well can get, spitting at the sky and shouting inarticulate curses, and then sinking into a murky sleep; and he saw the planked deck of a ship, people with familiar faces sitting at the oars, and an enormous forefinger---only the finger, without a hand, without a body, a dead finger with a bitten nail---pointing in turn at the living, laughing people, who vanished one after another, their laughter still ringing in the salty sea air, and the oars moving by themselves, as though vanished hands were still gripping them---an emptying ship, the laughter of the departed, the memory of their hands moving the oars, and the senseless finger with its blue, swollen knuckles...

In the morning Iolaos sat on the threshold of the tavern---passing Ionians shied away from the richly dressed man who had settled himself into the beggars' traditional spot---and vainly longed for a hangover that would let him forget both dream and reality.

There was no hangover; his thoughts came easily, and he wanted to howl.

"Can't afford a drink?" a fat, red-faced sailor asked sympathetically, turning off a narrow side street toward the tavern.

Iolaos lifted his head and stared at the sympathetic stranger.

A bright kerchief tied around the sailor's head hid his hair, and a massive earring in his left ear hung almost to his shoulder.

"Daidalos died in Sicily," Iolaos said distinctly, hoping the sailor would take him for a madman and leave, "of some stupid fever. Women tore Orpheus apart, Jason was crushed under wreckage, and Theseus fell off a cliff. What else is new in the world, sailor?"

"Good man!" The sea dog plopped down beside him and waved to the tavern-keeper, who had poked his head out the half-open door. "Hey, you, you thieving barkeep, bring us food and drink for two---I feel like sitting a while with a smart man! Come on, quick about it!"

"Where from?" Iolaos asked, none too distinctly, but the sailor understood him anyway.

"From Iolkos. Barely got away in time. Ah, poor Iolkos---it'll go up in flames yet! Basileus Akastos has shut himself up in the city, sitting there under siege---Akastos, that is ("A former Argonaut," Iolaos added mechanically)---and outside the walls, with their armies, are the Dioskouroi brothers, Kastor and Polydeukes ("Former Argonauts," Iolaos added mechanically), and that other one... what's his name?... ah, Peleus! The one who went campaigning with Herakles! The heroes are warring with each other, Nyx take their souls..."

"Over what?"

"Who knows over what?! Over nothing at all! So, smarty, let's drink to the memory of the departed who are still alive!..."

Iolaos lifted his head.

There was no sun in the sky.

There, instead, hung a jointed finger, and its bitten nail was pointing north.

Toward Iolkos.

Iolaos would never forgive himself for setting off that morning down the Dromoi to Iolkos, hoping to be in time to prevent senseless bloodshed---instead of hurrying home, to the twins, who had marched out at the head of an imposing army against Augeias the Eleian.

Favorable omens, like vultures biding their time, accompanied Herakles the whole way.
