### Third epeisodion

#### 1

Not for a moment did anxiety let go of Iolaus, having burrowed under his skin like a tick, jostling at his heart with its hairy little legs, poisoning every minute of his sleep --- and the most unpleasant part of it was that Iolaus could not find a single real reason for his worry.

Everything was going so well that it simply could not be true.

Alcides had forgotten about his madness; the secret of the Phlegraean Fields lay safely buried beneath the barrow of legend; the three surviving members of the Brotherhood of Salmoneus --- Neleus, Augeas, and Hippocoon --- were close at hand, in the Peloponnese, unable to leave their own lands, able only to wait for great Heracles to finally get around to them too, stripped as they were of open and hidden power; and even the gods, those irritable and unpredictable Olympians, were behaving with the utmost benevolence.

Apollo, flaying alive the satyr Marsyas, who had dared to challenge Musagetes to a musical contest; Artemis, shooting down the children of gossipy Niobe; Athena, turning Arachne the Lydian, her rival in the weaver's craft, into a hideous spider; Zeus, incinerating whole cities; Dionysus, punishing the Tyrrhenian pirates...

All that had receded into the past, and was even starting to be forgotten --- the Achaeans were not, by nature, a vengeful people.

And the stream of human blood that ran down the altars was drying up.

Perhaps that was why Iolaus had so readily answered the summons of his old acquaintance Theseus --- who, never having made peace with the quarrelsome Athenians, was now living on Scyros, a small island northeast of Euboea --- and, calling himself an overcautious fool, had left the Peloponnese.

He wanted to prove to himself that everything really was fine.

A week before his departure, volunteers had begun streaming into Tiryns from all over Hellas --- word had gone around that any day now Heracles would invade Eleia --- and so many people wanted a share of plundering tightfisted Augeas that the Argolis basin proved too small to hold them all.

The fearsome battlecry "Heracles is coming" was quite enough to make the wretched Eleians' hands drop helplessly to their sides; everyone else's hands, meanwhile, immediately began itching for a fight.

Nearby Mycenae grew nervous almost at once, making it plain that it had no wish to tolerate the presence of so many armed men in such dangerous proximity to the Mycenaean walls. But even this flare-up was resolved in the most fortunate way --- a messenger arrived from Arcadia, sent by the ruler Cepheus, a former Argonaut, offering Cepheus's own lands for quartering the army, as well as the services of the warlike Arcadians for the coming campaign.

The fate of Eleia, once the seat of the rebellious Salmoneus the Madman, and of its ruler Augeas, was decided.

One could set off for Scyros, to visit Theseus, with an easy conscience.

But an easy conscience he just couldn't manage.

Crossing the Isthmos and then Attica, hiring a small twenty-oared galley whose owner turned out to be an avid fan of horse racing and refused to take more than half the fare from an olympionikes[^77], Iolaus could not shake the feeling that somewhere overhead, point down, an invisible sword hung suspended, waiting only for the right moment to fall.

[^77]: Iolaus became an Olympic victor in the chariot race almost immediately after the Gigantomachy.

As everyone knows: everything comes to those who wait.

The house of Lycomedes, basileus of Scyros, who had given shelter to the exiled Theseus of Athens, did not in the least resemble a palace.

Nothing much to look at, really --- an estate worn down by time and by want of money.

And this estate was now sunk in mourning.

The hair of Lycomedes's servants and kinsfolk had been cropped short, their heads strewn with ashes, their garments mercilessly torn, and wailing and lamentation rose to the heavens almost without pause.

"What happened?" Breaking through the howling, hand-wringing cordon, Iolaus found Lycomedes himself in the megaron, gloomily contemplating the dying embers of the hearth.

The basileus of Scyros tore a tuft of hair from his already thinning head; thought a moment, and tore out another.

"What happened? Where is Theseus?!"

"Alas, alas for us, god-equal Iolaus," Lycomedes drawled mournfully, sunk deep in an enormous chair, "grief has entered our house, and our life is now as empty as a punctured wineskin. Great Theseus, whose fame can be compared only to that of Heracles, best of mortals, has left us, departing into the gloom of Hades. Anguish gnaws at my heart, anguish has crept in like a serpent..."

"When did Theseus die?! How did it happen?!" Iolaus felt a sharp urge to seize his honey-tongued host by the throat and shake the truth out of him, every detail of it. "He wasn't even thirty-five yet!"

"He wasn't," Lycomedes agreed, sorrow in his voice. "But the gods do not count our years when they call us to them. Great Theseus was summoned by his divine father Poseidon, and the hero fell from a cliff into the sea before my very eyes. It happened the day before yesterday, the morning after the departure of the Athenian envoy..."

The basileus of Scyros snapped his mouth shut in haste, realizing his slip --- but it was too late.

"How much did they promise you for Theseus's death?!" Little Lycomedes floundered in Iolaus's grip, his colorless, fish-like eyes bulging. "How much, you bastard?! Talk!"

The master of Scyros wheezed, foam appearing at his lips --- and Iolaus, flinging the basileus back into his chair, strode toward the door.

"You have insulted me twice, insolent Iolaus," Lycomedes cried after him, having recovered himself, "no, three times: as a guest, by raising your hand against your host; as a young man, by insulting my gray head; and, finally, as a false witness, by rashly accusing an innocent man! But I am not angry with you, no, I am not angry..."

Iolaus went out, slamming the door.

At sea, leaving Scyros behind, he kept thinking of Theseus --- his tongue would not turn to say "the late Theseus" --- as he remembered him still on Crete: short, quick-tempered, quick to strike and quick to make friends, a born skirt-chaser and a brilliant spearman, by all accounts the darling of the whole *Argo*, on which he had sailed as a boy of sixteen...

Having taken no part in the Argonauts' voyage himself --- at the time, Iolaus had been more preoccupied with old Possessed Galinthias than with any Golden Fleece --- Iolaus now suddenly pictured, with total clarity, the deck of the famous ship, and young Theseus pulling an oar together with Autolycus... while nearby, beside stern Tiphys the helmsman, stood Jason --- leader of the Argonauts --- and, listening to divine Orpheus running his fingers over the strings of his kithara, smiled at gloomy Iphicles, vainly hoping for a smile in return.

Oh heavens, could it really have been sixteen whole years since then?!

Arriving in Attica, Iolaus learned in a harbor tavern that on the Isthmos the old *Argo* --- set up there in Poseidon's honor --- had collapsed for no reason at all, and its wreckage had crushed to death a vagrant asleep in its shade.

The vagrant turned out to be Jason, once leader of the Argonauts.

At around the same time, on the meadows of Pieria, the maddened bacchante women, swept up in sacred frenzy, had torn divine Orpheus to pieces.

And finally, on Sicily, the creator of the Cretan Labyrinth, the great inventor Daedalus, had died suddenly of a sudden fever --- though he, at least, had not been an Argonaut.

These reports, this strange, wild, senseless epidemic of deaths, made the very ground sway beneath Iolaus's feet.

By evening he got drunk.

Terribly drunk, the way only people who take poorly to drink can get, spitting at the sky and shouting inarticulate curses, and then sinking into a murky sleep; and he saw the planked deck of a ship, people with familiar faces sitting at the oars, and an enormous forefinger --- only the finger, without a hand, without a body, a dead finger with a bitten nail --- pointing in turn at the living, laughing people, who vanished one after another, their laughter still ringing in the salty sea air, and the oars moving by themselves, as though vanished hands were still gripping them --- an emptying ship, the laughter of the departed, the memory of their hands moving the oars, and the senseless finger with its blue, swollen knuckles...

In the morning Iolaus sat on the threshold of the tavern --- passing Ionians shied away from the richly dressed man who had settled himself into the beggars' traditional spot --- and vainly longed for a hangover that would let him forget both dream and reality.

There was no hangover; his thoughts came easily, and he wanted to howl.

"Nothing to drink on?" a fat, red-faced sailor asked sympathetically, turning off a narrow side street toward the tavern.

Iolaus lifted his head and stared at his sympathizer.

A bright headscarf hid the sailor's hair, and a massive earring in his left ear hung almost to his shoulder.

"Daedalus died on Sicily," Iolaus said distinctly, hoping the sailor would take him for a madman and leave, "of some stupid fever. Women tore Orpheus apart, Jason was crushed under wreckage, and Theseus fell off a cliff. What else is new in the world, sailor?"

"Good man!" The old sea dog plopped down beside him and waved to the tavern-keeper, who had poked his head out the half-open door. "Hey, you, you robber-host, bring us food and drink for two --- I feel like sitting with a smart man a while! Come on, quick about it!"

"Where from?" Iolaus asked, none too distinctly, but the sailor understood him anyway.

"From Iolcus. Barely got away in time. Ah, poor Iolcus --- it'll go up in flames yet! Basileus Acastus has shut himself up in the city, sitting there under siege --- Acastus, that is ("A former Argonaut," Iolaus added mechanically) --- and outside the walls, with their armies, are the Dioscuri brothers, Castor and Polydeuces ("Former Argonauts," Iolaus added mechanically), and that other one... what's his name?... ah, Peleus! The one who went campaigning with Heracles! The heroes are warring with each other, Nyx take their souls..."

"Over what?"

"Who knows what over?! Over nothing at all! So let's drink, smarty, and remember the dead while they're still alive!.."

Iolaus lifted his head.

There was no sun in the sky.

There, instead, hung a jointed finger, and its bitten nail was pointing north.

Toward Iolcus.

Iolaus would never forgive himself for setting off that morning down the Dromoi to Iolcus, hoping to be in time to prevent senseless bloodshed --- instead of hurrying home, to the twins, who had marched out at the head of an imposing army against Augeas the Eleian.

Favorable omens, like vultures biding their time, accompanied Heracles the whole way.
