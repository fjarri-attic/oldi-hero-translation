#### 4

"Dad,"---the word cost Amphitryon no small effort---"I'm coming with you."

Iphikles looked at his son---a skinny, flaxen-haired boy staring at the floor.

"Coming where?" he asked, with exaggerated sternness.

The boy suddenly raised his head and looked Iphikles steadily in the face.

"To the palaestra," Amphitryon grumbled, and thought to himself: [A poor excuse for a father you make, son! I used to chase you and your brother out the door---loafers that you were! And you yourself were practically living at the palaestra from the age of six!]{.thoughts}

Iphikles gave a disbelieving snort, rubbed his sleep-stiffened neck, and suddenly laughed, catching his son under the arms and tossing him toward the ceiling.

"What, hero, sick of sitting with the women?!" he shouted. "Blood stirring? Well, that's as it should be---you and I have good, lively blood... Get ready, let's go!"

[He's gotten heavy somehow, my Iolaos!]{.thoughts} the thought flickered at the edge of his mind.

"If anyone starts bullying you---complain to your father!" Alkmene fussed. "And don't run around too much, it's bad for you!"

"That's right, mom!" Iphikles set his son down and kissed his mother on the cheek. "No running, no jumping, must boil the milk... Tough luck, mom---you wanted a girl, and a boy turned up! Hey, Iolie boy, what should you do when someone starts bullying you?"

"Shout," Amphitryon answered sullenly.

"Shout what?" Iphikles asked, surprised---he'd expected a different answer. "Shout what, exactly?! 'Dad, help'?"

"Oh, sure, wait around for you to come help!" A crooked smirk surfaced on the boy's high-cheekboned face. "First your river's flooded, then you're out of arrows... no, we Perseids shout something else!"

And while Iphikles stood rooted to the spot, unable to believe his ears, little Iolaos jumped up, grabbed his father around the neck, and in an instant scrambled onto his shoulders.

"They're picking on the little ones!"---the old battle cry thundered out, and two skinny legs clamped around Iphikles's neck with surprising tenacity, while two narrow palms cracked against Iphikles's ears with surprising force.

On his way down, the boy kicked his father in the chest---and landed less than gracefully, scraping his left knee.

"Well, now," Amphitryon said in vexation, "this won't do at all! No, to the palaestra, to the palaestra, this instant!---or I'll go sour here on milk and all your love! Catch up, pops!"

And he bolted out the door.

"Well, why not?" Iphikles declared suddenly. "Atta boy! Takes after me. Or his grandfather."

Alkmene waved her hands at him.

"Bite your tongue, son! If there's one thing I'd never wish on my grandson, it's his grandfather's fate! You know yourself---heroes don't live long. And the lives they have... are no joy to anyone. Look at Teiresias---blind, and he just lives on and on..."

"All right, mom," Iphikles cut her off. "Let's not talk about it. Alkeides swore an oath, I promised you---look, they invited us to Iolkos, to build the *Argo*, and we didn't go..."

And the door slammed once more.

Alkmene sighed and went to the gynaikeion, where a baby was crying and Asteropeia, barely audible, was lulling the girl to sleep.

For the first time, she felt truly old.

...And the palaestra was as noisy as ever. No one paid any attention to the arrival of Iphikles's son---which suited Amphitryon just fine. The only thing he had to keep track of was the lesson schedule: when the pupils went into the covered gymnasium, he ran on the stadium track; when the boys threw discuses and javelins, he went into the gymnasium; when they worked with weapons, he headed for the jumping pit.

By evening he knew for certain that all he needed was strength---his old skills had survived completely intact, unsurprisingly, and the young body learned quickly whatever it needed to; and this, on the one hand, pleased him, while on the other, it threatened to tear his untrained ligaments or dislocate joints that were childishly supple but still weak.

The slave woman who brought his midday meal later delighted Alkmene with the news that her beloved grandson was eating enough for three, with a hero's appetite---something that had never happened before.

And so it became the custom from that day on: the palaestra, plentiful food, and no more boiled milk.

One thing Amphitryon kept from the old Iolaos---he still went to bed early, and would wake around midnight when his solicitous grandmother tiptoed into her grandson's chamber, straightened the blanket over him, listened to his breathing, and withdrew.

And the little boy, teeth clenched, would wait until her footsteps faded, then leap up, slip out of his chamber, and, unnoticed by anyone, run off to the night palaestra for another two or three hours.

He needed to hurry.

A month later, Alkeides came back from Troizen. By then Amphitryon had had a hundred chances to enjoy the tale of his son's---his uncle's?---first visit to Pittheus the Wise, basileus of Troizen, when at the sight of the Kithaironian lion's hide flung over a chair, terror seized all of Pittheus's grandsons---all except young Theseus, who threw himself at the beast with an axe.

After which Alkeides had supposedly predicted a brilliant future for Theseus.

Amphitryon himself seriously doubted any particular brilliance in Theseus's future, if at twelve years old the boy went at a hide with an axe.

Still, when the whole family gathered in the megaron to hear Alkeides's account of his latest, highly successful journey---Amphitryon, watching from a secluded corner, did not like the look of Alkeides at all.

An unhealthy agitation kept washing over his elder son; his cheeks would go sharply pale one moment and break out in crimson blotches the next; Alkeides spoke far too loudly and waved his arms about as he talked, which had never been his habit before; a strange hysteria, out of place in a man of his age and build, showed through all his behavior.

After three days of watching Alkeides at the palaestra, Amphitryon became certain that his son was in perfect physical health---which he could not say of the health of his mind. Alkeides often shouted at his wife Megara (though, given her temperament, that was not especially surprising); with the children he was either pointedly affectionate or unreasonably irritable; besides that, he had taken to frequenting the temples, where he would stand for a long time watching the sacrificial rites and biting his lips, after which he would be gloomy and uncommunicative for the rest of the day.

He was like a drunkard long deprived of wine; the Taphians had a word for such a state: "khumar."[\*](#fn-khumar){.noteref}

Amphitryon was afraid even to think that his son might actually miss the curse he had borne since infancy---the fits of madness.

He was afraid to---and thought it all the same.

And at night, after exhausting vigils at the palaestra, six-year-old Iolaos dreamed of the Isles of the Blessed---a boundless, hopeless solitude---and a shade crying out into the emptiness:

"I am Amphitryon the Exile!..."

Sometimes it seemed to him that had he not died back then, at Orchomenos, five years ago---Alkeides, his son, whom Amphitryon and Zeus had fought over, each trying to wrench him from the other's hands, would have remained as he was.
