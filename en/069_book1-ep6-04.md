#### 4

"Dad," --- the word cost Amphitryon no small effort --- "I'm coming with you."

Iphicles looked at his son --- a skinny, flaxen-haired boy staring at the floor.

"Coming where?" he asked, with exaggerated sternness.

The boy suddenly raised his head and looked Iphicles steadily in the face.

"To the palaestra," Amphitryon grumbled, and thought to himself: "A poor excuse for a father you make, son! I used to chase you and your brother out the door --- loafers that you were! And you yourself were off to the palaestra from the age of six, no less!"

Iphicles gave a disbelieving snort, rubbed his neck still stiff from sleep, and suddenly laughed, catching his son under the arms and tossing him toward the ceiling.

"What, hero, sick of sitting with the women?!" he shouted. "Blood stirring? Well, that's as it should be --- you and I have good, ringing blood... Get ready, let's go!"

"He's gotten heavy somehow, my Iolaus!" --- the thought flickered at the edge of his mind.

"If anyone starts bullying you --- complain to your father!" Alcmene fussed. "And don't run around too much, you mustn't!"

"That's right, mom!" Iphicles set his son down and kissed his mother on the cheek. "No running, no jumping, must boil the milk... No luck for you --- you wanted a girl, and a boy turned up! Hey, little Iolaus, what should you do when someone starts bullying you?"

"Shout," Amphitryon answered sullenly.

"Shout what?" Iphicles asked, surprised --- he'd expected a different answer. "Shout what, exactly?! 'Dad, help'?"

"Oh, sure, wait around for you to come help!" --- a crooked smirk surfaced on the boy's high-cheekboned face. "One time your river's flooded, another time you're out of arrows... no, we Perseids shout something else!"

And while Iphicles stood rooted like a post, not believing his own ears, little Iolaus jumped up, grabbed his father around the neck, and in an instant scrambled onto his shoulders.

"They're picking on the little ones!" --- the old battle cry thundered out, and two skinny legs clamped around Iphicles's neck with surprising tenacity, while two narrow palms cracked against Iphicles's ears with surprising force.

Jumping down, the boy kicked his father in the chest along the way --- and landed less than gracefully, scraping his left knee.

"Well now," Amphitryon tossed out, annoyed, "what good is that? No, to the palaestra, to the palaestra, this instant! --- or you'll go soft here on milk and all this coddling! Catch up, pops!"

And he bolted out the door.

"Well, why not?" Iphicles declared suddenly. "Good lad! Takes after me. Or his grandfather."

Alcmene threw up her hands at her son.

"Watch your tongue, son! If there's one thing I'd never wish on my grandson, it's his grandfather's fate! You know yourself --- heroes don't live long. And what life they have... isn't a joy to anyone. Now Tiresias --- blind, and yet he goes on living and living..."

"All right, mom," Iphicles cut her off. "Let's not talk about it. Alcides swore an oath, I promised you --- look, they invited us to Iolcus, to build the *Argo*, and we didn't go..."

And the door slammed once more.

Alcmene sighed and went to the gynaeceum, from where came the sound of a baby's crying and Asteropeia's barely audible voice, singing the girl to sleep.

For the first time, she felt truly old.

* * *

...And the palaestra was as noisy as ever. No one paid any attention to Iphicles's son's arrival --- which suited Amphitryon just fine. The only thing he had to keep track of was the lesson schedule: when the students went into the covered gymnasium, he ran on the stadium track; when the boys threw discus and javelin, he went into the gymnasium; when they worked with weapons, he headed for the jumping pit.

By evening he knew for certain that all he needed was strength --- his old skills had come back completely intact, unsurprisingly, and the young body learned quickly whatever it needed to; and this, on the one hand, pleased him, while on the other --- it threatened torn, unprepared ligaments, or a dislocated joint, childishly supple but still weak.

The slave woman who brought his midday meal later made Alcmene happy with the news that her beloved grandson was eating enough for three, with a hero's appetite --- something that had never happened before.

And so it became the custom from that day on: the palaestra, plentiful food, and no more boiled milk.

One thing Amphitryon kept from the old Iolaus --- he still went to bed early, and would wake around midnight when his solicitous grandmother tiptoed into her grandson's chamber, straightened the blanket over him, listened to his breathing, and withdrew.

And the little boy, teeth clenched, would wait until her footsteps faded, then leap up, slip out of his chamber, and, unnoticed by anyone, run off to the night palaestra for another two or three hours.

He needed to hurry.

A month later, Alcides came back from Troezen. By then Amphitryon had had a hundred chances to enjoy the tale of his son's --- his uncle's? --- first visit to Pittheus the Wise, basileus of Troezen, when at the sight of the Cithaeronian lion's hide flung over a chair, terror seized all of Pittheus's grandsons --- all except young Theseus, who threw himself at the beast with an axe.

After which Alcides had supposedly predicted a brilliant future for Theseus.

Amphitryon himself seriously doubted any particular brilliance in Theseus's future, if at twelve years old the boy went at a hide with an axe.

Still, when the whole family gathered in the megaron to hear Alcides's account of his current, very successful journey --- Alcides himself did not sit well at all with Amphitryon, who was watching him from a hidden corner.

An unhealthy agitation kept washing over his elder son; his cheeks would go sharply pale one moment and flush crimson the next; Alcides spoke far too loudly and waved his arms about as he talked, which had never been his habit before; and through all his behavior there showed a strange hysteria, out of place in a man of his age and build.

After three days of watching Alcides at the palaestra, Amphitryon became certain that his son was in perfect physical health --- which he could not say of his spiritual health.

Alcides often shouted at his wife Megara (though, given her temperament, that was not especially surprising); with the children he was either pointedly affectionate or unreasonably irritable; besides that, he had taken to visiting the temples often, where he would stand for a long time watching the sacrificial rites and biting his lips, after which he would be gloomy and uncommunicative for the rest of the day.

He was like a drunkard long deprived of wine; the Taphians had a word for such a state: "khumar."[^khumar]

[^khumar] This is an authors' anachronistic joke. "Khumar" exists in Russian, meaning a hangover state. It was borrowed from Arabic through Turkic languages, where it had the same meaning. But it is very unlikely that it would be in use in the Bronze Age Greece. [TN]

Amphitryon was afraid even to think that his son might be missing his infant curse --- the fits of madness.

Afraid, but he thought it all the same.

And at night, after exhausting vigils at the palaestra, six-year-old Iolaus dreamed of the Isles of the Blessed --- a boundless, hopeless solitude --- and a shade crying out into the emptiness:

"I am Amphitryon the Exile!.."

Sometimes it seemed to him that had he not died back then, at Orchomenus, five years ago --- Alcides, his son, whom Amphitryon and Zeus had torn from each other's hands, would have remained as he was.
