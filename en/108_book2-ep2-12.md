#### 12

It smelled of slaughter.

Of raw meat, of slowly settling dust, of icy sweat, of the reek of a howl choked off mid-cry, of dirty, businesslike death with its arms bloodied to the elbows... it smelled of slaughter.

The snapped threads of the Dromos lashed across his eyes, blinding him, forcing him to squeeze them shut; Iolaus had not yet managed to grasp that he had broken through, that he was racing, racing on, holding his balance by instinct, reins gone slack, fused with the maddened horses and the seashell come alive on wheels; he was still only learning to see anew, still only beginning to breathe air instead of the searing fury that with a bird's shriek had been tearing at his chest with curved talons --- when someone hurled himself into the chariot beside him in a long leap, struck him with a taut, straining body, and wrenched the reins from his hands... the two of them tumbled over the rail together, rolling across the scorching ground; the enemy twisted like a viper, but Iolaus still managed to pin him underneath, bearing down, pressing him flat, though he couldn't catch hold of his arms...

And he felt something jammed down over his head.

"Get off me, you idiot!" the enemy rasped, in a voice strangely familiar.

Iolaus obediently got up and slowly felt at the headgear that had been forced onto him...

A helm.

An ancient helm of a long-forgotten shape, resembling a shallow, upturned bowl ringed with a row of knobs around its crown, a piece of thick leather covering the nape of the neck, and a narrow nose-guard; a perfect fit.

A helm.

"What's going on there, Hermeias?" someone called from a distance.

"Nothing," Hermeias answered, rising and brushing himself off. "A Dromos must've opened up on its own somewhere close by, and then a chariot... plowed right into me, the damn thing!"

And then, to Iolaus, in a hissing whisper:

"Don't take off the helm! If you're seen here, on Phlegrae --- that's the end of it! Understood?"

There was no need to say it twice.

Iolaus had already noticed that the Trickster was whispering without looking his way --- not out of secrecy, but because he himself could not see him.

The Lord's helm --- the one the rhapsodes sing of.

For even the very name "Hades" has, since time immemorial, meant "unseen."

...Five paces from Iolaus lay a dead man.

No, not a man.

Or was he?

An unnaturally large adolescent --- ten years old? twelve? fifteen?! --- who looked at once like a hideous god and a beautiful beast, whose face could not be looked upon without a shudder; the way one sometimes doesn't know, on meeting the unknown, whether to pray to it or flee from it.

Beside the boy's wounded body --- which, if one did not look too closely, seemed more like that of a human child --- lay scattered arrows fletched with pale-grey feathers, and a bow.

A bow made of wood and horn.

"Eurytus?" Iolaus breathed, barely moving his lips, understanding that this corpse too was no longer Eurytus of Oechalia, but merely carrion.

The Trickster nodded.

Iolaus bent down, tilted the dead man's head back, and stared for a long while at the slit throat.

Then he kicked, with his sandal, the stiffening hand still clutching a knife in its fist.

"If that was Eurytus, then he managed to kill himself," Iolaus said, feeling that he was about to break into a shout. "Dead men don't cut their own throats. Did you see his shade, Trickster?!"

"...What?" Hermeias asked in surprise --- unclear what about.

And Iolaus understood that the youth-god was managing, by some miracle, not to fall.

He went over to the swaying Hermeias, put a half-embrace around him --- the Trickster, with a sigh of gratitude, leaned into the offered shoulder --- and swept his gaze over Phlegrae.

The scorched plain, where the Family stood over the bodies of misshapen children.

Zeus Brotoloigos, who had crept like a thief into Alcmene's bedchamber and afterward allowed himself to meet Amphitryon face to face only once --- when the lawagetas lay dying at Orchomenus; Poseidon Ennosigaios, who had stood in a Theban alley over the husband of the Thunderer's mistress as the man rose up from his knees; Hera Argeia, who had sent venomous serpents that, by sheer miracle, never reached two eight-month-old infants; Ares Enyalius, whose path a mortal grandson of Perseus had once blocked, felling the god into the bloody mud; Apollo Aigletes, an archer swift to punish, who had grappled with a raging Heracles at Delphi; grimy smith Hephaestus, merry drunkard Dionysus, virgin huntress Artemis...

And wise Athena, who this very day would become Pallas[^73] --- there she was, bent over a dead Gigas, over a child named Pallas, briskly flaying the skin from the corpse with a curved blade, meaning to stretch it later over her shield.

[^73]: "The vanquisher of Pallas"; the epithet Athena received after the Gigantomachy.

The gods stood over the Gigantes; the surviving sacrifices --- over the fallen priests.

Exhausted adults in torn garments --- over the vanquished children.

And all of them, the living and the dead alike, were equally mortal.

A little way off, Alcides was tearing his chiton into strips and binding up Iphicles's arrow-shot thigh.

When he finished, he helped his brother to his feet --- and the twins, without looking back, made for the painfully throbbing Dromos.

Heracles was leaving Phlegrae.

"They killed the children who nearly killed us," someone from the Family muttered after him.

Backing away toward the Dromos through which he had come, Iolaus watched the faces of the gods as they looked at the departing Heracles.

The faces of the saved, who would never forgive their savior.

"A hero stands alone..." an echo whispered over the Phlegraean Fields.

But Iolaus no longer heard it.

And even if he had, he would not yet have grasped the new, secret meaning folded into those familiar words.
