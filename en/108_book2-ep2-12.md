#### 12

It smelled of slaughter.

Of raw meat, of slowly settling dust, of icy sweat, of the reek of a howl choked off mid-cry, of dirty, businesslike death with its arms bloodied to the elbows... it smelled of slaughter.

The snapped threads of the Dromos lashed across his eyes, blinding him, forcing him to squeeze them shut; Iolaos had not yet managed to grasp that he had broken through, that he was racing, racing on, holding his balance by instinct, reins gone slack, fused with the maddened horses and the seashell come alive on wheels; he was still only learning to see anew, still only beginning to breathe air instead of the searing fury that had been tearing at his chest with curved talons and the screech of a bird of prey---when someone hurled himself into the chariot beside him in a long leap, struck him with a taut, straining body, and wrenched the reins from his hands... the two of them tumbled over the rail together, rolling across the scorching ground; the enemy twisted like a viper, but Iolaos nevertheless managed to pin him underneath, bearing down, pressing him flat, though he couldn't catch hold of his arms...

And he felt something being jammed down onto his head.

"Get off me, you idiot!" the enemy rasped, in a voice strangely familiar.

Iolaos obediently got up and slowly felt at the headgear that had been forced onto him...

A helm.

An ancient helm of a long-forgotten shape, resembling a shallow, upturned bowl with a ring of knobs around its crown, with a piece of thick leather covering the nape of the neck, with a narrow nose-guard; a perfect fit.

A helm.

"What's going on there, Hermeias?" someone called from a distance.

"Nothing," Hermeias answered, rising and brushing himself off. "A Dromos must've opened up on its own somewhere, and then a chariot... plowed right into me, the damn thing!"

And then, to Iolaos, in a hissing whisper:

"Don't take off the helm! If you're seen here, on Phlegra---you're done for! Understood?"

There was no need to say it twice.

Iolaos had already noticed that the Trickster was whispering without looking his way---not to be discreet, but because he could not see him either.

The Sovereign's helm---the one the rhapsodes sing of.

For even the very name "Hades" has, since time immemorial, meant "unseen."

...Five paces from Iolaos lay a dead man.

No, not a man.

Or was he?

An unnaturally large adolescent---ten years old? twelve? fifteen?!---who seemed at once a hideous god and a beautiful beast, whose face one could not look at without a shudder; it is like meeting the unknown, when you cannot tell whether to pray to it or flee from it.

Beside the boy's wounded body---which, if one did not look too closely, resembled more that of a human child---lay scattered arrows fletched with pale dove-gray feathers, and a bow.

A bow made of wood and horn.

"Eurytos?" Iolaos breathed, barely moving his lips, understanding that this corpse too was no longer Eurytos of Oichalia, but merely carrion.

The Trickster nodded.

Iolaos bent down, tilted the dead boy's head back, and stared for a long while at the slit throat.

Then he kicked with his sandal at the stiffening hand, its fist still clutching a knife.

"If that was Eurytos, then he killed himself before anyone else could," Iolaos said, feeling that he was about to break into a shout. "Dead men don't cut their own throats. Did you see his shade, Trickster?!"

"Really?" Hermeias asked in surprise, though it was unclear what about.

And Iolaos understood that only a miracle was keeping the youthful god on his feet.

He went over to the swaying Hermeias, put an arm around him---the Trickster, with a sigh of gratitude, leaned into the offered shoulder---and swept his gaze over Phlegra.

The scorched plain, where the Family stood over the bodies of misshapen children.

Zeus Brotoloigos, who had crept like a thief into Alkmene's bedchamber and afterward allowed himself to meet Amphitryon face to face only once---when the lawagetas lay dying at Orchomenos; Poseidon Ennosigaios, who had stood in a Theban alley over the husband of the Thunderer's mistress as the man rose up from his knees; Hera Argeia, who had sent venomous serpents that, by sheer miracle, never reached two eight-month-old infants; Ares Enyalios, whose path a mortal grandson of Perseus had once blocked, knocking the god down into the bloody mud; Apollo Aigletes, an archer swift to punish, who had grappled with a raging Herakles at Delphi; the grimy smith Hephaistos, the merry drunkard Dionysos, the virgin huntress Artemis...

And wise Athena, who this very day would become Pallas[\*](#fn-73){.noteref}---there she was, bent over a dead Gigas, over a child named Pallas, briskly flaying the skin from the corpse with a curved blade, meaning to stretch it later over her shield.

The gods stood over the Gigantes; the surviving sacrifices---over the fallen priests.

Exhausted adults in torn garments---over the vanquished children.

And all of them, the living and the dead alike, were equally mortal.

A little way off, Alkeides was tearing his chiton into strips and binding up Iphikles's thigh where an arrow had pierced it.

When he finished, he helped his brother to his feet---and the twins, without looking back, made for the painfully throbbing Dromos.

Herakles was leaving Phlegra.

"They killed the children who nearly killed us," someone from the Family muttered after him.

Backing away toward the Dromos through which he had come, Iolaos watched the faces of the gods as they looked at the departing Herakles.

The faces of the saved, who would never forgive their savior.

"A hero stands alone..." an echo whispered over the Phlegraian Fields.

But Iolaos no longer heard it.

And even if he had, he would not yet have grasped the new, secret meaning folded into those familiar words.
