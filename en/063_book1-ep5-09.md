#### 9

Amphitryon had no strength left --- only experience and years, warring against each other; and he would have liked to know which would win out in the end. There was no time and no one left to give orders to; every reserve, the lawagetas himself included, had drowned in the snarling churn of battle, and only one thing remained to be done --- to parry and strike blindly, trusting the body and trying not to think. In order to survive. In order to not fall into the viscous quagmire from which there would be no rising again. In order to send yet another Orchomenian down the well-trodden road to Erebus. This one. And this one. And that one too, with the torn mouth. And another...

Amphitryon wedged himself into the narrow gap between two chariots that had collided, sealed himself off entirely behind his shield, and only now and then struck out short and vicious with the spear clenched in his right hand, catching with an old warrior's instinct the one instant when his leaf-bladed head could strike like a snake.

When the space around him opened up a little, he managed to fish a few javelins out of the mud --- and for a while kept the Minyans at bay, but the javelins soon ran out, and it all began again.

"Erginus!" rolled out very close by. "Er-gi-nus! Er-gi-nus!"

And a few dozen throats took up the cry.

The nearest Orchomenian turned --- and Amphitryon drove his spear into the exposed flank at once.

"Er-gi-nus! Forward, Orchomenus!"

Nothing but corpses lay heaped before the lawagetas now, and he risked a glance out from his shelter.

"Er-gi-nus! Er-gi-nus!..."

A chariot was racing across the swamp. It could not be --- and yet it was. Gilded, drawn by a pair of black horses, it trampled the quagmire as though a hard-packed road lay beneath its wheels, and the spattering mud would not cling to its gleaming plating.

And standing tall on the chariot was basileus Erginus himself --- who did not know that famous helmet of his?! --- striking with his spear left and right, driving the team all the while, heedless of the arrows and javelins whistling around him.

It seemed the Orchomenian basileus had grown fifteen years younger, and that his horses had sprouted wings.

"The gods promised not to interfere!" flashed through Amphitryon's mind; and at once the familiar voice of Hermeias said, "Only bloody Ares was absent from Olympus --- but who did not know Enyalius's opinion of any war?..."

Ares had not been on Olympus when the gods swore by the black waters of the Styx!

The Thebans broke ranks, staring wide-eyed at the chariot, while the Minyans, cheering each other on, were already massing around the halted black stallions, around the laughing false Erginus --- of that Amphitryon no longer had any doubt.

And from the hills came the heavy tread of six dozen shield-bearers, whom Pterelaos the Cyclops had miraculously pulled free of the fighting and sent to support the basileus.

If Pterelaos's shield-bearers managed to fight their way through to the chariot...

If a strong, unified fist formed here, at the center of the battle...

If...

Amphitryon sighed, threw down his shield, grown utterly unbearable to lift, and clambered over the heap of corpses piled before him.

"Stop! Get back! Stop, you old fool!" someone's voice howled inside his mind. Amphitryon was lying to himself, knowing full well that it was not one voice but two, one of them belonging to the trickster god in his winged sandals, the other his own --- the voice of that Amphitryon who wanted, madly, to live... ordering the voices, whoever they belonged to, to shut up, the lawagetas clambered over the barrier and, sinking knee-deep into the mud, walked toward the warrior in the familiar helmet.

* * *

...Oh, basileus Erginus was far from a fool! He had understood almost at once who had done him the honor of coming to Orchomenus's rescue --- and, naturally, he had no intention of spoiling the god's game, not when the god stood on his side, on the side of the prudent and pious basileus Erginus!

By then Erginus himself had long since lost his own helmet in the crush, his face was spattered with mud --- and, unrecognized by anyone, Erginus simply tried to keep close to his divine double, watching where the center of the battle shifted.

* * *

...An Orchomenian, plastered all over in stinking muck, loomed up before Amphitryon out of nowhere --- and vanished just as suddenly when the lawagetas silently drove his spear into the man's belly; but the shaft of the lodged spear snapped with a crack.

The warrior in the gleaming helmet was very close now. The disarmed lawagetas's eyes swept once more over the shifting quagmire, over a cart overturned on its side, its wheel torn loose... a wheel...

The mud gave a spiteful squelch, grudging to release its prize; Amphitryon froze for a moment, bent double at the waist, then with a desperate effort straightened up, hefting overhead the oaken, bronze-bound wheel --- weighing no less than a talent[^40] --- for one last throw.

[^40]: A unit of weight, roughly 26 kilograms.

...Erginus did not manage to notice where this stocky, no-longer-young Theban had sprung from, moving steadily toward the god saving Orchomenus on his gilded chariot.

Having silently driven his spear into someone's belly and stepped over the corpse, the Theban cast his eyes about --- and suddenly Erginus recognized his enemy!

He had seen him three times, when Amphitryon, on Creon of Thebes's orders, had escorted the tribute to Orchomenus; and once more, at the chariot races in Argos.

The accursed grandson of Perseus, who had lured him, basileus Erginus, into this treacherous swamp, took another step and, turning his back to the basileus, raised the battered wheel.

Erginus did not know whether a mortal could harm a god, but, looking at the Theban lawagetas, decided not to take the risk.

And he lunged forward, aiming the point of his blunted sword at Amphitryon's unprotected back, straight for the gap in his cracked armor.

* * *

...the pain struck suddenly and terribly. And that pain, as though in it the whole pain of his not-so-short life had come together, all of himself, already dead yet still living, all his contempt for gods and men who strike from behind --- all of it, everything he had, Amphitryon put into that wheel, which he now loved more than his wife, his children, his life, making the wood and bronze a part of himself, torn free with an animal roar.

As he fell, he had time to see the wheel strike the warrior in the gleaming helmet; and the god, flailing his arms absurdly, went flying into the mud.

Erginus groped frantically through the hot, sticky muck, trying to find his dropped sword --- to finish off, finish off the Theban! At last his fingers found the familiar hilt by some miracle; the basileus straightened up with a crooked smile, but some incomprehensible force flung him aside like a kitten, making him drop the weapon he had found with such difficulty.

...two hands, made of white-hot metal, jerked Amphitryon upward. And the lawagetas saw before him, through the slit of the horsehair-crested helmet, the fiercely burning eyes of Ares Enyalius.

"Too late," Amphitryon rasped with an effort, trying to smile at the ribbed visor of the helmet. "You're too late, god! You can finish me off... if you like..."

Blood, gushing from his throat, spattered Ares's helmet.

Ares lowered the choking lawagetas to the ground, slowly and gently, propping him against a nearby Minyan chariot.

Then he began pulling off his helmet.

He, Ares the outcast, was the god of war and he knew how to value his priests.

The battle around them fell silent in astonishment. Filthy, bloodied men stared wordlessly at the mortal and the god, not knowing who stood before them.

* * *

...Erginus snatched a spear from one of his warriors. The Theban lawagetas had to be finished off --- then the cry "Amphitryon is dead!" would tip the battle once and for all.

Who could say what notion might strike a fickle god's fancy? For it was not Ares but he, Erginus, who was basileus of proud Orchomenus!

Erginus drew back his arm to throw, swayed, took an unsteady step, and crumpled face-down, leaving all further cares to the living --- a long, feathered arrow-shaft jutted out from behind his ear.

The air shrieked again. Without turning, Ares caught the second arrow out of the air and stared in wild astonishment at his own palm, deeply scored by its head.

Wheeling around sharply, he saw on the far bank of the Cephissus a youth nocking the last arrow to the string of a barbarically long bow.

There he straightened and took aim, drawing the string back to his very ear --- he who had refused to be the driver of Ares's chariot.

And something else was moving in the seething waters of the river, below the crumbling barrier of stones, logs, and clay; as though the very god of the Cephissus had risen from the murky depths, wishing to come ashore.

One of the logs struck the stubborn swimmer, tossing him upward, and Ares saw the swimmer's face. It was the very same youth now drawing the bow on the bluff; it was the very same arrow. And there was so much fury and inexorable vengeance in that doubled face that Ares shuddered.

The next moment he staggered --- the third arrow struck the shoulder-plate of his cuirass, denting the metal.

Ares threw his head back to the sky and laughed.

Then the god looked down at Amphitryon, whose throat, choked with blood, rattled with every breath.

"You have good sons, lawagetas," Ares was not asking but stating it, and a strange smile wandered across his face. "Very good ones. Die, hero, and don't be afraid --- I, Ares, unloved son of a shortsighted father, know how to keep a secret..."

And he vanished, chariot and black stallions together, at the very moment the breathless Alcides tore free of the Cephissus's embrace and hauled himself onto the bank, shedding as he went the rope his brother had cast over him at the last moment.

The youth was a terrible sight --- beaten, soaked, utterly naked, his hair matted and smeared with clay, his eyes wild in a face pale as a corpse's.

Throwing the rope to one of the Thebans (who at once ran off toward a lone tree growing on the bank), Alcides wrenched a two-handed axe from some corpse and, roaring, hurled himself at Pterelaos's startled shield-bearers, hacking his way through toward the place where his father lay dying.

And the carnage, which had just begun to hush, found itself a purpose and a meaning once more.

A battle for the bodies of the leaders.

Some stocky warrior in a leather helmet studded with boar's tusks appeared beside Amphitryon, no one could say how. Simply flinging aside several Minyans, the warrior hooked Amphitryon --- who by now felt nothing at all --- under the arms with his right hand and, covering himself with an oval shield bearing the image of a snarling boar, dragged the lawagetas away.

Beside the stranger, almost at once, appeared the naked youth with his bloodied axe --- and when the recovering Minyans heard a cry from the direction of the Cephissus and turned, they saw, with superstitious horror, that very same youth again, only now, somehow, not with the axe but with a heavy spear snatched up along the way.

Even Pterelaos's chosen shield-bearers --- those still alive --- fell back: no one wished to fight the conqueror of the Cephissus, who seemed able to be in two places at once.

"Erginus is dead!" the warrior in the boar-tusked helmet bellowed, most opportunely; and that was the end of the war between Orchomenus and Thebes.

The Minyans began throwing down their weapons, surrendering.

"Is that you, Alcides?" the resourceful warrior asked wearily, pulling off his helmet and wiping the sweat from his brow. "And here I was hurrying... and still too late. Is that really you, Alcides?"

"It's me, teacher," Alcides answered, and looked steadily into Autolycus's hook-nosed face.
