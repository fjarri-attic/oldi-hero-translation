#### 6

If someone were to tell Creon that somewhere on the northeastern outskirts of the city (not far, as it happens, from the path leading to the hill already known to us) there stood a certain half-ruined little house, the basileus of Thebes would probably be quite astonished that anyone would bother him with such nonsense.

If the same little house were mentioned to any townsman living near the northeastern outskirts, that townsman would be no less astonished than Creon, since he had never once laid eyes on any such house.

If the same were reported to the solar Titan Helios, who once betrayed his Titan kin and went into the service of the Olympians, Helios (perhaps!) would rein in his fiery steeds for a moment, but would not be astonished.

Long ago the solar Titan Helios, eternal charioteer, forgot how to be astonished.

If it were told to the twins Alcides and Iphicles...

But why tell them anything? There they go, running, the soles of their sandals flashing, straight to that very house where their old friend Dummy is waiting for them.

Good for children: a year, two, two and a half --- and a chance acquaintance turns into a pal, and before long into an old pal... good for children!

Round the gnarled apricot tree with its fruit still green and horribly sour, now you must slow your steps, glance around --- and then run, run past the rotting hut to Dummy's house, paying no mind to the faint chill that rises somewhere in the stomach and vanishes again almost at once...

Only the piebald puppy, a foolish, perpetually hungry creature warming itself by the hut, sees the two boys, exchanging winks, dash right past and vanish as though they had never been --- but the puppy could not care less.

What it wants is a bone... and one that won't vanish anywhere.

By the house that was, in a manner of speaking, in Thebes, and at the same time, in a manner of speaking, was not, sat a dark-haired young man who resembled a very young Autolycus; eight-year-old Alcides and Iphicles called the young man Dummy, and he took not the slightest offense, since he had introduced himself to the twins by that very name in the first place.

This young man, in general, was very rarely offended by anything.

Perhaps because he was older than many an old man.

He sat with his eyes closed, leaning back against the shabby wall, his shapely legs sprawled out in crepidae sandals of superbly tanned leather --- and at the sight of him the twins' eyes lit with a predatory fire.

Their running became soundless (at least from the brothers' point of view), their fingers curled like claws (very fearsome claws!), one and the same grimace (the most terrifying in the world) twisted their identical faces, and, without quite reaching the young man, they leapt upon the sleeper like a beast upon its prey.

The prey, without opening its eyes, threw out its arms and caught the shrieking Alcides in midair, at the same time dodging Iphicles, who came thudding down beside it; then the prey neatly stacked one brother atop the other, rolled the resulting heap around in the sand, and rose beside it on all fours, snarling and baring its teeth viciously.

"Cer-r-rberus! I am the hellhound Cer-r-rberus!" the prey had really worked itself up. "I'll devour you, guts and all!.. grrr..."

It is doubtful that the souls in Hades behave, upon meeting the three-headed, dragon-tailed Cerberus, the way the brothers behaved just then --- for the beaming Alcides instantly leapt onto the hellhound's back, clutching its curly hair with both hands, while Iphicles, with a triumphant shriek, grabbed the self-proclaimed Cerberus by the leg in its splendid sandal and yanked it toward himself, sending the hellhound sprawling onto its belly with a piercing howl.

After all three of Cerberus's heads had been dutifully torn off, the dragon's tail tied in the most complicated knot imaginable, and the victorious heroes solemnly presented with their rewards --- a flatbread apiece and a handful of raisins each --- then came the turn of spiritual sustenance.

"A story!" the twins began whining. "Dummy, a story! You promised, remember... About Sisyphus!"

"No, about Tantalus!"

The brothers' opinion, united until now, split.

"About Sisyphus!"

"About Tantalus!"

"Get out of here with your Sisyphus!"

"And you --- with your Tantalus!"

"And you..."

Dummy cut the dispute short with two cuffs to the back of the head.

"I won't do Sisyphus," he declared firmly. "I'm sick of him. Tantalus is better."

"About how he was basileus of Sipylus," Iphicles picked up, "and the gods came to feast with him!"

"And how he feasted with the gods on Olympus," Alcides joined in, trying to shout over his brother. "And then stole nectar and ambrosia --- and handed them out to his friends!"

"And gave away the gods' secrets to them!"

"And told Zeus that his lot, the lot of Tantalus, was finer than the lot of the Olympians!"

"And stole Zeus's golden dog from the sanctuary on Crete! He talked Pandareus from Ephesus into it --- Pandareus carried the dog off, and Tantalus hid it!"

"And then swore a terrible oath that he'd never laid eyes on it!"

"And sacrificed his own son Pelops!"

"And fed his flesh to the gods!"

"And Zeus sent him --- to Hades! To eternal torment!"

"Serves him right! That'll teach him to feed the gods human flesh! Why, Demeter ate Pelops's shoulder --- and her stomach ached for it!"

Dummy watched the flushed, excited brothers with amusement.

"End of the story," he summed up. "You've told the whole thing yourselves already."

The twins exchanged a bewildered look.

"But Granny Eurythea says," Iphicles rattled off, as though suddenly remembering something, while stuffing the last of the raisins into his mouth, "that Tantalus wasn't basileus of the city of Sipylus at all, but the god of Mount Sipylus! And I told her she's old and doesn't understand anything! If Tantalus had been a god, he wouldn't have ended up in Hades! He'd have gone on lying... and gotten away with it. So there!"

Alcides sniffed doubtfully.

"Oh, sure! But if Tantalus wasn't a god, why didn't Zeus just strike him down with a thunderbolt right away? Back when he was stealing the nectar, or being rude to the Thunderer... why put up with it? Whenever I'm rude, I get it at once --- especially from Autolycus! You, Iphicles, always run off, and I catch it for both of us, just like Tantalus!"

"Don't quarrel," Hermeias the Dummy cut in. "You're both right. Tantalus was the god of Mount Sipylus once, he was... and then he wasn't. He became a mere basileus. Only the divine arrogance stayed with him --- and that's what he suffered for."

Now doubt seized both brothers at once.

"That can't happen," they declared in chorus. "A god is a god even in Hyperborea! Forever. You're lying, Dummy!"

Hermeias the Dummy said nothing, only smiled --- but it was not the smile the twins had grown used to, ever since meeting Dummy two and a half years before, in an alley near the marketplace.

"You're lying," Alcides repeated, less certainly now.

Iphicles fell silent altogether.

"You're lying," Alcides insisted. "Take Apollo --- his papa's a god, and his mama's a god too... a goddess, I mean! And Athena... Zeus gave birth to her right out of his head! And Hermes..."

"Hermes's papa is a god," Iphicles backed his brother up. "Zeus is his papa! Just like Alcides's..."

And he broke off, snapping his mouth shut in fright.

"My papa is Amphitryon," Alcides said, bristling. "And I'll clout you one! I've got my own papa, and Hermes has got his own..."

"And Hermes's mama is a nymph," Dummy said, a little sadly. "The nymph Maia Pleiades, daughter of Atlas. And Dionysus's papa is a god, but his mama is an ordinary woman, Semele, the foolish daughter of Cadmus, founder of your own city! And Dionysus isn't the only one..."

"Then how do you tell a god from a not-god?" Alcides blinked, forgetting the painful question of parentage. "If that's how it is, what's the difference?"

"That part's simple, actually," Dummy winked at both of them. "Look: say the two of you are basileis of the city of Sipylus. And I am the god of Mount Sipylus. And I ask you --- whose city is this?"

"Ours," the twins answered without hesitation.

"And whose is the palace?"

"Mine!" Alcides shouted instantly; Iphicles only nodded.

"And whose are the roads in the city?"

"Mine," this time Iphicles got there first.

"And the people?"

"Mine!" the twins began eyeing each other with distrust and jealousy.

"And Mount Sipylus, by which the city stands?"

"Mine!"

"No, mine!"

"No, mine!"

"Then I'll declare war on you!"

"And I'll... I'll..."

"There, you see --- that's exactly why you're men," Dummy shook his head. "Only a man says: 'This is me, and that is mine!' And is ready to kill for it. But the god of Mount Sipylus would say something quite different..."

Silence. A tense, attentive silence.

"A god would say: 'This is me --- and this mountain is also me! Every stone on it is me, every bush is me, the ravine is me, the chasm is me, the stream in the cleft is me, the streambed is me!' That is what a god would say..."

"And Zeus strikes him with a thunderbolt!" Iphicles shouted. "Right, Dummy?"

"Strikes whom, little one? If there's an 'I' and there's a 'mine' --- then, sure, a thunderbolt will do... Fire, thunder --- and the 'I' is gone, and the 'mine' becomes 'no one's,' and before long 'someone else's'! But if everything is 'I,' then whom do you strike with the thunderbolt? The stones, the stream, the birds, the bushes, the ravine --- whom? The mountain --- with a thunderbolt?"

"The mountain!" they both shouted.

"The whole mountain?"

"All of it! Wipe it off the face of the earth! Crack --- and it's gone!.."

"Wipe it off the face of the earth? But the goddess Gaia says of the earth: 'The earth is me!' And the mountain stream flows into the river Cephissus, and the river's god says: 'The river is me!' The water in the river, the reeds along its banks, the shallows, the rapids, the pebbles --- me!"

"Then him too, with a thunderbolt," the twins mumbled uncertainly. "And Gaia --- with a thunderbolt... everyone --- with a thunderbolt..."

Hermeias said nothing.

"The whole world --- with a thunderbolt?" Hermeias kept silent. "Over one mountain? Will there be thunderbolts enough?.."

"So that's why Zeus didn't touch Tantalus while he was still a god!" it burst out of Iphicles. "I am the mountain, the stones, the bushes... you can't strike the whole world with a thunderbolt over just one me!"

"And then he stole the golden dog," Alcides laid a hand on his brother's scraped knee. "And said of the dog: 'mine'... And of his lot he said: 'mine.' My lot is finer than the lot of the Olympians! And that's how he turned from a god into just a basileus... He didn't spare his own son --- a son isn't 'me,' a son is 'mine,' why spare him? Sacrifice him!.."

Iphicles looked around with an inexplicable tremor.

"Is this house mine?" he seemed to be tasting the words. "Is this house me? Is the tree me? Is the flatbread me?"

No. It sounded foolish, foolish, wrong, not right...

The boy's gaze fell on his brother, and something kindled deep within it.

"You are me," Iphicles slapped Alcides joyfully on the shoulder. "Look at yourself, Alcides! Just look at yourself! You are me! Understand?"

"I'm looking," Alcides did not tear his eyes from Iphicles's face, "I'm looking... at myself. You are me! Right?"

"Right!"

"Right," Dummy agreed. "You have no idea, lads, just how right that is. Now then --- let's go fight!"

"Not by the rules?" the twins' delight knew no bounds.

"Of course," Dummy confirmed. "How else? This isn't the palaestra, heroes..."

And they went off to fight, not by the rules.
