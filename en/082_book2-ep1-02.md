#### 2

[I wonder, is it a good thing or not so good, being a layabout?]{.thoughts} Iolaos smirked, shifting to a more comfortable grip on the reins, and turned the chariot to skirt Thebes toward the coast of Attike, intending to reach the harbor of Oropos within a day or two.

The thought had been nagging at him for about three months now---ever since the moment when Iolaos had spat on the feet of the herald Kopreus Pelopid[\*](#fn-46){.noteref}, who, with a sour look on his face, had proclaimed the end of Herakles's service to the Mycenaean wanax Eurystheus.

The hypocritical coward Kopreus had backed away, the soldiers escorting him had assumed expressions of prudent blankness (the dekarchos even got something in both eyes at once), while Alkeides and Iphikles had burst into deafening laughter and simply walked off, each throwing an arm around one of Iolaos's shoulders.

Kopreus, of course, will never forgive it... but who cares. All the more so since Mycenae, spoiled rotten, had no idea what it owed the twins in the first place; to them Herakles wasn't a hero in a lion's skin, and in a way wasn't even a living being at all.

He was unpaid hired labor and a symbol of prosperity.

It was no accident that the cautious weakling Eurystheus had even forbidden Herakles to set foot inside the city---many believed it was out of cowardice, but Iolaos knew it wasn't---and communicated with his servant through Kopreus.

And the latter never once failed to make it clear who was who.

Meaning: who's the herald of the great wanax around here, and who's just some Herakles fellow!

There was nothing to do but endure; and, unnoticed by Mycenae's toadies, to quietly turn Tiryns---Herakles's residence---into an impregnable fortress capable of withstanding any siege.

The first five years of forced service and six completed labors had been more than enough for Iolaos to understand: gold-rich Mycenae was a foul city, but it was his homeland; and it was also the linchpin of someone's secret designs.

Mycenae---the most influential center of Hellas---was invisibly embraced by a semicircle of ancient Dromoi that separated the city and the Argolid basin from the rest of the Peloponnese---Lakonia, harsh Arkadia, low-lying Elis, the fertile north of Messenia, and the coast of Achaia. This semicircle pulsed unevenly, as though trying to shake Mycenae off into the sea, and from time to time it let loose monstrous visitors (the local population called them boars, lions, or birds, simply for lack of other names), who sooner or later had to be bought off.

Bought off with people.

And somewhere near every Dromos there was invariably one of the Tartaros-Possessed hanging about---some character of the most innocent appearance, like poor Molorchos, the Nemean shepherd, who obligingly prepared to sacrifice his own grandson to Herakles, or the hospitable centaur Pholos of Pholoe, who first tried to get Alkeides drunk and then set a whole mob of his kinsmen, driven wild by the smell of wine, on the twins.

At the prompting of this "someone," the exhausted Arkadians or Eleians would invariably hit upon a fine idea: the monster is on a rampage, the gods are silent, the heroes are off gallivanting somewhere---so wouldn't it be better if one man in a hundred, chosen by lot, voluntarily... and so on and so forth.

By general consensus, it would indeed be better.

Iolaos had once even sketched a map right in the sand, since stubborn Hermeias, owing to the peculiarities of divine thinking, absolutely refused to believe that any one particular city had been singled out.

Here's Mycenae. Here, to the south, Elaious and Lerna with its remarkable Hydra; to the southwest, Nemea with its invulnerable lion; then, farther northwest, Psophis, plagued by the raids of a gigantic boar, at the foot of Mount Erymanthos; farther north still, the marshes of Stymphalos with their sweet little birds, encased in more metal than the Orchomenian shield-bearers...

"What about Augeias?" The Trickster persisted. "The Eleian basileus Augeias, with his shitty stables? Is he a monster too?!"

"Ask his subjects that," Iolaos said irritably, sweeping the sand into a heap, and Hermeias fell silent.

In exactly the same way, wrathful Artemis had once fallen silent, swallowing every reproach over the capture of the elusive Keryneian Hind---whose fault was it, after all, that the huntress-goddess had never before found the time to look at the creature those idiot Arkadians had consecrated to her! But now, having seen the viciously snorting beast with metal horns and hooves that Herakles had caught, Apollo's sister instantly realized that, in the end, she herself would have had to... and she herself hadn't much wanted to.

The hind had turned out just too dreadful; it was just as well she hadn't managed to produce any offspring.

So after Herakles had dispatched yet another monster along with its local Possessed, it would be Iolaos's and Hermeias's turn. The youthful god would drag the struggling shade of the Possessed off to Hades, after which he'd seal the now ownerless Dromos shut, hammering it fast; and Iolaos, for his part, would teach the population to lay bleating, terrified rams on the altar, instead of leading their own children, or travelers they'd caught, off into the woods and swamps.

As a rule, one of the twins would stand nearby at such times, idly toying with a club, which considerably strengthened the persuasiveness of Iolaos's speeches.

Then the Trickster would vanish, the brothers and Iolaos would return to Tiryns---and other envoys of Mycenae would arrive to take their place, headed by the omnipresent herald Kopreus.

A hero, too, in his own way.

Pay up, Peloponnese, for the labors of Herakles, slave of Mycenae!

The death of the Nemean lion brought Eurystheus a share in the local copper mines; the Lernaian Hydra, another unobstructed outlet onto the Argolid Gulf; the Keryneian Hind, the gratitude of Arkadia's sheep-herders; the Erymanthian Boar and the Augean stables, a tithe of Elis's rich harvests and of tightfisted Augeias's herds...

Later Mycenae's appetite grew, and the Peloponnese became too small for it---Eurystheus set his sights on wealthy Crete.

Iolaos would remember for the rest of his life how he, Alkeides, and young Theseus---who had volunteered as a sacrifice to the Minotaur for just that reason---had smashed the Cretan Labyrinth to bits. Human bones crunched underfoot, smoke stung the eyes, a whole brood of the Tartaros-Possessed fiercely defended their stronghold, dying beside altars shaped like bulls' heads, and Iphikles was sorely missed---he'd had to be sent back to the mainland, sent off pointedly, noisily, so that everyone would remember Herakles's departure on the eve of the Cretan rout.

Later the Mycenaean fleet turned up off the shores of Crete as if by chance, and that same Kopreus got down to work[\*](#fn-47){.noteref}, but none of that interested Iolaos, because the Labyrinth's Dromoi had already been sealed by Hermeias.

And the gash in his side had almost healed.

Two of the Tartaros-Possessed who'd miraculously slipped away were run down half a year later by Theseus, right there in Attike---and, unfortunately, he made rather too much noise about it, so that the Achaians' memory retained more than it should have: that Herakles had brought back the Cretan Bull, and that Theseus, later, returning from the Minotaur, had killed the beast near Marathon.

Fortunately, no one had yet thought to connect the two events.

Next came Thrace, where the interests of Herakles and Eurystheus very nearly coincided: the former needed Diomedes's man-eating mares, the latter, breeding stallions of the Thracian herds; after that, the Amazons of Themiskyra and their custom of sacrificing the weaker half of humanity---meaning men; the far-flung expeditions for the cattle of Geryon and the apples of the Hesperides---and it was here that the twins flatly refused to take Iolaos along, citing his inability to move quickly from one Dromos to another... then, the taming of three-headed Kerberos, that sweetest of souls, the hound guarding the way out of Hades---a task which, with a little help from Hermeias, some affection, and a sack of marrow bones, turned out to be no great feat at all...

And then, suddenly, it all ended.

Completely.

And Hermeias had hinted that it might do Herakles some good to rest for a while.

Rest very quietly, as befits a free man after honest labors.

Sitting in Tiryns, resting very quietly, and going dull-witted with boredom, Iolaos suddenly caught himself, with horror, listening indifferently to the high-flown nonsense that wandering rhapsodes sang at every crossroads.

Nonsense about the labors of Herakles.

The twins, though, actually found it amusing.

Something inside him had suddenly snapped, some string; everything had turned bland and meaningless---like, for instance, the arrival in Tiryns of Alkmene, banished from Thebes together with her husband Rhadamanthys and her quarrelsome daughter-in-law Megara... Iolaos met Alkmene with calm cordiality, the way a dutiful grandson ought to greet a frail old grandmother; and that calm was not put on but genuine, allowing him to talk of nothing at all and smile with his lips alone, while waiting out the fits of sudden drowsiness that overcame Alkmene.

For some reason, and to his own surprise, he remembered how he had taught the twins never to call him father, or Amphitryon---only Iolaos.

And he regretted it.

* * *

...Behind him, Lichas stirred.

Listening to him fidget, Iolaos recalled how they'd picked up this boy---or rather, this walking nuisance---in Thrace. Back then, the teeth of Diomedes's accursed mares had all but claimed Lichas, and every now and then Iolaos regretted that Herakles hadn't arrived in Thrace a little later, letting the mares snack on Lichas first.

But Alkeides had forbidden chasing off the pestering little vagabond, and the boy had tagged along after them, grinning happily whenever Alkeides called him his herald.

"My very own little Hermes," Alkeides would say, laughing.

Iphikles, too, had taken a liking to restless Lichas, so Iolaos had resigned himself and said nothing.

Though he was often ready to drown this little Hermes of Alkeides's in the nearest puddle.

"Well, what's going on in Thebes?" Iolaos asked without turning around.

"Swine," came the reply from behind him.

"All of them swine?" Iolaos said with a smirk, urging the horses to a quicker pace.

"All of them. All you hear is how they wiped Herakles's nose for him back when he was little. But tell them the truth, and they laugh. That wolf-cur Lykos, self-appointed basileus, has climbed right up on their backs---and they tolerate it... I mean, they tolerated it. Like I said, swine..."

Iolaos didn't like that last slip of the tongue one bit.

"Tolerated, or tolerate?" he asked again, sternly.

"Well, tolerated... why?"

"I'm listening," Iolaos said curtly.

Like the lash of a whip.

Drawn back to cut.

"What's there to listen to?... So, I climbed into this Lykos's thalamos"---Iolaos nearly dropped the reins---"figured I'd stir up some trouble! Teach him not to go banishing Herakles's kin! The guards were sleeping off their drink, you could've driven a chariot right through---and there's Lykos, asleep. Face all blue and puffy, reeking of wine so bad you'd want a bite to chase it down! So I took a piece of charcoal from the hearth and wrote on the wall..."

"Wrote what?"

"Nothing much. A dog's death for a dog, or something. Then I go to leave, and Lykos lets out this snore, jumps up---and flops back down. Just lies there. He's lying, and I'm standing. That's it, I think, I'm done for. But he's just lying. I'm standing, and he's lying. And I---"

"And you're standing," Iolaos ground out through his teeth. "Go on!"

"And then I got tired of standing. I lunged at him---and he wasn't even breathing anymore. Just foam on his lips. Poisoned, or something? Well, I wiped my hands on the blanket and left."

"And that's it?"

Lichas blew his nose noisily and said nothing, studying the sparse wild olive trees on the surrounding hills.

"Is that it or isn't it, I'm asking you?!"

"See, you're always yelling at me, Iolie," Lichas muttered, with some wariness, "and it's not even my fault! I didn't write anything else, except maybe I signed it at the bottom..."

"Signed it how?"

"Nothing special. Just, you know, 'Herakles.' And nothing else."

Iolaos only grunted and bit his lip.

"But now everyone's going to thank Herakles for it," Lichas said, trying to justify himself. "They don't even know yet, back in Thebes, that our service to Eurystheus is over! By the way, how's that megaira Megara doing back in Tiryns? Now there's a woman---only Herakles could handle one like that! The bosom, the hips, the temper! Worse than the Hydra..."

Iolaos was always amazed at Lichas's knack for jumping from one subject to another.

"Megara has nothing to do with it," Iolaos cut the boy off. "Herakles gave Megara to me."

"For good?" Lichas marveled. "Gods, to do that to your own kin..."

"If she survives---for good."

Lichas actually squealed with delight.

It seemed he regarded Iolaos as something like a tamed monster.

"And you handled her?! Come on, tell me, Iolie! I mean, even Herakles himself..."

"Your Herakles is a doormat." Iolaos thawed. "Never did know how to handle women. Wrong time for the beating, wrong way for the bedding. Not like me: first I took the reins to that bitch from morning till evening, then to bed---from evening till morning! On the third day I come home: table set, bed made, and the reins hung in a place of honor, all but mounted in gold! Now that's a labor for you! But who's going to appreciate it?"

"I'll appreciate it," honest Lichas promised. "So, are we off to Euboia to get Alkeides married off again? Or did Iphikles suddenly get the urge?"

"What do you mean, 'we'?! You, in any case, aren't going anywhere! You're staying in Oropos!"

"It's just, Iolie, you don't know yet that I'm coming with you to Euboia," Lichas objected in a sleepy voice. "And Alkeides and Iphikles don't know either... never mind, they'll find out soon enough. Who ever heard of a wedding without me!"

"True enough." Iolaos sighed, resigned.

Sometimes he seriously suspected that Lichas was a bastard son of one of the Olympians, specially trained to make life miserable for him and the twins.

The child had turned out just too obnoxious.
