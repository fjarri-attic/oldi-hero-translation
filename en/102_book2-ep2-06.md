#### 6

...Over the stormy Aegean Sea sailed the ships to the west, heavily burdened with plunder; at the oars sat the heroes, heavily burdened with thought...

And each was occupied with thoughts of his own.

Most of the warriors were reckoning aloud what to spend their shares on; their eyes shone with joy, clouding over only now and then, when the faces of fallen comrades rose up in memory.

Mighty Telamon kept feeling at his split chin from time to time and smiling happily with swollen lips: devoted to Heracles with a child's whole-hearted admiration, he was ready to endure anything at all from the son of Zeus and his mortal brother --- besides, just before they set sail Heracles had given Telamon the Trojan princess Hesione as a bride, singling the old comrade out above all the others.

Oicles of Argos tossed in delirium, and the thoughts of the gravely wounded son of Amphiaraus the Soothsayer were known to no one --- not even to himself.

Iphicles pulled at his oar and thought of Podarces, youngest of Laomedon's sons and the only one left alive; having passed through Phlegrae, the forced lover of the Fallen's descendants, sold and betrayed by his own father, looked upon the world with an old man's eyes --- and had wisely agreed to take the Trojan throne, not yet cooled, for which it had been necessary to stage a whole show of ransom, turning Podarces into Priam.[^66]

[^66]: Laomedon's youngest son Podarces, before becoming king of Troy, was ransomed out of formal slavery by his sister Hesione, who gave up her peplos for her brother; Podarces means "given," Priam means "ransomed."

Lichas daydreamed about how, back home, he would tell the seasoned heroes burning with envy that they hadn't been taken to Troy: "You? To Troy?! You'd spend a year just getting ready, two more sailing there, then sit under the walls for nine years, tearing each other's throats out, and if you did take the city, it would only be by a miracle! No, thank you --- we, Heracles's companions, don't care for waiting!..."

The ships fared on over the stormy Aegean Sea; heavy in the west the shaggy clouds were gathering, threatening the seafarers with storm...

Iolaus frowned, puzzled by Hermeias's absence --- the Trickster had sworn he would be in Troy without fail, yet he still hadn't shown up; and without the god Psychopompos, there was no way to learn where the soul of Laomedon, slain on Heracles's altar, had gone, or whether it had followed the shade of Eurytus the Possessed.

Alcides was keeping silent.

After three years of servitude to Omphale, he had grown remarkably taciturn altogether.

Over the stormy Aegean Sea sailed the ships, until there arose a terrible storm, calling up contrary winds from every part of the world; sea and land alike a sudden dark cloud wrapped, and down from the threatening sky came heavy night.

The first gust of a strangely cold northwesterly wind --- the joint handiwork of puff-cheeked Zephyrus and warlike Boreas --- came out of nowhere, showering the half-naked oarsmen with salt spray and the piercing breath of far-off snows; then it went tearing off again, ruffling the foam-capped crests of the waves.

A brief lull that fooled no one any longer --- and then the squall struck with its full force; the ships, already halfway through their crossing, began to be swept irresistibly off course, the frenzy of the maddened elements playing with the plaintively creaking shells of the hulls, laughing at the futile efforts of oarsmen and helmsmen alike, carrying the ships wherever the winds and waves saw fit --- and not at all where the exhausted men wished to arrive.

The sky, gone dark in an instant, was overcast with clouds; thunder growled hoarsely at some unseen enemy, zigzags of lightning stitched the sky to the sea, lighting up with a deathly glow the coastal cliffs that flashed by on the port side.

"Mykonos, is it?!" Iphicles shouted, trying to outdo the roar of the storm; he knew these waters fairly well. "Look how far we've been carried! At this rate, by morning we'll go crashing straight into Rhodes... and there it's cliff on cliff --- they won't even find our bones!"

Iolaus, standing next to the helmsman, only shrugged.

"Clearly that petty Olympian Family had a hand in this," he muttered soundlessly, gasping for breath. "Have they been at each other's throats up there, or what?!"

Iolaus was almost right.

"...So, Hermeias has brought good news: my beloved son Heracles is cleansed, having atoned with three years of servitude for his guilt before the Family and before mortals (a waste of time, if you ask me --- we would have forgiven him anyway, but never mind) and has valiantly taken impregnable Ilion. Answer me, Hermeias: is Heracles ready to march with the Family against the Gigantes?"

"I think so, dad."

"You think so --- or ready?!" The Thunderer's bushy brows drew together slightly, and somewhere on Earth there came a faint rumble of thunder.

"He is ready," the Trickster nodded, after a brief hesitation.

"Then it is time to march on the Phlegraean Fields! For even mortals marvel at the long-suffering patience of their gods --- especially after the insolent demands put forward by those bastards of Tartarus!"

"Oh sure, they marvel," Hermeias thought, while Zeus roused the Family with a sovereign speech, "of course they do... But where exactly do we know all this from? About the Gigantes' ultimatums, about how they want to put out the Sun, redraw the borders of the world, and are demanding the Olympian goddesses for wives?! Why, from those very same mortals! The Gigantes themselves haven't so much as poked their noses out of Phlegrae, they've sent no envoys --- yet all of Gaia is buzzing with rumors! Isn't this the Possessed's doing? What would the Gigantes want with our goddesses --- if it's for sacrifice to themselves, it makes no difference, Artemis or Ares!..."

"Thus, today the great day has come!" the Cloud-Gatherer proclaimed solemnly, summing up a speech the Trickster had let go in one ear and out the other. "Your opinion, god of war, firstborn son of mine?"

"I'm not quite sure I understood what exactly you're proposing, father," Ares spoke up, accepting a cup of ambrosia from the hands of his lovely little sister Hebe. "Take Heracles and break in on Phlegrae with the whole Family? Head-on? And what about the reserve?"

"Well, let's leave you behind then," sun-faced Apollo muttered irritably, vexed that his opinion hadn't been asked first; Ares was about to snap back, but Zeus's brows twitched, thunder rumbled somewhere very close by now, and the quarrel died in its infancy.

"Let's not quarrel," Ares set an example of peaceableness, "for tomorrow, perhaps, someone is fated to meet a final death..."

"Not tomorrow --- today," Zeus corrected him testily. "I did say that today the great day has come!"

"The great day is today," the god of war agreed, "but it's better to attack Phlegrae tomorrow. Remember, Father --- in your time you didn't always go charging straight ahead, never disdaining a bit of military cunning."

"Perhaps," the Thunderer allowed. "But back then I was pretty much alone --- no one to rely on, no one to talk things over with --- whereas now... Hermeias, cunning is your department; the floor is yours."

"Allow me instead, O most wise ones," Momus the Mocker --- son of Nyx the Night, and brother to both Thanatos the Death and Hypnos the Sleep --- elbowed aside the hesitating Trickster. "Everyone, the Gigantes included, knows how our gold-sandaled mistress Hera dotes on the Lone Cleaner, indulging him now with madness, now with something else... I said 'everyone knows,' not 'that's how it actually is' --- so there's no call for Hera's eyes to go flashing at me, a babbling jester; I'm not her husband, and she's no Medusa the Gorgon to me!"

"Get to the point, blabbermouth!" Zeus barked.

"At once, O Great One! So then, why shouldn't Hera, together with her beloved little brother Poseidon, stir up a modest storm for variety's sake --- one that will drive Heracles's ship wherever the waves please, that is to say, to the nearest Phlegraean Dromos, though outsiders needn't know any of that! There's malicious intent plain to see, the harm from it wouldn't hurt a fly, and we'll deliver our Cleaner straight where he's needed, nice and quiet!"

"Not bad," the Thunderer nodded approvingly. "Go on!"

"And next, you, High-Thunderer, will fly into a terrible rage at your jealous wife and hang her between sky and Gaia by chains --- silver ones, no, better make them golden! --- upside down (Hera tried to object, but Zeus cut his spouse off, apparently inspired by Momus's idea). At the same time, we'll let it be known to every priest, Pythia, seer, and marketplace beggar that a grand scandal has broken out in the Family --- they'll swallow it whole, wouldn't be the first time, would it?! Heracles at sea, Hera up in the air, Zeus in a rage, Hera's defenders knocked down to Earth with thunderbolts, scattering every which way... to the Phlegraean Dromoi, which we'll keep quiet about! That way word will reach the Gigantes too: the Family has no time for them, mired as it is in squabbles. They'll let their guard down, and come morning we'll strike Phlegrae! First on our own, and behind us --- father Zeus, with beloved Heracles and Nike the Victory!"

"Approved," --- even knowing that the counsel of Momus, truthful through lies, often led whoever followed it into a dead end, the Thunderer swept the Family with a meaningful glance. "Especially the part about the chains... now, where's the nearest Phlegraean Dromos here in the Aegean Sea? On the island of Kos? Well then, let's not delay. Ares in reserve --- that's what he gets for asking; Hephaestus, double time it to the forge for the chains; Momus the Mocker..."

"I'll run over to Hypnos with Ate[^68]," Momus said, rubbing his hands together, hiding the feverish gleam in his eyes. "Let dear brother send out some lying dreams --- it's no small thing, addling the heads of all Hellas! And you, Scepter-Bearer, why don't you get started?!"

[^68]: Goddess of deception.

Zeus nodded once again, thought a moment, and, somewhat uncertainly, let loose the first thunderbolt.

The second one went down considerably better.
