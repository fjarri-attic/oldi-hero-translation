#### 6

...Over the stormy Aegean the ships sailed on to the west, heavily burdened with plunder; there at the oars sat the heroes, heavily burdened with thought...

And each was occupied with thoughts of his own.

Most of the warriors were reckoning aloud what to spend their shares on; their eyes shone with joy, clouding over only now and then, when the faces of fallen comrades rose up in memory.

Mighty Telamon would now and then touch his split chin and smile happily with swollen lips: devoted to Herakles with a child's whole-hearted admiration, he was ready to endure anything at all from the son of Zeus and his mortal brother---besides, just before they set sail Herakles had given Telamon the Trojan princess Hesione as a bride, singling the old comrade out above all the others.

Oikles of Argos tossed in delirium, and the thoughts of the gravely wounded son of Amphiaraos the Soothsayer were known to no one---not even to himself.

Iphikles pulled at his oar and thought of Podarkes, youngest of Laomedon's sons and the only one left alive; having passed through Phlegra, this forced lover of the Fallen's descendants, sold and betrayed by his own father, now looked upon the world with an old man's eyes---and had wisely agreed to take the still-warm Trojan throne, which had required staging a mock ransom that turned Podarkes into Priam[\*](#fn-66){.noteref}.

Lichas daydreamed about how, back home, he would tell the seasoned heroes, burning with envy at not having been taken to Troy: "You? To Troy?! You'd spend a year just getting ready, two more sailing there, then sit under the walls for nine years, tearing each other's throats out, and if you did take the city, it would only be by a miracle! No, thank you---we, Herakles's companions, don't care for waiting!..."

Over the stormy Aegean the ships sailed on; in the west, the shaggy clouds heavily gathered, threatening seamen with tempest...

Iolaos frowned, puzzled by Hermeias's absence---the Trickster had sworn he would be in Troy without fail, yet he still hadn't shown up; and without the god Psychopompos, there was no way to learn where the soul of Laomedon, slain on Herakles's altar, had gone, or whether it had followed the shade of Eurytos the Possessed.

Alkeides was keeping silent.

Since his three years of servitude to Omphale, he had grown surprisingly taciturn.

Over the stormy Aegean the ships sailed on, till a terrible tempest arose, calling up contrary winds from every part of the world; a dark cloud suddenly covered the sea and the land, and down from the threatening heavens descended the heavy night.

The first gust of a strangely cold northwesterly wind---the joint handiwork of puff-cheeked Zephyros and warlike Boreas---came out of nowhere, showering the half-naked oarsmen with salt spray and the piercing breath of far-off snows; then it went tearing off again, ruffling the foam-capped crests of the waves.

A brief lull that fooled no one any longer---and then the squall struck with its full force; the ships, already halfway through their crossing, began drifting irresistibly off course, the frenzy of the maddened elements playing with the plaintively creaking nutshells, laughing at the futile efforts of oarsmen and helmsmen alike, carrying the ships wherever the winds and waves saw fit---and not at all where the exhausted men wished to arrive.

The sky, gone dark in an instant, was overcast with clouds; thunder growled hoarsely at some unseen enemy, zigzags of lightning stitched the sky to the sea, lighting up with a deathly glow the coastal cliffs that flashed by on the port side.

"Mykonos, is it?!" Iphikles shouted, trying to make himself heard over the roar of the storm; he knew these waters fairly well. "Look how far we've been carried! At this rate, by morning we'll go crashing straight into Rhodes... and it's nothing but rocks there---they won't even find our bones!"

Iolaos, standing next to the helmsman, only shrugged.

"Clearly that Olympian Bunch had a hand in this," he muttered soundlessly, gasping for breath. "Have they been at each other's throats up there, or what?!"

Iolaos was almost right.

* * *

"...So, Hermeias has brought good news: my beloved son Herakles is purified, having atoned with three years of servitude for his guilt before the Family and before mortals (a waste of time, if you ask me---we would have forgiven him anyway, but never mind) and has valiantly taken impregnable Ilion. Answer me, Hermeias: is Herakles ready to march with the Family against the Gigantes?"

"I think so, dad."

"You think so---or is he ready?!" The Thunderer's bushy brows drew together slightly, and somewhere on Earth there came a faint rumble of thunder.

"He is ready." The Trickster nodded after a brief hesitation.

"Then it is time to march on the Phlegraian Fields! For even mortals marvel at the long-suffering patience of their gods---especially after the insolent demands put forward by those bastards of Tartaros!"

[Oh sure, they marvel,]{.thoughts} Hermeias thought, while Zeus roused the Family with a kingly speech, [of course they do... But how do we even know about it? About the Gigantes' ultimatums, about how they want to put out the Sun, redraw the borders of the world, and are demanding the Olympian goddesses for wives?! Why, from those very same mortals! The Gigantes themselves haven't so much as poked their noses out of Phlegra, they've sent no envoys---yet all of Gaia is buzzing with rumors! Could this be the Possessed's doing? What would the Gigantes want with our goddesses---if it's for sacrifice to themselves, it makes no difference, Artemis or Ares!...]{.thoughts}

"And so, today the great day has come!" the Cloud-Gatherer proclaimed solemnly, summing up a speech that had gone in one of the Trickster's ears and out the other. "Your opinion, god of war, firstborn son of mine?"

"I didn't quite follow what you're proposing, father," Ares spoke up, accepting a cup of ambrosia from the hands of his lovely little sister Hebe. "Take Herakles and barge into Phlegra with the whole Family? Head-on? And what about the reserve?"

"That'll be you, then," sun-faced Apollo muttered irritably, vexed that his opinion hadn't been asked first; Ares was about to snap back, but Zeus's brows twitched, thunder rumbled this time very close by, and the quarrel was nipped in the bud.

"Let's not quarrel." Ares set a peaceable example. "For tomorrow, perhaps, some of us are fated to perish for good..."

"Not tomorrow---today," Zeus corrected him testily. "I did say that today the great day has come!"

"The great day is today," the god of war agreed, "but it's better to attack Phlegra tomorrow. Remember, father---in your day you didn't always go charging straight ahead; you weren't above a bit of military cunning."

"Perhaps," the Thunderer allowed. "But back then I was pretty much alone---no one to rely on, no one to talk things over with---whereas now... Hermeias, cunning is your department; the floor is yours."

"Allow me instead, O most wise ones." Momos the Mocker---son of Nyx the Night, and brother to both Thanatos the Death and Hypnos the Sleep---elbowed aside the hesitating Trickster. "Everyone, the Gigantes included, knows how our gold-sandaled mistress Hera dotes on the Lone Cleaner, indulging him now with madness, now with something else... I said 'everyone knows,' not 'that's how it actually is'---so there's no call for Hera's eyes to go flashing at me, a babbling jester; I'm not her husband, and she's no Medusa the Gorgon to me!"

"Get to the point, blabbermouth!" Zeus barked.

"At once, O Great One! So then, why shouldn't Hera, together with her beloved little brother Poseidon, stir up a modest storm for a change---one that will drive Herakles's ship wherever the waves please, that is to say, to the nearest Phlegraian Dromos, though outsiders needn't know any of that! The malice is there for all to see, the harm is next to nothing, and we'll deliver our Cleaner straight where he's needed, nice and quiet!"

"Not bad." The Thunderer nodded approvingly. "Go on!"

"And next, you, High-Thunderer, will fly into a terrible rage at your jealous wife and hang her between sky and Gaia by chains---silver ones, no, better make them golden!---upside down (Hera tried to object, but Zeus cut his spouse off, apparently inspired by Momos's idea). At the same time, we'll let it be known to every priest, Pythia, seer, and marketplace beggar that a grand scandal has broken out in the Family---they'll swallow it whole, wouldn't be the first time, would it?! Herakles at sea, Hera up in the air, Zeus in a rage, Hera's defenders knocked down to Earth with thunderbolts, scattering every which way... to the Phlegraian Dromoi, which we'll keep quiet about! That way word will reach the Gigantes too: the Family has no time for them, mired as it is in squabbles. They'll let their guard down, and come morning we'll strike Phlegra! First on our own, and behind us---Zeus the Father, with beloved Herakles and Nike the Victory!"

"Approved." Even knowing that the counsel of Momos, truthful through lies, often led whoever followed it into a dead end, the Thunderer swept the Family with a meaningful glance. "Especially the part about the chains... now, where's the nearest Phlegraian Dromos here in the Aegean Sea? On the island of Kos? Well then, let's not delay. Ares in reserve---that's what he gets for asking; Hephaistos, double time it to the forge for the chains; Momos the Mocker---"

"I'll run over to Hypnos with Ate[\*](#fn-68){.noteref}," Momos said, rubbing his hands, hiding the feverish gleam in his eyes. "Let my dear brother send out some lying dreams---it's no small thing, addling the heads of all Hellas! And you, Scepter-Bearer, why don't you get started?!"

Zeus nodded once again, thought a moment, and, somewhat uncertainly, let loose the first thunderbolt.

The second one went considerably better.
