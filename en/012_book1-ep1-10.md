#### 10

By nightfall they reached the outskirts of Kirra --- reached it thoroughly soaked but without incident, and the priests of the local temple of Apollo Aegletes[^14], catching sight of the sacred laurel wreaths, at once lodged everyone in the chambers adjoining the temple, set aside specifically for pilgrims.

[^14]: "Radiant" --- one of Apollo's epithets.

Quick-witted Thelem instantly picked out which of the priests was in charge, and went off with him, waving his arms and expounding to the bewildered old man something about the grandsons of Perseus, favorites of the gods, and suchlike matters. The old priest's bald pate reflected the starlight anxiously, Nasal held forth with great enthusiasm --- and when Thelem came back, behind him trailed a line of temple slaves, bent double under the weight of wicker baskets loaded with an assortment of food and wine amphorae.

Amphitryon made a show of scolding the extortionist, and, as it turned out, needlessly --- the priest's chief reason for such graciousness was not Nasal's speechmaking at all but the merchant galleys in Kirra's harbor, laden with Taphian tribute; so the bald servant of the Sun God had good reason to count on a handsome payoff for the temple.

Which he did not fail to declare to Amphitryon with disarming candor.

However, he chose a poor moment for it --- in the morning the grandson of Perseus woke with a savage ache in his back and palms scraped nearly to raw flesh, and to all the priest's hints he only grunted and scratched at his raw thigh, then sent Licymnius and seven soldiers down to the harbor, refusing the priest's innocent wish to send along a good dozen sturdy slaves as an escort with Licymnius.

The priest started to stammer something about Apollo's invisible arrows, which struck down not only wrongdoers but skinflints too, and about the possible ill omens the Pythia in Delphi might pronounce --- but Amphitryon, rather rudely, turned his back on the priest and walked off without another word.

Near the temple lay a small shaded grove where a spring gushed forth, said by legend to cure ninety-nine different ailments. Amphitryon suffered from no such abundance of afflictions, but on reaching the spring he gladly plunged his much-suffering hands into the icy water and knelt there a long while, eyes closed, savoring the peace of utter mindlessness.

"And solitude," he wanted to tell himself, but did not, because the moment of weakness passed, and the animal instinct that had saved Amphitryon more than once in his turbulent life told him to turn and look carefully around.

Some ten paces from him, half-hidden by the trunk of an ash tree and lush thickets of wild rose, an old man sat on a log as wrinkled as himself. A fat, flabby old man with a sagging, almost womanish chest --- which meant he really ought not to wear clothing that bared his left shoulder and part of his chest. He ought not to, and yet he did. Perhaps because he could not see himself. His sunken eyelids seemed to have long since grown together and caved deep into their sockets, into the place where eyes had once been, and where now there was only darkness and memory.

"I know you," Amphitryon said quietly, certain the old man could hear him perfectly well. "You're Tiresias, the famous seer, a native of Thebes. Creon himself once complained that it's precisely at home, in Thebes, that you turn up least of all --- especially when you're needed most."

The old man shrugged with complete indifference. A large fly, buzzing, landed on his cheek and briskly rubbed its forelegs together, but he did not even try to wave it off.

"After all," Amphitryon thought distantly, "if I can sit on the ground, and this blind man on a log, then why shouldn't a fly sit on his cheek?"

"You're Tiresias," Amphitryon repeated, shaking the glittering drops from his hands. "The seer. They say Athena blinded you for seeing her naked, but gave you the gift of foresight in exchange."

"So they say," Tiresias answered indifferently.

His voice turned out to be unexpectedly deep and resonant.

"And they also say Hera blinded you when, in her dispute with Zeus over who takes greater pleasure in love --- man or woman --- you sided with Zeus and said that a woman takes nine parts of the pleasure for herself, and a man only one part. Hera blinded you, and Zeus rewarded you with foresight."

"So they say," the old man answered in the same tone.

"Then which one's the truth?"

"I don't know."

"Well then," Amphitryon stood, brushed off his knees, and approached the ash tree behind which Tiresias sat, "in any case, you can't complain the gods have been unjust to you."

"Indeed I'm not complaining," the old man said calmly, gazing off to one side with the pits where his eyes should be. "The gods are just. I can even imagine a god who would take from you, Amphitryon Perseid, your home, your wife," (Amphitryon flinched) "your children and your possessions, afflict you with a hundred diseases, and then, as a reward for your patience, give you in exchange another home, another wife, other health, other children, other possessions... Yes, such a god would be just too. But would you say that this god is also kind?"

Amphitryon shook his head no, forgetting that Tiresias could not see him.

The blind man unhurriedly reached out his hand, his thick fingers finding a wild rose blossom unerringly, but Tiresias did not pluck it --- merely held it a moment and let it go, so that the bush swayed slightly, playing with patches of sunlight.

"And so are you too, grandson of Perseus, and so am I, a seeing blind man," the old man said quietly, "like a wildflower in the hand of the gods: they may stroke it, they may pluck it, or they may..."

"Or they may prick their finger on it," Amphitryon finished, jaw muscles rolling like stone under his cheeks.

"They may," Tiresias smirked. "I cannot see you with earthly eyes, proud grandson of a famous grandfather, but I can feel the breath of Ananke the Inevitability coming off you. If you wish to ask me something, ask, for I'll be gone soon. Or leave first and ask nothing."

"When will I die?" Amphitryon asked, unexpectedly even to himself, peering into the serene face of the blind man, overgrown with sparse, murky whitish hair that resembled lichen growing in caves.

"Not soon. Though unexpectedly, all the same."

"How will I die?"

"In battle. Men like you rarely die at home."

"Will I... will I have an heir?"

"Yes. But you will have to fight a god for him; and at first the god will win, and then --- you. I'll tell you no more than that."

"Who... who came to my house that night?!"

For some reason Amphitryon had no doubt that Tiresias knew exactly which night he meant.

The old man did not answer. He rose heavily from the log, slowly unbending his creaking knees, and stood still, leaning on a staff that had appeared from who knew where, gazing past Amphitryon's shoulder.

"I heard a din of voices," Tiresias at last unsealed his bloodless lips. "It came from Olympus. And the voice that thunders spoke and declared that a mortal woman of the line of Perseus the Gorgon-slayer would soon bear a great hero. Tell your wife, O prudent Amphitryon, that she carries the son of Zeus --- and the attempts on her life will cease."

Behind Amphitryon a stifled cry rang out. He turned quickly --- and saw Alcmene, white as snow, standing by the spring with her hands pressed to her chest in fright. Amphitryon rushed to his wife, gathered her up tightly and drew her against him --- as though he wanted to press Alcmene inside himself, into his wildly pounding heart.

"Did you hear?" the heart asked, huge and furious, deafening, ready to burst through his chest and spill out into the world as fire, burning it or building it anew.

"Yes."

"Do you believe his words?"

"Yes."

"I love you..."

"I love only you."

"I know."

* * *

...When Amphitryon looked again to where Tiresias had been standing, there was no one there anymore. The old ash tree stood motionless, a coolness breathed against his back from the spring whose water cured every illness except death and hatred, the disturbed wild rose blossom was smoothing out its pink petals, and the sky above the grove was bottomlessly blue and innocent like the gaze of a newborn god.

"Tell your wife, O prudent Amphitryon, that she carries the son of Zeus --- and the attempts on her life will cease..."

"Tiresias didn't say it," it seemed to Amphitryon that he was shouting, the way he had before that wild ride along the cliff's edge, but in truth his lips only moved without a sound, "he didn't say, 'Your wife carries the son of Zeus.' No, he said, 'Tell your wife... tell her --- and the attempts will cease!'"

"I'm afraid," Alcmene said, barely audible. "I'm so, so afraid..."

"Don't be," Amphitryon answered. "Zeus will guard you from all harm."

"And you?"

"Me too."

"That night," Alcmene pressed her forehead into her husband's shoulder, "that night... who was first with me? You --- or Him?"

"I," Amphitryon said distinctly, glad that his wife could not see his face just then, a face like that of a warrior whose open wound a physician is washing with sour wine, "I was first. I was first, and He was second. You carry the son of Zeus, woman of the line of Perseus. And you are guilty of nothing. So there's no need for us to go to Delphi."

"Was that loud enough, Tiresias?" he asked silently. "Loud enough for everyone who needs to hear it?!"

And he smiled, happy with the lie he had just told.
