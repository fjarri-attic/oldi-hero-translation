#### 4

Alcides scratched a mosquito-bitten cheek.

Then he turned his head and threw a single sidelong glance at the six travelers in dust-covered clothes who had appeared at the edge of the clearing.

He had long heard them approaching --- only a dead man could have failed to hear the six of them crashing through the forest.

The travelers, for their part, eyed the naked youth sprawled in the shade of a laurel with appraising looks, and were in no hurry to come closer.

At last the short man standing at the front, dressed more poorly than the rest --- evidently the guide --- arranged his pockmarked, flat face into an expression he considered pleasant, and stepped forward.

"Rejoice, goatherd!" he tossed off haughtily.

"Rejoice as well, goatherd," Alcides answered in the same tone, scraping up cheese crumbs and popping them into his mouth.

"I am no goatherd!" the short man took offense, tugging at his crudely stitched chlamys, pinned at the shoulder with a brooch set with large, obviously fake stones.

"Well then, rejoice that you're no goatherd," Alcides swallowed the crumbs and turned away.

The short man flushed crimson, but decided against picking a fight and tried to start the conversation over.

"I am Amphinomus of Orchomenus, son of Lycius, a Minyan," he declared, standing proudly with his hands on his hips. "And who are you, O worthy and quick-witted youth? If you're a god, we'll bring you an offering. If you're a man, we'll share a meal with you, for cheese crumbs are no fit food for a hungry, impudent stripling! Well?"

"Rejoice, Amphinomus, son of Lycius!" Alcides responded, more amicably now, sitting up. "I am Alcides, son of Amphitryon, a Theban."

A mischievous glint flashed in the pockmarked short man's eyes --- and he suddenly threw himself prostrate before the bewildered Alcides.

"Forgive our ignorance, O great hero!" the short man winked slyly at his smirking companions. "Orchomenus's envoys are we, bound for seven-gated Thebes to gather the tribute --- we knew you not, O mighty one! Us, messengers of basileus Erginus --- will you grant leave to continue upon our way?!"

"Granted," Alcides grunted, seeing perfectly well that the short man was mocking him. "To all four winds, for all I care."

"Let them have their fun," he thought. "It costs me nothing... I'll ask Pan later to lead them a merry chase around Cithaeron!"

The Minyans' hearty laughter only strengthened the youth's resolve to avoid a scene. Why start an empty, pointless brawl with the arrogant Minyans from the warlike city of Orchomenus?

The Minyans themselves, however, had no intention of giving up such a fortunate and amusing pastime. Settling themselves on the grass around Alcides, they inspected him without ceremony, as though he were merchandise in a shop, clicking their tongues in a businesslike way.

"We thank you for your permission, great hero," a dark-haired young fellow joined in, scarcely older than Alcides himself; the earring in his ear swung playfully, flashing with a chrysolite as green and gold-flecked as the one set in the ring on the Orchomenian's finger. "Would you be so gracious, godlike one, as to answer me, Proclus, nephew of basileus Erginus --- how many feats have you already managed to accomplish?"

Alcides did not answer, but no answer was required of him.

"Shame on you, Proclus!" spoke up a huge soldier with a scar across a nose remarkable in every respect (Alcides noted absently that the soldier had grown rather fat with age). "Why, our hero valiantly slew the fearsome villain Linus, brother of the monstrous Orpheus, who treacherously made our hero learn barbarous, blasphemous songs!"

The big man folded down one finger, and Alcides flinched as if slapped, but restrained himself.

"Then the pride of Hellas got a hundred daughters of basileus Thespius with child in a single night --- and Thespius himself for good measure! Now isn't that a feat?!"

The scar on the soldier's nose flushed crimson with pleasure, and a second finger was ostentatiously folded down.

"And finally," the witty giant pointed to the lion's hide lying nearby, "after a long battle our hero destroyed a field mouse that in these parts went by the name of the Man-Eater of Cithaeron! Is this not proof that our hero's father is none other than Dias the Thunderer himself, and not some Amphitryon fellow, mentioned by the great Alcides purely out of modesty!"

Alcides flashed his eyes menacingly, but thought it best to hold his tongue.

"And our esteemed hero's mother pleasured the Olympian so well that after her caresses he doesn't even want to look at other women! No doubt his mama herself, dreaming of Zeus with his thunderbolt at the ready, snores and drools in her sleep!"

The burst of laughter broke off abruptly: with a movement too quick to follow, Alcides reached out and touched, lightly, almost tenderly, the hairy forearm of the scar-nosed soldier.

"Say one more thing about my mother," Alcides said quietly but distinctly, "and you'll become handsome, because I'll tear off that filthy nose of yours. Understood?"

He meant to add something more, had even risen to his feet, when he suddenly felt with horror the first tongues of an alien, mildew-scented slime creeping inside him --- the harbingers of the coming of the mad goddess Lyssa and the indistinctly muttering Fallen.

"A fit," flashed through his mind. "How can this be?!"

"Well now, the hero spoke --- and scared himself!" the big man, who had involuntarily flinched back at first, looked the paled Alcides up and down. "One can tell at once he's godlike!"

"I... I need to go," the youth managed with difficulty, feeling the clearing swim before his eyes.

"You do," the scarred soldier agreed, baring a short curved sword. "And go you will, in a moment. But first..."

Neither Alcides nor the envoys of haughty Orchomenus knew that the sacrificial stone had already been chosen, the necessary words already spoken, that the slave boy rescued by Iphicles, indifferent to everything, lay stretched out face up, with a ritual stone knife raised above him; nor did they know that from a thorny thicket, the shrewd, keen eyes --- sharp beyond her years --- of a wizened old woman with the sharp little face of a weasel were watching them intently.

Galinthias, for her part, did not know that right beside her, invisible to mortals, stood a lean, broad-shouldered warrior in a breastplate that gleamed in the sun, greaves cast with lions' muzzles in massive silver, and a horsehair-crested helmet that all but completely concealed its wearer's face.

In the next instant, a great many things happened at once.

The curved sword seemed to migrate into Alcides's hand of its own accord.

The scar-nosed soldier cried out, grabbing at his broken wrist.

Helm-flashing Ares, son of Hera and Zeus, laughed.

The sacrificial knife came down, gripped this time in the fist of the limping servant.

Helios the Sun turned away from Cithaeron.

And Alcides, his reason slipping away, remembered that a hero must not flee from Tartarus.

A hero must fight.
