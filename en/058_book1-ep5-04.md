#### 4

Alkeides scratched his cheek where a mosquito had bitten him.

Then he turned his head and threw a single sidelong glance at the six travelers in dust-covered clothes who had appeared at the edge of the clearing.

He had been hearing them approach for some time---only a dead man could have failed to hear the six of them crashing through the forest.

The travelers, for their part, eyed the naked youth sprawled in the shade of a laurel with appraising looks, and were in no hurry to come closer.

At last the short man standing at the front, dressed more poorly than the rest---evidently the guide---arranged his flat, pockmarked face into an expression he considered pleasant, and stepped forward.

"Rejoice, goatherd!" he called out haughtily.

"Rejoice as well, goatherd," Alkeides answered in the same tone, scraping up cheese crumbs and popping them into his mouth.

"I am no goatherd!" The short man bristled, tugging at his crudely stitched chlamys pinned at the shoulder with a brooch set with large, obviously fake stones.

"Well then, rejoice that you're no goatherd." Alkeides swallowed the crumbs and turned away.

The short man flushed crimson, but decided against picking a fight and tried to start the conversation over.

"I am Amphinomos of Orchomenos, son of Lykios, a Minyan," he declared, standing proudly with his hands on his hips. "And who are you, O worthy and quick-witted youth? If you're a god, we'll make you an offering. If you're a man, we'll share a meal with you, for cheese crumbs are no fit food for a hungry, impudent stripling! Well?"

"Rejoice, Amphinomos, son of Lykios!" Alkeides responded, more amicably now, sitting up. "I am Alkeides, son of Amphitryon, a Theban."

A mischievous glint flashed in the pockmarked short man's eyes---and he suddenly threw himself prostrate before the bewildered Alkeides.

"Forgive our ignorance, O great hero!" The short man winked slyly at his smirking companions. "Orchomenos's envoys are we, bound for seven-gated Thebes to gather the tribute---we knew you not, O mighty one! Us, messengers of basileus Erginos---will you grant leave to continue upon our way?!"

"Granted," Alkeides grunted, seeing perfectly well that the short man was mocking him. "Go to the four winds, for all I care."

[Let them have their fun,]{.thoughts} he thought. [It costs me nothing... I'll ask Pan later to lead them a merry chase around Kithairon!]{.thoughts}

The Minyans' hearty laughter only strengthened the youth's resolve to avoid a scene. Why start an empty, pointless brawl with the arrogant Minyans from the warlike city of Orchomenos?

The Minyans themselves, however, had no intention of giving up such a promising and amusing pastime. Settling themselves on the grass around Alkeides, they inspected him without ceremony, as though he were merchandise in a shop, clicking their tongues in a businesslike way.

"We thank you for your permission, great hero," a dark-haired young fellow joined in, scarcely older than Alkeides himself; the earring in his ear swung playfully, flashing a chrysolite as green and gold-flecked as the one set in the ring on the Orchomenian's finger. "Would you be so gracious, godlike one, as to answer me, Proklos, nephew of basileus Erginos---how many feats have you already managed to accomplish?"

Alkeides did not answer, but no answer was required of him.

"Shame on you, Proklos!" spoke up a huge soldier with a scar across a nose remarkable in every respect (Alkeides noted absently that the soldier had grown rather fat with age). "Why, our hero valiantly slew the fearsome villain Linos, brother of the monstrous Orpheus, who treacherously made our hero learn barbarous, blasphemous songs!"

The big man folded down one finger, and Alkeides flinched as if slapped, but restrained himself.

"Then the pride of Hellas knocked up a hundred daughters of basileus Thespios in a single night---and Thespios himself for good measure! Now isn't that a feat?!"

The scar on the soldier's nose flushed crimson with pleasure, and a second finger was ostentatiously folded down.

"And finally"---the witty giant pointed to the lion's hide lying nearby---"after a long battle our hero destroyed a field mouse that in these parts went by the name of the Man-Eater of Kithairon! Is this not proof that our hero's father is none other than Dias the Thunderer himself, and not some Amphitryon fellow, mentioned by the great Alkeides purely out of modesty!"

Alkeides's eyes flashed menacingly, but he thought it best to hold his tongue.

"And our esteemed hero's mother pleasured the Olympian so well that after her caresses he doesn't even want to look at other women! No doubt his mama herself, seeing Zeus in her dreams with his thunderbolt at the ready, pants and drools!"

The burst of laughter broke off abruptly: with a movement too quick to follow, Alkeides reached out and touched, lightly, almost tenderly, the hairy forearm of the scar-nosed soldier.

"Say one more thing about my mother," Alkeides said quietly but distinctly, "and you'll become handsome, because I'll tear off that filthy nose of yours. Got it?"

He meant to add something more, had even risen to his feet, when, to his horror, he felt the first tongues of an otherworldly, mold-scented slime creeping inside him---harbingers of the mad goddess Lyssa and the incoherently muttering Fallen.

[A fit,]{.thoughts} flashed through his mind. [How can this be?!]{.thoughts}

"Well now, the hero spoke---and scared himself!" The big man, who had involuntarily flinched back at first, looked the now-pale Alkeides up and down. "You can tell right away he's godlike!"

"I... I need to go," the youth managed with difficulty, feeling the clearing swim before his eyes.

"You do," the scarred soldier agreed, drawing a short curved sword. "And go you will, in a moment. But first..."

Neither Alkeides nor the envoys of haughty Orchomenos knew that the sacrificial stone had already been chosen, the necessary words already spoken, that the slave boy rescued by Iphikles, indifferent to everything, lay stretched out face up, with a ritual stone knife raised above him; nor did they know that they were being closely watched from a blackthorn thicket by a wizened old woman with the sharp little face of a predatory weasel, whose beady eyes were far keener than her age would suggest.

Galinthias, for her part, did not know that right beside her, invisible to mortals, stood a lean, broad-shouldered warrior in a breastplate that gleamed in the sun, greaves adorned with lion heads cast in solid silver, and a horsehair-crested helmet that hid nearly all of its wearer's face.

In the next instant, a great many things happened at once.

The curved sword seemed to pass into Alkeides's hand of its own accord.

The scar-nosed soldier cried out, clutching his broken wrist.

Helm-flashing Ares, son of Hera and Zeus, laughed.

The sacrificial knife came down, gripped this time in the fist of the limping servant.

Helios the Sun turned away from Kithairon.

And Alkeides, his reason slipping away, remembered that a hero must not flee from Tartaros.

A hero must fight.
