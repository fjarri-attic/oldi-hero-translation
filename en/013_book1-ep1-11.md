#### 11

When Amphitryon and Alkmene returned to the temple---which did not happen anytime soon---agitation and bustle reigned there. Priests scurrying back and forth parted before Alkmene, casting fearful sidelong glances at her; temple slaves respectfully averted their eyes and furtively made warding signs; and the soldiers who had come with Amphitryon crowded in the courtyard, exchanging meaningful glances, their faces alight with delight and pride.

"What happened?" Amphitryon seized pale Likymnios by the shoulders and gave him a light shake (he knew no better method for loosening tongues; or rather, he did know one, but it wasn't suited for relatives).

Likymnios shook his head frantically and jumped back.

"I will tell you, O most venerable Amphitryon, what happened"---the bald priest, who had somehow turned up at his side, solemnly raised his right hand to the sky, at once coming to resemble a withered olive tree. "While you were graciously pleased to go to the spring, I was offering the morning sacrifices at the altar. And there I received a sign. Though at first I understood nothing of it."

"But you understood afterward?" Amphitryon inquired, barely restraining the urge to strangle the old fool.

"Not then either," the priest answered gravely. "It's simply that Teiresias of Thebes turned up afterward (I trust the name means something to you?!) and explained it all to me."

The priest looked at his right hand, raised heavenward, thought a moment, worked his thin lips, and threw up his left hand as well.

"Rejoice, O woman," he wailed at the top of his lungs, startling the slaves and the birds in the treetops, "rejoice, thou marked by Zeus, for---"

He never got to finish "for," because Amphitryon clapped his heavy palm over the priest's mouth. Strange as it may seem, everyone present found this perfectly natural---apparently the husband of a woman marked by Zeus was permitted a great deal.

"Never let me find you again, old sir, vociferous," Amphitryon began, for greater persuasiveness taking up the tongue of aoidoi, the wandering singers, not knowing that he had unknowingly foreshadowed, almost word for word, a saying of one of the future heroes of Hellas---one who would, in the end, be slain in his own bath by his wife and her lover. "So speak plainly now, do not make me angry; so you will be safer! Understood?"

The old man hastily bobbed his bald pate and gestured to show that he understood and would not do it again.

"Marked by Zeus..." came muffled from beneath the palm.

"That much we know ourselves," Amphitryon took his hand away and swept a grim look over the assembly. "What else did Teiresias say?"

"He said," the priest stammered, "he said... that the Thunderer had declared to the Olympians that from this hour on he would never again touch a mortal woman. Only it's unclear why..."

"Well, that part's clear enough," Amphitryon grunted.

The priest stared at him, bewildered.

"Because Zeus will never find a better woman!" Amphitryon raised his voice so it would carry to everyone. "Nowhere, ever! So beautiful, and... so chaste, that the god had to take her husband's form to achieve his end! Is that clear now, you dolts?!"

He looked them all over once more---soldiers, priests, slaves---letting his gaze linger a long while on each face.

It was clear to all of them.

It was perfectly clear to all of them.

"I'm hungry," Amphitryon stretched his whole body, like a man who has just finished a wearisome task. "Hey, Nasal, do your best..."

And gave the priest a condescending wink.
