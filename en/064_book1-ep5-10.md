#### 10

Amphitryon did not doubt for a moment that he died.

His whole body felt an extraordinary lightness; breathing was entirely unnecessary; the sun beat straight into his eyes, making him squint, and all around him, for some reason, rustled the sacred grove near Kirra, where willful fate had once, nineteen years ago, carried Amphitryon and a pregnant Alcmene on their way to the oracle of Delphi.

And at his very feet bubbled a wondrous spring, healing every affliction save death.

And in the shade of an old ash tree, absentmindedly toying with a wild-rose blossom that clung to his fingers, on that very log where the blind seer Tiresias once had sat, sat a stern man, only slightly heavier with age, who bore a striking resemblance to a forty-year-old Amphitryon.

"I know you" --- Amphitryon was not sure whether he had spoken this aloud or merely thought it --- "you are Zeus, son of Kronus, grandson of Uranus."

"And I know you," the man answered, or thought, slowly inclining his heavy, broad-browed head. "You are Amphitryon, son of Alcaeus, grandson of Perseus, my great-grandson. Rejoice, mortal: you died as you lived --- a hero."

"Rejoice as well, Olympian: one day you too will die as you lived --- a god."

Zeus studied Amphitryon searchingly, while he, in turn, examined the Kronid's palms: hard, calloused warrior's palms bearing strange marks of old burns.

"That must hurt like nothing else!" --- the lawagetas judged, without sympathy, but with understanding and respect.

"They all loved me," the god suddenly sighed. "At least, they said they loved me. All of them: Leda, Europa, Danae, Semele, Io --- and I repaid them in kind, scattering my seed as generously as a plowman scatters grain. I was a bull, a swan, a golden rain, a mighty and inscrutable divinity; only once did I try to become mortal, coming to your wife... I will never have children again, Perseid! Do you understand what that means --- never?!"

"Yes, I understand that, Kronid. I will never have anything again, ever. You shouldn't have tried on my hide --- you would have done better to come as a bull... and not to Alcmene. But Alcmene will weep for me, not for you. You scattered what you called love, believing devoutly in its inexhaustibility; you left me a rumpled kline and a weary wife, while I left her my lie, to save her life and the lives of the sons who called Alcmene mother and called me father, while you agonized over your spent seed! Here, in the grove near Kirra, blind Tiresias foretold to me that I would die in battle --- and I died in battle; that I would have an heir --- I have two, whatever men may say of it; that I would fight a god for my son, and that the god would win first, and then I would. Who won, Thunderer?"

The wild-rose blossom jerked on its supple stem and, pricking Zeus's finger, slipped free and swayed, playing with the pink flesh of its petals.

"You won, Amphitryon. First --- I, when you lied to your wife and to men; then --- you, when I kept silent for nineteen years, though our shared lie burned my heart worse than thunderbolts burn my hands... but Tiresias did not tell you the whole truth. Enjoy your victory, for you depart together with the lie, while I, Zeus the Olympian, father of the great hero Alcides --- remain! So, who comes out ahead now, mortal? You are dead, and even I can do nothing more for you. Perhaps my brother Hades will be kinder..."

Amphitryon was still looking at the now-empty log when someone laid a hand on his shoulder.

"Hermeias!" the old lawagetas exclaimed with joy, turning around. "What brings you here?"

"Come, Amphitryon" --- Hermeias kept carefully averting his eyes, like a thief caught in mischief --- "come, I'll guide you... it's not far."

"All right," Amphitryon agreed. "I do get it. If we must, then we must."

"No!" the Trickster suddenly cried out, tracing a circle around himself and Amphitryon with his caduceus, which hissed fearsomely. "No, not yet!... Let the whole Family rage at me for it afterward --- but you must see this! Look!"

And Amphitryon saw.

* * *

...He saw the bloody swamp, the filthy field of battle left behind him; he saw Alcides standing over his father's body --- over *his* body --- with Iphicles beside him; he saw, struck by it for the last time, how alike the brothers were to one another... he saw the wheel that had hurled Ares Enyalius to the ground, he saw the eyes of those still living, he heard the tremor in the voices of the captive Orchomenians as they recalled his name --- the name of Amphitryon, son of Alcaeus, grandson of Perseus, Amphitryon the Exile, Amphitryon the lawagetas...

And he heard the words of the son for whom he had fought against a god.

"I swear by my father" --- Alcides's voice rang taut as a bowstring, and the warriors exchanged glances at these words, casting sidelong looks now at the lawagetas's body, now at the sullen sky --- "I swear by my father that Ares will never get from me the sacrifice he wants! Never! And if, for that, war must forget my name, and I become its filth... then so be it! Do you hear me, father?!"

"I hear you, silly!" Amphitryon smiled. "I hear you, no need to shout... Hermeias, what do you think --- will he keep that oath?"

"I don't know."

"Nor do I... Well then, shall we go?"

"Let's go..."

* * *

Neither Hermeias nor Amphitryon knew then that Alcides would keep his oath for exactly five years --- until the day a priestess of the Delphic oracle would name him Heracles.

Nor did Alcides himself know it.
