#### 4

Many that day believed things were going hard for them; but a dekarchos of the Trojan guard at the Scaean Gates was convinced that no one had it worse than he did.

Even the one thing that had given him pleasure since early morning --- the absence of the second dekarchos, laid up with a snakebite --- now drove him to despair, since it saddled him with twice the responsibility while robbing him of anyone to consult.

And someone to consult, he wanted more than, on a hot day after two jugs of millet beer, a man wants to step aside and ease his soul --- which in men, as everyone knows, resides in the belly.

Judge for yourself --- first the tower sentries had spotted unfamiliar ships coming from the west, and at first thought nothing much of it, taking the vessels for merchantmen. A dozen or two trading tubs were forever milling about in the Trojan harbor; and to suppose that great Ilion could be attacked by so paltry a band of raiders, in broad daylight no less, would take a madman. Within the city walls stood upward of fifteen hundred well-armed soldiers; over the course of any siege, arming the townsfolk besides, the army could be swelled two- or threefold --- and reinforcements would come pouring in from all across the Troad, from the cities of Abydos and Arisbe, from Zeleia at the foot of wooded Ida[^62], from little Cilla, from allied Larisa and Lyrnessus... three ships, you say?

[^62]: A mountain range east of Troy.

Ha!

Still, when the ships, having rounded the island of Lemnos from the south and tiny Tenedos from the north, failed for some reason to turn toward the harbor and instead made straight for the shore, the dekarchos grew a little uneasy and decided to send a messenger to the ruler. On the one hand, Laomedon's hot-tempered, despotic nature (many called him a "living god," each meaning something different by it) was known to all, and a false alarm would mean trouble for the dekarchos; on the other hand...

And on that other hand, the prudent dekarchos turned out to be entirely right. Biting his lips in helpless frustration, he watched from the fortress wall as the plundering unfolded below; hastily gave the order to open the Scaean Gates the moment Laomedon's hurrying soldiers and chariots swung out of the surrounding streets; at his own risk let a crowd of shrieking refugees into Troy and only then ordered the gates shut; and finally, spitting curses, watched in full and unhappy detail the none-too-successful opening of the assault on the rampart.

Some of the refugees had already managed to spread through the city, and Troy began to resemble a pot coming to a boil over the fire. The rest crowded below, at the gates, and the guards had to keep driving back the most eager of them, forever trying to scramble up onto the wall or a tower. Standing out from the crowd was a company of ten mounted Thracians, who had ridden in for the fair just before the raid. The dekarchos had long had a fondness for Thracian horsemen --- though he himself neither liked nor trusted horses, even ones harnessed to a chariot --- and when one of the Thracians swung down off his mount and made for the stairway leading up to the wall, the dekarchos waved the guards aside: let him through!

Up close the Thracian proved unexpectedly massive and broad-shouldered; standing beside him felt calm and steady, the way it sometimes feels to stand beside a cliff. The dekarchos gave his guest a friendly wink and thought that, should things come to that, ten fellows like this one would hardly go amiss.

"Pirates?" the Thracian asked, loosening the winged hood that had been covering the lower half of his face.

A face utterly unfamiliar to the dekarchos. Good-natured, round-cheeked, with a shaved mustache and a soft chestnut beard.

"Yeah," the dekarchos said, thrusting out his lower lip in contempt. "Fools, the lot of them! Thought they'd take it by main force..."

"They're holding up well enough, though," the Thracian observed thoughtfully, pointing toward the fighting. "Might be some of them get away to sea yet."

"Not a chance!" the dekarchos said indignantly. "Look, there's more of ours running up... they'll flank them and cut every last one down!"

This last referred to the five hundred men racing toward the shore from the direction of the hill of Batieia --- the very ones for whom Laomedon had just thanked fate.

"Running, sure enough," the Thracian agreed good-naturedly. "Only --- are they yours?"

"Whose else would they be?" An unpleasant little snake of doubt crawled into the dekarchos's soul (which, as had already been noted, resided in his belly) and began to stir, coiling itself into cold rings. "If they're running from here to there, they must be ours!"

"From here, sure enough, to there," the Thracian said, oddly, and fell silent.

By now the running men had covered half the distance.

"Must be Arisbe sent help," the dekarchos said, feeling the little snake of doubt swell into a full-grown snake. "No, wait --- from there it's half a day one way, even at a run... Zeleia, maybe?"

"Not a chance!" The dragon of doubt hissed viciously, jabbing him painfully in the liver with its back spines. "Five hundred men, from Zeleia? And when would they even have heard of the raid?!"

"Fine hide, that," the Thracian blurted out, for no apparent reason. "Worn a bit thin, but a good hide all the same..."

"What hide?" the dekarchos asked mechanically, followed the direction of the Thracian's gaze, and suddenly understood what was meant.

He understood it an instant before the five hundred men of the supposed reinforcements, led by a giant in a lion skin thrown on over his armor, struck the Trojans storming the rampart from behind --- and the battle turned into a slaughter.

"Me and the lads, riding here from Lake Gygaea," the Thracian said, clapping the stunned dekarchos on the shoulder and grinning from ear to ear, "we kept wondering: now what's this half-dozen ships doing, putting in under the cover of that headland off Lesbos? Empty country out there... the only mercy is it's a stone's throw to Troy one way and to Maeonia the other! Well, now it's clear: great Heracles has served out his time with Omphale --- and he's taking strong-walled Troy!"

"Heracles," the dekarchos said, and it seemed to him that someone else, not he, was speaking the words, "Heracles is taking Troy..."

"There's a good lad!" the Thracian beamed. "Quick on the uptake! And Heracles, well, that's just how he is: whatever he sets out to take, he takes..."

The Thracian turned toward the crowd seething below.

"Hey, Lichas!" he bellowed at one of his own men. "You were right! It's Heracles! Drinks are on me!"

The one the Thracian was calling to --- a lanky, pole-thin youth of about twenty, bearing the common Thracian name Lichas --- dropped down at once and slipped in among the refugees.

"Heracles!" he shrieked piercingly, and for some reason his unpleasant voice reminded the dekarchos of a trip to Oechalia three years before, when the dekarchos had escorted Laomedon and his sons to the late basileus Eurytus. "Heracles is at the gates of Troy! Run for your lives!"

"Run for your lives!" the crowd took up the cry, raggedly, hurrying to pour into the lanes leading toward the city center --- and soon only the guards and the Thracians remained at the Scaean Gates.

"And what if you can't?" the dekarchos thought, with resignation.

"You'd best give the order to open the gates," the mighty Thracian's good humor knew no bounds. "There goes your Laomedon now... wouldn't do to leave a king shut out of his own city."

Coming to his senses, the dekarchos barked an order at the gate guards, who barely had time to haul the huge bronze-bound oaken beam that served as a bar out of its sockets before Laomedon's chariot came whirling in through the gates, with three more close behind it.

"Bar them! Bar the gates, you sons of whores!" Unable to hold back the horses, which had bolted at the bit, Laomedon barely managed to swerve into the lane leading to the central agora.[^63] "Bar them, damn you!"

[^63]: A square.

The chariots of the Trojan princes thundered past after him; the last one, tilting on the turn, overturned, its driver flung out to strike his head against the corner of a house and lie still, while the horses tore on with a shrill whinnying. One glance toward the coast was enough for the dekarchos to understand: the smallest delay in carrying out Laomedon's last order, and the accursed Achaeans, with the owner of the lion skin at their head, would come pouring into the city on the heels of the fleeing king, who had abandoned his shattered army.

Sensing that his men were wavering between flight and loyalty to their duty, the dekarchos drew a deep breath and made ready to rush down and personally take command of the defense of the Scaean Gates --- but his legs refused to serve the valiant warrior, and every order, unborn, died and began to rot right there in his chest, choking his breath.

Coming up the stone steps of the stairway to meet the dekarchos was the bar from the Scaean Gates.

"Out of the way," the bar tossed off, drawing level with the leather soles of the dekarchos's sandals, then moved on and upward; now it was level with the knees of the dekarchos, who had leapt back; now it had reached his belly... an arrow, loosed by a terrified sentry on the tower, screeches off the bronze sheathing of the oaken beam, but the bar continues steadily on its way.

The dekarchos can now see clearly the shoulders straining under the impossible weight, the knotted arms, palms braced against the worn bronze, the taut belly bristling with coarse black hair --- but the face of the man carrying the bar he cannot see.

Only the crown of his head; only curly hair, quite heavily touched with gray.

"Is... is that one of yours?" the dekarchos asks, blinking foolishly, backing toward the Thracian.

"One of ours, one of ours," the other agrees, watching the man with the bar with undisguised envy. "Believe me, friend: I'm his equal in height, and through the shoulders I might almost be broader than he is, and if I'm not a son of Zeus, then at the very least I'm a grandson[^64] --- and still I couldn't do it! Up the steps, with that brute on my neck... no, I couldn't."

[^64]: Through his father, the pious Aeacus, Telamon is a grandson of Zeus; through his mother, Endeis, a grandson of the centaur Chiron.

"Spare me the modesty, Telamon," a voice rumbles from beneath the bar; and then, to the dekarchos: "Want to live?"

"I do," the dekarchos answers honestly, after a moment's thought.

"Then step aside and shout to your men to stand down and stop shooting. Got it? Telamon, see to it..."

The Thracian's reliable cliff of a presence draws closer, turns out to be right at his side; the dekarchos's short sword slips from its scabbard of its own accord and, coming alive, gives its former master a flat-of-the-blade smack across the backside.

"Shall we?" Telamon asks cheerfully, and just then a wild crash makes them both jump.

The man who has just flung the bar down on the far side of the wall turns around --- and the dekarchos recognizes him.

They have met before: in Oechalia, at Eurytus's court.

Three years ago.

"Heracles is taking Troy," --- something has shifted hopelessly inside the dekarchos's head; he feels it, though he cannot say what it is, and he cannot for the life of him stop grinning. "Heracles is taking Troy..."

"Why is taking?" Telamon laughs, and below, the false Thracians laugh in reply, herding together the guards, who offer no resistance. "Why --- is taking?"

"Ah, right," the dekarchos corrects himself. "Has taken."
