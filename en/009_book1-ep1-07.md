#### 7

Talking Alcmene out of the journey to the oracle proved impossible --- the hereditary stubbornness of the Perseids had awoken in her, and Amphitryon knew from his own experience that further persuasion was pointless in such cases.

All the more so since Alcmene, five months along, felt wonderful, carried her noticeably rounded belly with stately dignity, and was in no other way different from the Alcmene of before --- save for an unexpected fondness for Rhodian smoked meats. As for Thessalian ones, after that incident at the market, she could not even bear to look at them.

Amphitryon agreed, reluctantly --- though he could not shake the thought that he was making a monstrous mistake, indulging his wife's whim to seek a prophecy from Apollo Delphinios, or from any other deity, one of whom had, in passing, out of a moment's idle curiosity, trampled their family happiness underfoot.

Alcmene, however, knew nothing of his torment and radiated joy, nestling up to her husband... while her husband avoided her, pleading business, afraid to let even a hint slip that might reveal to Alcmene the cause of his gloom --- and there was only one thing Amphitryon regretted.

That in all the long years of his life --- the life of a grandson of Perseus, son of one of the Mycenaean kings, a warrior, an unwilling killer, an exile, a husband, a warlord-lawagetas --- in all those years he had never learned two things.

To forgive insults and to take pride in another's charity.

He grew grim, cursed his own pride, and drank at night.

Once he let slip to Creon that he feared the journey to the oracle. Fortunately, wine had little hold on Amphitryon these days, and this time he managed to catch himself before his indiscreet confessions went too far --- and besides, Creon, basileus of Thebes and a statesman, understood him in his own way. Weren't there enough petty brigands running loose in Boeotia[^12]? The next day, bleary from lack of sleep, Amphitryon was horrified to learn that solicitous Creon had assigned them an escort of soldiers quite sufficient to take Delphi by force --- should such an idea ever occur to anyone at all.

[^12]: The central and most fertile region of Central Greece, with the capital in Thebes.

He had to decline as politely as possible, and personally select fifteen veterans of earlier campaigns, men ready to charge at anyone for Amphitryon's sake --- and, most importantly, capable of charging calmly and with good judgment.

A veteran is a warrior who survived, and that says it all.

Creon approved his friend's choice, and Amphitryon kept quiet about the fact that from the most tenacious survivors he had picked the most experienced, from the most experienced --- the most loyal, and from the most loyal --- the least superstitious, men able to keep from flinching at thunder from a clear blue sky, and fond of swearing by the more intimate body parts of the Olympians.

For some reason, this seemed very important to him.

On the sixth day of the month of Hecatombeon[^13], to the cheers of the crowd, a procession of three chariots drawn by pairs of Mitannian horses rolled out through the northwestern gates of Thebes and set off, followed by fifteen soldiers bawling out bawdy soldiers' songs at the top of their lungs. Amphitryon himself drove the first chariot, with Alcmene standing behind him, smiling to left and right and waving a branch of laurel in greeting; the second chariot was driven by young Licymnius, Alcmene's half-brother (their father --- Electryon, the youngest son of Perseus --- was shared between them, while Licymnius's mother was the Phrygian woman Midea), as wildly black-haired as his sister; and the reins of the third chariot were held by none other than Thelem the Nasal, released from watch duty at Amphitryon's request, and beaming like a freshly polished buckle.

[^13]: Corresponds roughly to the period from July 15th to August 15th.

Amphitryon himself, forced into ceremonial robes embroidered with purple where it was needed and where it wasn't, could not wait for the hour when they would draw far enough from the city that he could finally tear off the hateful rags and change into clothing more fitting for a man on campaign --- even if this campaign was nothing more than a pilgrimage to sacred Delphi. At the same time, he intended to move Alcmene onto Licymnius's chariot. The reason was that Amphitryon was rightly considered the best charioteer in Boeotia --- and perhaps not only in Boeotia --- and, like any master proud of his skill, allowed himself, while driving, a good many liberties not altogether suitable for transporting a pregnant woman.

Especially when that woman is your wife, and you want her to carry safely to term and bear you an heir.

Licymnius, on the other hand, despite his age, was considered an experienced and level-headed driver.

...When the last soldier had passed through the gates, and the crowd of Thebans began to disperse, trading impressions, a faint shadow fell across the people's faces, sliding away toward the still-open leaves of the gate --- though the sun shone in full force and there was not a single cloud in the sky.

"Mama, mama!" a blue-eyed boy of about five tugged at the edge of his mother's peplos. "Look! Someone walked right past the sun! Come on, mama!.."

"What?" the plump mother asked back inattentively, quite absorbed in discussing yesterday's fish and wool prices with a friend. "What do you want? Are you hungry?"

"Someone walked right past the sun!" the little boy repeated patiently. "And he went off way over there!"

He pointed westward, in the direction the procession bound for Delphi had gone.

"Oh?" the mother responded. "Well, good then. We'll be home soon --- and I'll feed you..."

The boy was about to cry, but caught sight of a grass snake poking its head out of a burrow, and changed his mind.
