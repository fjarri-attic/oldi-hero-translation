#### 4

For several days after this lamentable incident, Amphitryon walked about as though struck by thunder: he did not hear what was said to him, kept blundering into tables and klinai, dropped every piece of household ware he touched; he spoke little, and mostly beside the point, ate almost nothing, yet drank a great deal without getting drunk; he would often freeze in place, staring long at the sky (or the ceiling), his lips moving soundlessly...

Poor Alcmene was quite worn out watching what was happening to her husband, unable to help him in any way --- but after some ten days Amphitryon's sound constitution reasserted itself, and he began, little by little, to come back to himself. He remembered the business that had piled up in the meantime, ordered a pair of negligent slaves flogged for a start, which promptly turned them into diligent ones, very diligent indeed --- in short, the grandson of Perseus and son of Alcaeus of Mycenae was quickly becoming his former active, loud-voiced, imperious self once more; and Alcmene breathed a sigh of relief.

Praise heaven, the gods had not taken her husband's wits!

Not that anyone had actually intended to take Amphitryon's wits away --- the gods, it seemed, had enough of their own, and if they hadn't, they would have been even less likely to think of borrowing so precious (or not so precious) an item from the husband of the fair Alcmene.

It was simply that Amphitryon Perseid --- once called an exile, and later a hero --- had, for the first time in his life, seriously wondered what his life in this world was worth, and indeed what any life at all was worth. It was not so much Elphistic's death itself that had shaken Amphitryon --- in his time he had seen enough deaths, some more absurd than this one, some more terrible, and every other kind besides --- as the circumstances of that death. The visit to Alcmene of a god who had, in the end, disgraced himself; his own, Amphitryon's, foolish and careless telling of it --- and the hook in the back of the Troezenian's skull... and the voice, the unpleasant voice, of the ragged man in the filthy hooded chlamys, who had warned of the dire consequences of excessive loquacity.

But all the same, Amphitryon was not one for prolonged brooding, especially when thinking or not thinking made no difference at all. And then, as ill luck would have it, word came from the pastures in the nearby valley of the Cephissus that brigands had turned up there, stealing goats and sheep.

Amphitryon immediately armed part of his household --- choosing men who were tried and tested in battle --- and set out southeast toward the Cephissus to put a stop to the brigandry.

As it turned out, though, almost as soon as he arrived, there was brigandry, but no brigands; or rather, it's not that there were none at all, and goats truly were disappearing --- but only because the shepherds of the flocks, that is, the herdsmen, were exceedingly fond of meat. And fonder of it, more often, than was proper. Especially with young wine, which in those parts almost flowed in streams.

There was meat aplenty. It bleated and baaed and grazed close at hand. The meat belonged to the master, and it was delicious --- and there lay the whole contradiction. So every time, the herdsmen honestly fought the temptation --- and every time, the temptation won...

Having discovered this, Amphitryon, instead of flying into a rage as his companions had expected, laughed for a long time, then ordered the guilty herdsmen whipped (mostly for their incompetence at lying) and the eaten animals deducted from the share of the newborn flock due to them; after which he set off on the way back.

The herdsmen watched him go, scratching their welted backs, marveling at how light the punishment had been.

At the sight of meat, they felt sick to their stomachs...

As for Amphitryon, once back in Thebes, he forgot all about the herdsmen and the losses the moment he laid eyes on his own house.

Its entire eastern wing, where the gynaeceum --- the women's quarters --- was located, bore unmistakable traces of fire. The same story was told by the smoke-blackened doorway, the courtyard coated in a layer of grimy soot, and the cracked, blackened roof tiles, where several slaves were now busy laying a new roof.

Amphitryon's breath caught; he quickened his pace, then broke into a run...

Alcmene, limping slightly, came out to meet him from the door of the megaron --- the central hall for men's feasts and business meetings, ringed by a low stone balustrade; she came down the steps, and Amphitryon, with inexpressible relief, drew his wife to him. He stroked her silky hair, seemed to be saying something, and could not stop himself, feeling her heart beat, feeling her breath lift Alcmene's breast --- he could not bring himself to let her go, especially now, after the shock he had experienced, and only one thought pounded in his head: "Praise Zeus --- alive!.. alive... alive!.."

Why it was Zeus, of all gods, who deserved the praise, and not some other --- of that he did not think.

Later he listened to the story of what had happened, and his face betrayed nothing throughout.

* * *

...Cries of "Fire!" rang out in the middle of the night. Apparently someone had knocked over an oil lamp, the carpet caught --- and from there it went up in flames.

Still half-asleep, Alcmene did not grasp at once what was happening, but the unceasing cries of "We're on fire!" and the smoky haze already seeping through the cracks quickly told her what had happened. Hastily wrapping herself in her peplos[^11], Alcmene rushed for the door, but a fold of the carpet, as though alive, slid beneath her feet, and a sharp pain pierced her ankle... smoke crept all around, her throat was raw, her head was spinning... Alcmene understood with utter clarity that she could not get up --- and would either burn or suffocate in the smoke. She screamed with the last of her strength --- and plunged into a bottomless smoky well, at whose rapidly approaching bottom...

[^11]: A loose-fitting outer garment worn by women.

She was carried outside just in time by two freedmen among the household servants, who had broken down the door to the gynaeceum. Almost at once a downpour struck, beating down and smothering the ravenous tongues of flame bursting from beneath the roof --- and Alcmene came to herself in the rain. The rain trod heavily upon the dying fire, and the smell of freshness quickly drove the choking reek of smoke from her lungs... fortunately, no one had died, and the fire brought no great losses either.

Though who had tipped over the fateful lamp, or whether there had even been a lamp at all, was never established.
