#### 2

"I'm growing old," Amphitryon thought, despondent, clutching his head in his hands, and was startled by this uninvited resignation himself --- it had come upon him like a guest one has no wish to see, yet cannot turn away.

He smoothed his hair, absently, then looked down at his palms --- broad, calloused, their lines of fate cut sharp and deep.

A few hairs lay crosswise over the lifeline.

One dark, and three grey.

Amphitryon blew them away, and the hairs spun in the air, settling to the floor slowly, as though reluctant.

...Midnight found him in the megaron, sitting in complete solitude at his old, favorite little table with legs carved into lion's paws; before a cup of wine from which Amphitryon had not once troubled himself to take a sip.

The house slept the anxious sleep of a man wearied by the day, who knows the coming morrow will bring him no relief. The slaves and servants slept, tired out from gossiping and shuddering; Alcmene, curled up on her kline, had lost herself in sleep at last, though her fixed, unmoving stare still haunted Amphitryon --- he feared in earnest that his wife's mind would not survive the calamity that had fallen upon her; the nurse Eurythea slept the shallow sleep of the old, and no one knew that she was fated never to wake again here, in this house, in this vain and fleeting world --- only the soul of the quietly departed nurse would stir for a moment on its way to Hades, to pass by the White Rock and find eternal oblivion... and in their own chambers slept the twin boys, Alcides and Iphicles, each of them insisting that he, and he alone, was the killer of Linus the kitharode, and that his brother was lying to shield him.

Amphitryon's palms clenched into fists and came down on the table, startling the wine into spilling from the cup and spreading into a blue-black puddle.

"So here it is, the curse of the Perseid line," Amphitryon thought, because not thinking of it was impossible, because thinking of Linus's body, made ready for tomorrow's funeral fire, was a hundred times more painful; thinking of the murdered man, of the murderer, until it all closed into one blazing circle, until it stopped mattering what he thought of --- the way it sometimes stops mattering where you plunge a knife into yourself, into the heart, the throat, the belly... it doesn't matter where, so long as it goes in to the hilt.

Amphitryon remembered his grandfather well --- the great Perseus, a sour, taciturn old man whose hands were almost always clasped behind his back, as though Perseus hated them and tried not to notice those hands, the ones that had hurled the accursed discus that killed Acrisius, Perseus's own grandfather, who had spent his whole life hiding from his grandson. As a small child Amphitryon had been very afraid of the unaffectionate old man, and only on reaching manhood did he understand that Perseus had, in fact, been anything but old at the time --- barely past forty-five, which for a half-god hero, in whose veins ran a rivulet of the divine ichor that serves the Olympians in place of mortal blood, was hardly an advanced age at all.

While staying in Midea, the twenty-three-year-old Amphitryon learned that in Tiryns, at the age of sixty-four, Perseus the Gorgon-Slayer had died; and, arriving for the funeral, Amphitryon simply could not make himself believe that it was the body of Medusa's conqueror now blazing on that funeral pyre.

There burned the corpse of an old man who had once killed another old man, his own grandfather, and had never forgiven himself that involuntary guilt to the end of his days.

Three years later, Amphitryon hurled a club at a fleeing cow, missed, and split open the skull of Electryon, his uncle and Alcmene's father.

And now Amphitryon was forty-five, just as Perseus had once been, when his five-year-old grandson had gazed at him in fright, knowing nothing yet of his own fate --- and in the house slept the boys, one of whom had, that very morning, broken his teacher's neck, whether on purpose or by accident.

Alcides, or Iphicles.

Each of them insists: I did it.

Someone is lying.

Who, and why?

Perhaps it was payment due for the quiet, peaceful years since the disappearance of Myrtilus the archer?

Even Alcides's fits had stopped... or else the boy had learned to sense them coming and hide himself away somewhere, to wait out the seizure alone.

Alone, with Iphicles --- there was no other kind of solitude for the twins.

Why, why must the Perseids forever hide hands stained with the blood of kinsmen or teachers?! Or was Alcides's madness not the scheming of a jealous Hera at all, but the result of Alcmene and Amphitryon's close kinship?

Amphitryon took up the cup, raised it to his lips, and, without drinking after all, set it back down. The faint clink broke the silence, which shrank back startled, waited out the fading of the barely audible echo, and filled the megaron once more with its soft, downy soundlessness.

Four years before that, he had discovered that the children had learned to lie. Watching a discus-throwing contest in the palaestra from a distance, Amphitryon had watched Iphicles's discus fall farther and farther, with a noticeable lead over the other students' throws; Amphitryon grew absorbed and missed the moment when Alcides stepped away from the line and vanished somewhere. Later, toward evening, Castor praised Alcides --- sparingly, as always, but with a hint of suppressed satisfaction --- for winning the contest. Amphitryon was quite astonished, but chose not to intervene in front of the students.

"You praised the wrong one," he told Castor, once they were alone together. "It was Iphicles who was in form today..."

Castor laughed, which did not happen to him often.

"Daddy!" Castor said with relish. "Learn to tell your own children apart! I, of all people, know who I'm praising!"

Amphitryon did not argue --- but he began watching the children closely, and within half a year he could tell himself, with full certainty: "They are lying, and doing it deliberately." There were times when Amphitryon wanted to step in and prove that yesterday it truly had been Alcides who beat Lycomedes, splitting the latter's nose beautifully and entirely within the rules, yet today it was none other than Iphicles who crossed the line first, beating the long-legged Polyctor by eight podes[^34] at the very least, and at best by...

[^34]: A pous (foot), plural podes --- about 30 cm.

He said nothing.

He had firmly learned the lesson of thirteen years past --- don't be quick to speak! Don't be quick, because the result of your loquacity, O Amphitryon, darling of the gods, might well be a hook driven into the back of someone's skull!

He said nothing, and in the palaestra (and, in time, in the minds of the Thebans) Alcides came firmly to reign --- always first, always unattainable, the future hero, son of Zeus, beside whom his brother Iphicles, son of Amphitryon, existed almost unnoticed, himself a future hero of no small standing, which was hardly surprising given such forebears and teachers, but merely the second, or the third; and that, you must agree, stirs the onlookers, the gods, and the girls a good deal less.

It all fell out naturally for Zeus, honorably for Alcides, safely for Iphicles, and just a little hurtfully for him, Amphitryon Perseid --- though what, one might think, did he have to be hurt about, he who knew every last hidden detail of what was happening better than any Moira!

Did he, though?

...With a sharp motion Amphitryon swept the cup off the table, rose, and left the megaron.

Coming down from the terrace into the courtyard, he stood there a moment, head tipped back, gazing into the bewildered, blinking eyes of the starry titan Argus Panoptes; then, glancing furtively about, he took a short, hard run-up and, in two touches, threw his body over the fence into the street, and came to a stop by the gate, still locked --- only now locked from the other side.

"I'm growing old," Amphitryon thought again, catching his breath and rubbing his scraped thigh.

All around him the cicadas chirred, mocking.

Amphitryon breathed deeply of the cool night air, holding it in his chest longer than usual, and set off with a rolling gait toward the palaestra.
