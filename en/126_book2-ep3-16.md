#### 16

Iolaus came back from Thebes gloomy and taciturn. He answered the questions of those close to him in monosyllables, and Laodamia, sensing that the subject was unpleasant to him, tried not to touch on the ill-starred journey at all.

But little by little everything returned to its old course---whether Laodamia had managed to warm her withdrawn husband back to life, or whether the quiet of the backwater and the second youth granted to Iolaus had done their work.

Phylace grew steadily richer; basileus Acastus doted on his new kinsman, making the fullest use of his advice and his wide acquaintance, and the basileus often felt that his niece's husband was much older than his years. And Iolaus began to travel again, each time noisily announcing his departure to everyone around---but coming back after a few months surprisingly quietly, almost on the sly, and on the way home introducing himself to everyone as Protesilaus from Phylace. So that soon many in the neighborhood (and not only in the neighborhood) began to forget who Laodamia's husband really was. Protesilaus[^85], or some such... a very sensible young man... Yes, of course, we've met! What's he famous for? Whose son is he? Iphicles's, I think, one of the locals, but I don't know for sure, never got round to asking... What? Iolaus? Well, who doesn't know Iolaus, son of *that* Iphicles, the driver of Heracles himself?! Only he moved to Sardinia ages ago... or was it Sicily?.. Oh, I don't remember, I'll have to ask Protesilaus---he's sure to know!

[^85]: Protesilaus (Gk. "Iolaus the First" and at the same time "First of the People"), son of Iphicles, basileus of Phylace, would go down in the history of the Trojan War as the warrior who was the first to fall at Troy. Although after his death, unlike other dead men, he appears (accompanied by Hermeias) to his wife Laodamia and talks with her---and Laodamia vanishes from Phylace, having staged her own suicide.

Iolaus had indeed sailed to Sardinia and to Sicily more than once: he traded, helped to set the Achaean colonies in order, introduced the islanders to Mycenaean fashions and the Hellenic way of life---and, of course, to stories about the gods, the half-gods, and the best of mortals, Heracles; how could one do without that, especially when the stories came first-hand?!

On one of his regular visits Iolaus was no little surprised to discover on Sardinia a brand-new and rather charming temple, erected in honor of... Iolaus! All the more so since, by the accounts of priests and worshippers alike, the cult was flourishing.

Iolaus gave an embarrassed grunt, offered a sacrifice to himself---the ram turned out to be uncommonly tasty---and, having finished his business, hurried to sail home earlier than usual.

"To blazes with the lot of them," he reasoned, quite rightly, once aboard, "no sense getting the flock used to regular appearances. Blurt out something wrong, and the seers will wear you out with their interpretations afterwards!"

Soon the same story repeated itself on Sicily.

"Fine," Iolaus decided, "not bad at all, even... Let the Family rack their brains at their leisure now---wherever has our friend Iolaus got to?! Let them search the temples! As for peaceable Protesilaus of Phylace, merchant, bearer of culture and beloved husband of Laodamia---his humble person is hardly likely to interest the Olympians..."

Iolaus rarely visited Heracles. And not only because it was hard for him to see his son drowned in an invisible past, trying for the hundredth time to understand who was before him: Alcides or Iphicles?

That pain had had time to dull.

It was simply that the words of the seer Tiresias, passed on to him, had lodged firmly in the former lawagetas's mind---the words of a man whose sightless eyes were already peering into the gloom of Erebus.

*Heracles will die by the hand of one already dead.*

::: {.small-scene-break}
:::

Amphitryon, returned from Hades, suspected *what* these words might mean for him personally.

By the hand of one already dead.

By *his* hand.

The curse of the Perseid line.

And that was why he rarely visited Heracles, and was always in a hurry to leave again as soon as he could.

The former lawagetas was afraid of himself.

So passed eight unremarkable years.

From time to time Heracles helped his new neighbors in some petty, pointless wars---he himself hardly fought at all, since his name alone was enough for the gates to open and carts laden with ransom to roll out of them.

In the memory of the Achaeans still bled the events of the not-so-distant past, when three noble houses had been cut down root and branch, and the smoke of funeral pyres had for days on end darkened the frightened sun over Eleia, Pylos and Sparta.

No one wanted a repeat.

Faithful Lichas always accompanied his idol and watchfully saw to it that Heracles was allotted a worthy share of the spoils. Heracles himself had not the slightest interest in the matter; his young wife Deianira, on the other hand, who was making the most of the freedom of life with a famous and easygoing husband, found this state of affairs entirely to her liking.

In all that time Heracles did not have a single fit.

Which was why Iolaus was so alarmed by the news that Heracles, out of the blue, had struck dead the boy Eunomus, son of Architeles, a kinsman of Oeneus---Deianira's father...

Before the passing merchant had even finished his muddled story, Iolaus, who had already got lost in the tangled family ties of the victim, swore in exasperation.

Could it be that the Fallen and the Possessed had still not left his son in peace?!

A week later everything cleared itself up.

Little Eunomus had mistakenly given Heracles dirty water to wash his hands, for which he earned a cuff on the back of the head. But either Heracles had misjudged his strength, or the boy happened to be uncommonly frail---in short, poor Eunomus dropped dead and never got up again.

Heracles, roused from his reveries, sincerely repented of what he had done and, although the dead boy's kinsmen at once forgave the hero, made ready to leave Calydon. He had no right, he said, to abuse the hospitality of a city where he had polluted himself with the killing of an innocent youth, and it was time he took his leave. Just as soon as he and his wife decided where to go---they would start packing.

Neither the entreaties of Deianira, who did not want to leave her homeland, nor the pleas of her father, the ruler Oeneus, had any effect.

Heracles was leaving Calydon.

This news fully satisfied Iolaus. The main thing was---there had been no fit. Although... had it turned out to be madness, Iolaus would then have known for certain which of the brothers had died in Pheneus, and which was now living out his allotted span.

But there had been no fit.

As for the rest...

And Iolaus hastened to send a messenger to Trachis, Phylace's neighbor, to its basileus Ceyx, who back in the old days had helped the twins and Iolaus with arms, and with warriors too (duly receiving a share of the spoils for it). There was no need to explain twice to clever Ceyx all the advantages of extending hospitality to Heracles---Iolaus received gifts from the ruler of Trachis, and a herald set off to Heracles with an offer of shelter and purification from any miasma.

[And it's easier for me too,]{.thoughts} Iolaus reasoned. [Trachis is close at hand---I'll keep an eye on him if anything happens; and we won't see each other more than we need to. Oh, Tiresias, Tiresias, if only I knew for sure what you meant...]{.thoughts}

Tiresias had died about half a year earlier, having set out on his last pilgrimage to Delphi---on a litter; he passed away before even reaching Phocis.

Then again, the blind seer had been so decrepit by then that, it seemed, divine providence had had nothing to do with it.

:::

Having received the invitation from hospitable Ceyx, great Heracles did not hesitate for long---and the very next day gave the order to pack up and move out.

There was a great deal of baggage---Deianira was a thrifty housekeeper, and faithful Lichas, too, did what he could to add to his idol's prosperity.

Clothes, household utensils, weapons, jewels, provisions, goats and sheep, bulls and cows, a small herd of horses, wife, children, servants, slaves, members of the household, people seeing them off---there was no listing it all, even lumping everything in one heap.

The whole caravan moved extremely slowly and only toward evening reached the Evenus, a river in the east of Aetolia.

They pitched camp not far from the bank, deciding to cross in the morning.

The slave women and maidservants whispered among themselves, giggling. From these mysterious whisperings it emerged that anyone who wished was ferried across the Evenus by a certain centaur, Nessus. But from the men he took the usual fare, whereas from the women... A very passionate centaur, they say. So modest girls from decent families had better not make use of his services. Otherwise, it happens, you cross just one time---and then you go riding back and forth five or six times in a row!

By the end you're even mixing up the banks---which is which; all you remember is that on that one we did it like so, and on this one---like that...

Some of the slave women, snatching up jugs, were already hurrying down to the river---supposedly for water. They came back after sunset, without the jugs and without water, but with shining, languorously misted eyes.

Seeing the dreamy smiles on their friends' flushed faces, the women who had stayed in camp in their turn hurried to the river---the forgotten jugs couldn't be left to go to waste, could they?!---but just then Deianira emerged from the traveling tent.

"Enough gadding about!" she shouted sternly. "Back to work, you idlers! Oh, you can't trust anyone with anything..."

And, gathering up the hem of her long himation, she proudly processed down to the river.

The maidservants exchanged glances and decided not to go into details.

Only as it began to grow light, and the edge of sleepy Helios was about to appear any moment above the horizon, which lay hushed in expectation, did Lichas, suffering from a heavy hangover, clamber out onto the riverbank.

And no wonder---polishing off two enormous pithoi of barely watered red between himself, two sentries, and one slave woman!

And so the sight that met his eyes on the far bank of the river at first seemed to the suffering Lichas a ghastly hangover delirium.

In the reeds, two people were loving a horse.

[Now I'm seeing things!..]{.thoughts} thought Lichas, splashing water on his face and rubbing his puffy eyes.

The vision, however, did not go anywhere, whereas his washed eyes began to see a great deal better, and the pre-dawn breeze was successfully dispersing the haze of mist hanging over the water---so that Lichas soon realized that he was not imagining anything at all: it was simply that there, in the reeds, some woman was ardently copulating with that very same centaur-ferryman over whom the slave women had been sighing lustfully all night.

And the occupation had so absorbed them both that they did not give a damn about Lichas, who was splashing noisily and goggling at them with his hungover eyes.

Lichas snorted, was about to guffaw, but looked a third time---and finally made out which woman in particular was now moaning beneath the snorting centaur.

No, Lichas was no strict champion of morality, but such a vile encroachment on his master's property he could not allow!

And shortly afterwards he burst into Heracles's tent with a shout:

"Heracles! Get up! Your wife's out there riding that horse!.. or the other way round..."

Heracles, slow on the uptake with sleep (though, to be fair, in recent years he had not been too quick on the uptake when awake either), leapt up and flew stark naked out of the tent, snatching up his famous bow and his arrows steeped in Lernaean bile.

Deianira---who had been in every way encouraging the centaur, thoroughly worn out after the night---proved to be a quick-witted woman, and at the sight of her naked husband with a bow in his hands she shrieked at the top of her lungs, causing the poor centaur to start at the most inopportune moment:

"Help!!!"

Nessus the ferryman was surprised, since until then he had managed in this business without any helpers---and he was never to be surprised again, because the next second Heracles's arrow pierced his amorous heart.

He died instantly.

Euthanasia---a happy death...

Deianira, meanwhile, struggled out from under the carcass that had slumped onto her---and her gaze fell on a small jug lying nearby. At once an old belief came to the woman's mind: that the blood of one who has died during the act of love makes an excellent love potion; and while Heracles, Lichas and the servants were crossing over to the ravished victim, she managed to collect Nessus's dark blood, flowing from the pierced chest, into the jug and stop it up tightly with a tuft of grass.

You never know... it might come in handy.

And Heracles said nothing to his wife; he did not even listen to the torrent of her muddled explanations. He looked at the centaur's corpse, sighed obscurely, and pushed the body into the water; and then for a long time he watched the horse-man float away downstream, having paid with his life for his last crossing.

On the bank where the camp was, the slave women, who had come running, furtively wiped away their tears.

In the evening, as they made camp for another night, Deianira could not hold out and told her trusted maidservant about the little jug of blood. Naturally, ordering her to keep the little jug's existence in the strictest secrecy.

"I swear by little Eros!" the maidservant whispered fervently. "How clever you are, mistress! My great-grandmother Galinthias, daughter of Proetus, served Hecate the Three-Formed, and more than once she told my grandmother, and my grandmother told my mother, and my mother told me, that you have to smear such blood on a garment and give it to the man you want to bewitch to put on! He'll be yours till death!"

And once more she swore by Eros, by Hecate, and by the shade of her late great-grandmother Galinthias, may she rest in peace, that she would keep the secret.

The rest of the way to Trachis passed without incident.

Basileus Ceyx met Heracles with all possible cordiality and in no time purified him of the miasma of the accidental killing (innocently inquiring: why hadn't this been done back in Calydon?).

Heracles's household set about settling into their new home, the great hero himself sank once again into his memories, and Deianira breathed a sigh of relief: a quiet husband who did not meddle in her affairs suited Deianira far better than that never-missing archer she had seen on the bank of the Evenus.

Even if he did call his own wife now Megara, now something else...

Iolaus came to visit, played with his young cousins, made sure that everything was more or less in order---and hastened to vanish again.

Days added up to weeks and months, nothing of consequence happened, and so, some two years later, Iolaus, returning once again from Sicily, was thunderstruck by the news:

Heracles had marched out on campaign against Oechalia!
