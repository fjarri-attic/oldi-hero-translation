#### 12

War and death, Ares and Thanatos, set out on their inexorable march across the Peloponnese. Only this time the part of Ares was taken on by Iolaus, and that of iron-hearted Thanatos by Heracles.

Iolaus knew better than anyone that heroes are mortal---and of late he had been seeing proof of it almost daily. But heroes who have come to the end of their earthly path go on living in the memory of their descendants, in temples and rites, in the songs of rhapsodes, in the recollections of old men and in the tales mothers tell their restless children. Even the mighty Olympians have to reckon with this, since myths of the deeds of Perseus, or, for that matter, the Dioscuri, often stir far greater delight in their listeners than songs of the deeds of the Thunderer himself.

Heroes have to be reckoned with.

Even after their death.

Which means Heracles must be reckoned with twice over!

And when the god-equal hero does at last ascend to the Olympus promised him (which Iolaus very much doubted), the myth of his life must stand as unshakable as the omphalos, the navel of the earth! And first and foremost, people must remember once and for all:

No one ever defeated Heracles!

And also:

Heracles never forgave anyone anything!

And when the heavens did not open over the blasphemer in the lionskin who had killed the Molionid theoroi hurrying to the Isthmian Games; when the gods kept silent and men only shrugged; when the Olympians never even thought of taking up the challenge thrown down to them, and Heracles, pointedly leaving himself unpurified of the miasma of his deed, began preparing a new campaign against Eleia, quickly (but without fuss)---then even the most benighted Achaeans understood that nothing and no one would stop a Heracles like this.

And if for one reason or another you had no intention of joining the army of the great son of Zeus, then at the very least you had better see to it in good time that you got out of his way.

All the more so since---but only between us, in a whisper!---the gods, it seems, had understood this considerably sooner than mortals.

...Eleia was taken, as they say, in stride. Something inside Heracles really had died, burned out---and when he plunged his sword into the fat belly of basileus Augeas, who was squealing hysterically, he felt nothing: neither rage, nor the satisfaction of vengeance fulfilled, nor hatred, nor even simple excitement. A faint regret, perhaps---there, having to kill again... But this was his work, the very beginning of the work; and he was used to always seeing a thing through to the end.

In part, he was no longer here.

Heracles had died.

Now he was finishing what he had not managed to do in his lifetime.

People who had used their children as breeding stock must not walk the earth.

All of Augeas's sons except young Phyleus were killed, and Phyleus, who had been to Phlegrae, silently ascended the Eleian throne and began by laying the foundation of a temple to Heracles.

Ten days later Heracles's army stood beneath the walls of Pylos.

Neleus and his sons, knowing they had nothing to lose, fought to the death---and fell, every last one of them. Once again the only one left alive was the youngest, Nestor, who had not been in Pylos when the city was taken. Though had Nestor been in the city, he would most likely have been left untouched.

He, too, had once been to Phlegrae.

The city was plundered, but its inhabitants, who hastened to prove their loyalty to the victors, were merely laid under a moderately burdensome tribute. Iolaus personally cut down two Myrmidons who had gone too far, and when an enraged Telamon came to have it out with him, answered dryly:

"Heracles is not a murderer but a hero. And we are his soldiers, not highway robbers. Cruelty must be reasonable. Or do your marauders want all of Hellas to hate us?"

And Telamon found nothing to say in reply.

The hardest was the battle at Sparta. Hippocoon's men were rightly considered seasoned fighters, and despite their small numbers they managed to lay low a good many before they themselves lay down in their native Laconian earth.

Hippocoon was one of the last to fall, struck down by Iolaus's spear. The Spartan basileus met his death with dignity and, so it seemed to Iolaus, almost with relief---mired and entangled in the intrigues of the Brotherhood of Salmoneus, the hereditary warrior had judged death in battle the best way out for himself; though, then again, no one had offered him any other.

And once again it all repeated itself. With the one difference that the vacated throne was taken not by a son but by the dead man's brother, Tyndareus by name. The new basileus harbored no particularly tender feelings for Heracles, but nonetheless did not refuse the power offered him, and took up the reins of government in a city only just beginning to recover from the shock.

The sword of war at last slid back into its sheath.

No one escaped the reckoning.

No one among mortals.

Eurystheus did not count---for he was not one of the Possessed; besides, the Mycenaean wanax had so far never raised his hand directly against Heracles or his kin.

Though Iolaus had long felt a growing premonition that sooner or later the turn of Eurystheus the Mycenaean would come as well.

Sooner or later---but not now.

For today, the work was done.

...The parting turned out sullen and somehow forced. Two chariots stood at a crossing of well-worn trade roads. The wider road led northeast, to Thessaly; Iolaus was hurrying to Phylace, where he had left Laodamia.

The other road, narrower, turned west, toward Calydon, where foolish internecine strife had just ended, one that had cost the life of Meleager the Invulnerable, and not his alone. Heracles, for some reason, had taken it into his head that he was obliged to look after the late Meleager's sister---the looking-after, evidently, included marrying this very sister, Deianira by name---and now intended to visit Calydon.

In fact, in recent days Iolaus had begun to notice, with alarm, certain strange changes in Heracles's behavior.

It had begun right after the taking of Sparta.

Now Heracles drank a great deal, hardly getting drunk at all; though Iolaus, of all peopple, knew perfectly well that his son had never before been much of a lover of Dionysus's heady gifts, and had never really known how to hold his drink.

Besides that, Heracles had begun to ramble. He dreamed while awake, sometimes froze for a long time in one pose, gazing with utterly empty eyes into distances known only to him; more than once he called Iolaus father in front of others---and people nodded sympathetically, trying to keep well away from the great hero, since everyone had heard plenty about his terrible fits of madness.

But these were not fits.

Heracles's madness had become quiet; he talked aloud with his dead brother, with his father Amphitryon (addressing Iolaus, who stood beside him, but not seeing him, or seeing him somehow wrongly), with the children he had killed back in his youth...

At last Iolaus understood.

Heracles was retreating into the past, sinking into it deeper and deeper---and no power could bring him back to the present day anymore. His soul was going away to where he was not yet called Heracles, where his father, his brother, his children were still alive...

And was it worth trying to lure him back into the victorious, famous, hopeless "now"?!

Iolaus resigned himself.

He was saying goodbye to his son, who was leaving for Calydon, yet it felt as though he were talking to an empty wineskin, to a jug drained to the bottom, in whose depths lay hidden the unreachable drops of the real Heracles---but "not here" and "not now."

For the umpteenth time Iolaus tried, if only by the scars left on the shell of this man lost within himself, to determine who stood before him: Alcides or Iphicles?

He tried---and could not.

He could only guess who had died then, on that dusty, stifling evening in ill-fated Pheneus, where the local basileus was now raising a temple and a stadium dedicated to Iphicles Amphitryad, brother of god-equal Heracles.

None of these thoughts did anything to improve Iolaus's mood.

Nor did the news received the day before from Messenia: the proud greybeards, the brothers Dioscuri, Castor and Polydeuces, had quarreled over stolen herds with the brothers Apharetidae, Idas and Lynceus; in the bloody clash that followed, of all four only Polydeuces was left alive, and him (so rumor had it) his heavenly father, Dias the Thunderer, had taken up to Olympus.

[No body found, I'll wager,]{.thoughts} Iolaus thought, grinding his teeth, when he heard the news.

And the dead finger in the sky twitched like a half-crushed worm as a few more benches emptied on the phantom *Argo*.

Heroes went on killing each other.

And those who didn't want to...

Near Delphi, only a few days ago, the brilliant physician Asclepius had perished. It was whispered that Zeus had struck the healer down with his thunders for trying to raise a dead hero.

Once again, no body was found.

And Iolaus realized that the accursed finger had been tormenting more than him alone---otherwise wise Asclepius would never have risked raising the dead.

Nothing could be changed. And if Iolaus rushed about Hellas too much, trying to be everywhere in time and using the Dromoi left and right, then most likely some annoying yet deadly mishap would befall him too: a falling tree, a robber's knife in the back, a collapsing cliff or roof, a fire...

For some reason he recalled the bronze hook in the back of Elpisticos the Troezenian's head---a misfortune that had happened more than forty years ago.

No, the former lawagetas did not want such a fate for himself.
