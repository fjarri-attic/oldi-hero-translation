#### 12

War and death, Ares and Thanatos, set out on their inexorable march across the Peloponnese. Only this time the part of Ares was taken on by Iolaos, and that of iron-hearted Thanatos by Herakles.

Iolaos knew better than anyone that heroes are mortal---and of late he had been seeing proof of it almost daily. But heroes who have come to the end of their earthly path go on living in the memory of their descendants, in temples and rites, in the songs of rhapsodes, in the recollections of old men and in the tales mothers tell their restless children. Even the mighty Olympians have to reckon with this, since myths of the deeds of Perseus, or, for that matter, the Dioskouroi, often stir far greater delight in their listeners than songs of the deeds of the Thunderer himself.

Heroes have to be reckoned with.

Even after their death.

Which means Herakles must be reckoned with twice over!

And when the god-equal hero does at last ascend to the Olympos promised him (which Iolaos very much doubted), the myth of his life must stand as unshakable as the omphalos, the navel of the earth! And first and foremost, people must remember once and for all:

*No one ever defeated Herakles!*

And also:

*Herakles never forgave anyone anything!*

And when the heavens did not open over the blasphemer in the lionskin who had killed the Molionid theoroi hurrying to the Isthmian Games; when the gods kept silent and men only shrugged; when the Olympians never even thought of taking up the challenge thrown down to them, and Herakles, pointedly leaving himself unpurified of the miasma of his deed, began preparing a new campaign against Elis, quickly (but without fuss)---then even the most benighted Achaians understood that nothing and no one would stop a Herakles like this.

And if for one reason or another you had no intention of joining the army of the great son of Zeus, then at the very least you had better see to it in good time that you got out of his way.

All the more so since---but only between us, in a whisper!---the gods, it seems, had understood this considerably sooner than mortals.

...Elis was taken, as they say, in stride. Something inside Herakles really had died, burned out---and when he plunged his sword into the fat belly of basileus Augeias, who was squealing hysterically, he felt nothing: neither rage, nor the satisfaction of vengeance fulfilled, nor hatred, nor even simple excitement. A faint regret, perhaps---there, having to kill again... But this was his work, the very beginning of the work; and he was used to always seeing a thing through to the end.

In part, he was no longer here.

Herakles had died.

Now he was finishing what he had not managed to do in his lifetime.

People who had used their children as breeding stock must not walk the earth.

All of Augeias's sons except young Phyleus were killed, and Phyleus, who had been to Phlegra, silently ascended the Eleian throne and began by laying the foundation of a temple to Herakles.

Ten days later Herakles's army stood beneath the walls of Pylos.

Neleus and his sons, knowing they had nothing to lose, fought to the death---and fell, every last one of them. Once again the only one left alive was the youngest, Nestor, who had not been in Pylos when the city was taken. Though had Nestor been in the city, he would most likely have been left untouched.

He, too, had once been to Phlegra.

The city was plundered, but its inhabitants, who hastened to prove their loyalty to the victors, were merely laid under a moderately burdensome tribute. Iolaos personally cut down two Myrmidons who had gone too far, and when an enraged Telamon came to have it out with him, answered dryly:

"Herakles is not a murderer but a hero. And we are his soldiers, not highway robbers. Cruelty must be reasonable. Or do your marauders want all of Hellas to hate us?"

And Telamon found nothing to say in reply.

The hardest was the battle at Sparta. Hippokoon's men were rightly considered seasoned fighters, and despite their small numbers they managed to lay low a good many before they themselves lay down in their native Lakonian earth.

Hippokoon was one of the last to fall, struck down by Iolaos's spear. The Spartan basileus met his death with dignity and, so it seemed to Iolaos, almost with relief---mired and entangled in the intrigues of the Brotherhood of Salmoneus, the hereditary warrior had judged death in battle the best way out for himself; though, then again, no one had offered him any other.

And once again it all repeated itself. With the one difference that the vacated throne was taken not by a son but by the dead man's brother, Tyndareos by name. The new basileus harbored no particularly tender feelings for Herakles, but nonetheless did not refuse the power offered him, and took up the reins of government in a city only just beginning to recover from the shock.

The sword of war at last slid back into its sheath.

No one escaped the reckoning.

No one among mortals.

Eurystheus did not count---for he was not one of the Possessed; besides, the Mycenaean wanax had so far never raised his hand directly against Herakles or his kin.

Though Iolaos had long felt a growing premonition that sooner or later the turn of Eurystheus the Mycenaean would come as well.

Sooner or later---but not now.

For today, the work was done.

* * *

...The parting turned out sullen and somehow forced. Two chariots stood at a crossing of well-worn trade roads. The wider road led northeast, to Thessaly; Iolaos was hurrying to Phylake, where he had left Laodameia.

The other road, narrower, turned west, toward Kalydon, where foolish internecine strife had just ended, one that had cost the life of Meleagros the Invulnerable, and not his alone. Herakles, for some reason, had taken it into his head that he was obliged to look after the late Meleagros's sister---the looking-after, evidently, included marrying this very sister, Deianeira by name---and now intended to visit Kalydon.

In fact, in recent days Iolaos had begun to notice, with alarm, certain strange changes in Herakles's behavior.

It had begun right after the taking of Sparta.

Now Herakles drank a great deal, hardly getting drunk at all; though Iolaos, of all peopple, knew perfectly well that his son had never before been much of a lover of Dionysos's heady gifts, and had never really known how to hold his drink.

Besides that, Herakles had begun to ramble. He dreamed while awake, sometimes froze for a long time in one pose, gazing with utterly empty eyes into distances known only to him; more than once he called Iolaos father in front of others---and people nodded sympathetically, trying to keep well away from the great hero, since everyone had heard plenty about his terrible fits of madness.

But these were not fits.

Herakles's madness had become quiet; he talked aloud with his dead brother, with his father Amphitryon (addressing Iolaos, who stood beside him, but not seeing him, or seeing him somehow wrongly), with the children he had killed back in his youth...

At last Iolaos understood.

Herakles was retreating into the past, sinking into it deeper and deeper---and no power could bring him back to the present day anymore. His soul was going away to where he was not yet called Herakles, where his father, his brother, his children were still alive...

And was it worth trying to lure him back into the victorious, famous, hopeless "now"?!

Iolaos resigned himself.

He was saying goodbye to his son, who was leaving for Kalydon, yet it felt as though he were talking to an empty wineskin, to a jug drained to the bottom, in whose depths lay hidden the unreachable drops of the real Herakles---but "not here" and "not now."

For the umpteenth time Iolaos tried, if only by the scars left on the shell of this man lost within himself, to determine who stood before him: Alkeides or Iphikles?

He tried---and could not.

He could only guess who had died then, on that dusty, stifling evening in ill-fated Pheneos, where the local basileus was now raising a temple and a stadium dedicated to Iphikles Amphitryad, brother of god-equal Herakles.

None of these thoughts did anything to improve Iolaos's mood.

Nor did the news received the day before from Messenia: the proud greybeards, the brothers Dioskouroi, Kastor and Polydeukes, had quarreled over stolen herds with the brothers Apharetids, Idas and Lynkeus; in the bloody clash that followed, of all four only Polydeukes was left alive, and him (so rumor had it) his heavenly father, Dias the Thunderer, had taken up to Olympos.

[No body found, I'll wager,]{.thoughts} Iolaos thought, grinding his teeth, when he heard the news.

And the dead finger in the sky twitched like a half-crushed worm as a few more benches emptied on the phantom *Argo*.

Heroes went on killing each other.

And those who didn't want to...

Near Delphi, only a few days ago, the brilliant physician Asklepios had perished. It was whispered that Zeus had struck the healer down with his thunders for trying to raise a dead hero.

Once again, no body was found.

And Iolaos realized that the accursed finger had been tormenting more than him alone---otherwise wise Asklepios would never have risked raising the dead.

Nothing could be changed. And if Iolaos rushed about Hellas too much, trying to be everywhere in time and using the Dromoi left and right, then most likely some annoying yet deadly mishap would befall him too: a falling tree, a robber's knife in the back, a collapsing cliff or roof, a fire...

For some reason he recalled the bronze hook in the back of Elpistikos the Troizenian's head---a misfortune that had happened more than forty years ago.

No, the former lawagetas did not want such a fate for himself.
