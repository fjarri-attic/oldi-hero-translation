#### 5

Leaving Amphitryon's house, Tiresias set off unhurried down the street, his right palm resting on his guide's muscular shoulder, the tip of his staff, gripped in his left, tapping lightly against the road.

He had long since grown used to his blindness, made his peace with it, even come to love, in some measure, this darkness that let him reason and draw conclusions in calm; at times he felt himself a pure spirit, confined by chance in a mountain of fat flesh --- and so he was, more often than not, unkempt and absent-minded.

The one thing Tiresias could never grow used to was the gift of foresight.

Foretelling people's futures on the strength of ordinary knowledge --- of human hopes and yearnings, of the skill of piecing crumbs of the everyday into a solid block of understanding --- oh, that was no great labor for Tiresias! He listened, remembered, compared --- and foretold, and did so not vaguely and ambiguously, as the Delphic oracle did, but plainly and unequivocally, for which rulers loved Tiresias... and, likely enough, the gods loved him too.

For that --- they loved him.

But when the dark and hateful wave of foresight closed over his head, when he drowned in the future, choking on its bitter pulp, and afterward was sick with the remnants of fate --- then Tiresias himself often failed to grasp the meaning of his own answers, or grasped it too late, which was its own torment.

Yet in those minutes he could not stay silent.

...Today, though, he had not managed to say much of anything. Because already at the threshold, on the very point of leaving, while some old woman's little voice muttered something at his back, Tiresias was deafened by the rumble of that underworld which Tiresias called Tartarus, and that rumble wove, in some strange way, together with the ringing hum of those heights which Tiresias called Olympus --- the two voices mingling, twisting into a spiral, turning into a purple-and-gold cocoon (Tiresias had not been blind from birth, and his memory still knew how to see), and there, in the two-colored depths, a two-bodied being with a single child's face stirred, pouring out a stream of power without end or limit, a wild, primal might beyond good and evil, beyond reason and madness, beyond...

Tiresias stopped, gripping his slave guide's shoulder hard, his sightless eyes fixed straight ahead.

At the far end of the street, where it ran into the market, a gaunt, filthy ragamuffin in a tattered, hooded chlamys was capering about. The beggar held two dead snakes in his hands, frightening passersby with them, who gave the ragged man a wide berth and cursed under their breath. At last the beggar managed to slip one snake into some woman's basket --- and did it so deftly that the woman herself noticed nothing --- whereupon he settled down and came up to Tiresias.

"Coochie-coo!" The beggar pursed his lips into a tube and thrust the head of the remaining snake into the blind man's face, deftly dodging the guide-slave's fist as he did. "Have a treat, old fellow!"

"Whom do you mean to deceive, Hermeias?" Tiresias asked quietly, waving his guide off. "Me? The son of the nymph Chariclo? Go deceive the sighted, Trickster, lie to those hardened in their seeing blindness!"

"Why would I deceive you, old fellow?" the beggar laughed, pulling faces. "You'll deceive yourself well enough, without my help!"

"Here is why," Tiresias reached out and touched the dead snake's head. "O-oh," the blind man added almost at once. "You have a fine toy there, Hermeias Cyllenios[^19]! These snakes let a man draw one breath in --- and he has no time left to breathe it out again... I shouldn't wonder if it turns out these fine snakes became your toy somewhere along their way to Amphitryon's house! And that others crawled on from there, quite different ones..."

[^19]: Cyllenios (the Cyllenian) --- an epithet of Hermeias (Hermes), born on Mount Cyllene in Arcadia.

"If it turns out?" The beggar feigned astonishment. "Do you mean to say you don't know everything under the sun, wise Tiresias?"

"No," the blind man answered calmly. "I do not know everything under the sun. But neither are you all-knowing, Trickster --- and in that we are equals. When I get home --- I will make you an offering. Farewell."

And he moved on down the street, feeling out the road with the tip of his staff. Soon the slave, who had waited off to the side, caught up with him and, as ever, set his shoulder beneath Tiresias's hand.

The beggar watched them go for a long while.

"Your soul I shall lead into Hades with especial honor," he muttered, flinging the dead snake at the back of a craftsman passing by. "Though I don't suppose that will happen anytime soon..."

And he ran off, escaping the wrath of the man he'd hit.
