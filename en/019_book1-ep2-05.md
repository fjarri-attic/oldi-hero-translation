#### 5

Leaving Amphitryon's house, Teiresias made his unhurried way down the street, his right palm gripping his guide's muscular shoulder, the tip of the staff in his left hand tapping lightly against the road.

He had long since grown used to his blindness, made his peace with it, even come to love, in some measure, this darkness that let him reason and draw conclusions undisturbed; at times he felt himself a pure spirit, confined by chance in a mountain of fat flesh---which was why he was, more often than not, unkempt and absent-minded.

The one thing Teiresias could never grow used to was the gift of foresight.

Foretelling people's futures from an ordinary knowledge of human hopes and yearnings, from a knack for piecing crumbs of the everyday into a monolith of understanding---oh, that was no great labor for Teiresias! He listened, remembered, compared---and foretold, and did so not vaguely and ambiguously, as the Delphic oracle did, but plainly and unequivocally, for which rulers loved Teiresias... and, likely enough, the gods loved him too.

For that---they loved him.

But when the dark and hateful wave of foresight closed over his head, when he drowned in the future, choking on its bitter pulp, and afterward vomited up the remnants of fate---then Teiresias himself often failed to grasp the meaning of his own answers, or grasped it too late, which was its own torment.

Yet at such moments he could not stay silent.

...Today, though, he had not managed to say much of anything. Because right there on the threshold, just as he was leaving, while an old woman's reedy voice muttered something at his back, Teiresias was deafened by the rumble of that underworld which Teiresias called Tartaros, and that rumble was strangely interwoven with the ringing hum of those heights which Teiresias called Olympos---the two voices mingling, twisting into a spiral, turning into a purple-and-gold cocoon (Teiresias had not been blind from birth, and his memory still knew how to see), and there, in the two-colored depths, a two-bodied being with a single child's face stirred, pouring out a stream of power without end or limit, a wild, primal might beyond good and evil, beyond reason and madness, beyond...

Teiresias stopped, gripping his slave-guide's shoulder hard, his sightless eyes fixed straight ahead.

At the far end of the street, where it ran into the market, a gaunt, filthy ragamuffin in a tattered, hooded chlamys was capering about. The beggar held two dead snakes in his hands, using them to frighten passersby, who gave the ragged man a wide berth and cursed under their breath. At last the beggar managed to slip one snake into some woman's basket---and did it so skillfully that the woman herself noticed nothing---whereupon he stopped his antics and came up to Teiresias.

"Coochie-coo!" The beggar puckered his lips and thrust the head of the remaining snake into the blind man's face, deftly dodging the slave-guide's fist as he did. "Have a treat, gramps!"

"Whom do you mean to deceive, Hermeias?" Teiresias asked quietly, waving his guide away. "Me? The son of the nymph Chariklo? Deceive the sighted, Trickster, lie to those hardened in their sighted blindness!"

"Why would I deceive you, gramps?" the beggar laughed, pulling faces. "You'll deceive yourself well enough, without my help!"

"Here is why." Teiresias reached out and touched the dead snake's head. "O-oh," the blind man added almost at once. "You have a fine toy there, Hermeias Kyllenios[\*](#fn-19){.noteref}! These snakes let a man draw one breath in---and he has no time left to breathe it out again... I would not be surprised to learn that these fine snakes became your toy halfway along their journey to Amphitryon's house! And that from there on, quite different ones crawled the rest of the way..."

"To learn?" the beggar marveled. "Don't you know everything under the sun, wise Teiresias?"

"No," the blind man answered calmly. "I do not know everything under the sun. But neither are you all-knowing, Trickster---and in that we are equals. When I get home---I will make you an offering. Farewell."

And he moved on down the street, feeling his way with the tip of his staff. Soon the slave, who had been waiting off to the side, caught up with him and, as ever, set his shoulder beneath Teiresias's hand.

The beggar watched them go for a long while.

"Your soul I shall lead to Hades with special honor," he muttered, flinging the dead snake at the back of a craftsman passing by. "Though I don't suppose that will happen anytime soon..."

And he ran off, fleeing the enraged passerby.
