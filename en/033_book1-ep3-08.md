#### 8

Hermeias spent that night, the night after the day of the contests, in thoughts --- thoughts that were far from always being pleasant (or rather, had been almost always unpleasant); and the morning began accordingly.

For some reason his thoughts kept returning, again and again, to his first meeting with the twins, which had taken place a little over two years before.

It had been a trifling matter for Hermeias to put himself in the boys' path as they ran to the market, terribly proud of their mother's errand to buy all manner of greens and entirely oblivious to the sturdy Thessalian slave trailing them at a cautious distance. Hermeias, on the other hand, noticed everyone at once: the boys, an old man passing by, and the escorting slave --- and the last of these suited him not at all, with the result that the slave was suddenly seized by a griping stomach and, cursing yesterday's fish that had seemed "a little off," slipped away between the houses and was gone.

As for Alcides and Iphicles, they first slowed their steps, then stopped altogether, staring in fascination at a young vagabond in whose hands three --- no, four --- no, even five! --- glossy pomegranates fluttered like tiny setting suns.

"Ooh!" --- two identically shaped mouths rounded in wonder.

At last the vagabond stopped juggling, winked slyly at the brothers, and tossed each of them a pomegranate. Having caught them, the twins simply froze with their mouths open, as though about to stuff the gifts in whole, peel and seeds together --- while the vagabond's hands turned out empty, and the remaining fruit (one? two? three?) had vanished no one knew where.

What followed was simple enough --- curiosity, interest, fondness, friendship, links in that deceitful chain by which one living creature draws another to itself, whether you be mortal, god, titan, or monster. Whenever the children lost themselves entirely in some new game proposed by the tireless Dummy, Hermeias would study their faces surreptitiously (in essence, one and the same face) and search hungrily for any resemblance to himself, to Apollo, to Dionysus, to Zeus Kronid's other sons; he would peer into that shared face, capable of laughing and crying at once, changing with every day lived --- unlike the faces of the Family, unchanging and familiar... he would search, find something, and at once understand that he was mistaken.

And when these children --- who resembled Amphitryon, who resembled Perseus, and only after that resembled an Olympian --- asked for the first time what the difference was between a god and a mortal, it cost Hermeias great effort not to let the twins see how much the question had shaken him.

If the Family were ever to learn that he, Hermeias the Trickster, had tried to explain to mortals the difference between "I" and "mine," the difference between the eternal and the finite... Prometheus's fate would seem enviable to Hermeias then! The Family needs Half-Man Cleaners, capable of killing for good --- but the moment a Cleaner begins to wonder at the likeness between Medusa and Athena, between the Chimera and Pan, between monster and god; the moment he touches the line between mortal and immortal... oh, such thoughts, such comparisons, the Family does not forgive!

And so Bellerophon, slayer of the Chimera, loses his mind, wandering the earth a blind madman; Perseus the Gorgon-Slayer, the best of the best, kills his own grandfather with a bronze discus --- and spends the rest of his days sitting idle in Tiryns, atoning for an unwitting guilt; Cadmus the Thebes-Builder, who slew a dragon and became one himself, turns into a monstrous serpent, having tasted for himself the fate first of hero, then of monster; the Aloadae brothers pile Ossa on Pelion, mountain on mountain, and keep Ares imprisoned for more than thirty months, but Artemis's doe darts between them, and the mighty-giant Aloadae brothers kill one another...

Fierce Idas of Messenia is still alive, who a year ago dared raise his hand against Apollo over his bride, the river-nymph Marpessa; alive too, though exiled, is another of Apollo's rivals, young Coroebus of Argos; terrible punishment has not yet overtaken Ixion, the rebellious Lapith who dared to love Hera, Zeus's wife, as an equal loves an equal; many are alive, many who dared to step forward, dared to love, to rebel, simply to snap back rather than only kill on command.

Alive for now --- but Atropos the Moira has already taken up her bronze shears.

Mortals must not stop to think why the gods do not kill monsters themselves.

Mortals must not wonder: why, in order to make the immortal mortal, and then dead, is a Cleaner needed, a mortal hero acquainted with death firsthand?

Heroes must not stop to think. Dangerous.

Dangerous for them, and not for them alone.

...Hermeias sighed, scratched an ankle bitten by some disrespectful insect, and waved a hand --- ah, there was no thinking through all his thoughts anyway! All the more so since in the Family this sort of thing simply wasn't done. Otherwise dad would long since have plucked Alcides out of Thebes, tucked him away somewhere under his own wing, and raised his Lone Cleaner without interference! Strange --- Alcides was his son, after all, and a future hero, and the hope of Olympus, and yet somehow it is as if he was not his own! Didn't Zeus love him, or what? Even he, Hermeias, had grown attached to the boys...

And it was here that it lashed Hermeias like a whip. Attached? Him? To mortals?! He hadn't learned anything worthwhile yet, hadn't once seen one of Alcides's fits --- though that was exactly what he'd come to Thebes for --- he just hung about here like a fly on a bald spot, and had gone and taught the boys to use the Dromos[^27] --- Perseus had needed a full year to learn it, and even then had to be escorted afterward so as not to get lost, while these twins, after only their third try, were tearing back and forth like madmen with no escort at all! A Lone Cleaner indeed! Interesting --- two lone ones, and twins at that --- how was one even supposed to count that?!

[^27]: From Greek for "corridor".

Maybe he should go ask the Pythia?

Then Hermeias recalled the centaur Chiron's murky hints about doubts over the Thunderer's paternity --- and grew altogether gloomy. Just his luck, to be born into the Family with both wit and talent! Jokes aside, few in the Family burdened themselves with excessive philosophizing. Mostly they reached for a weapon --- one for the thunderbolt, another for the trident, a third for the bow, or something else entirely...

If they reached for their own heads first, there'd be about half as much trouble, one would think.

Only no one in the Family could be given such advice --- Dummy, they'd say, have you gone completely...

"...Dummy! Dummy, come on! You're sitting here, and you don't know a single thing! And in the city --- there are such goings-on! Such goings-on! And they've canceled lessons at the palaestra! And..."

This time, the one they called Dummy, who himself so loved appearing out of nowhere and vanishing without a trace, was forced to admit that he had honestly missed the moment of the noisy twins' arrival. The boys sprang up before him as if out of the ground, having dashed along the Dromos entirely unnoticed, and at once began babbling over each other about the very same thing --- each determined to be the first to deliver the stunning news --- while Dummy, smiling absently, listened to both of them with half an ear, not paying much attention to the meaning of the words pouring out like peas.

"So it wasn't for nothing that I've been teaching them," Hermeias thought, looking at the tousled, scratched-up boys with their sunburned, peeling noses. "They've mastered the Dromos --- they could walk it blindfolded now! --- and they crept up so well that even I didn't notice..."

The youth shook his head, as if freeing himself for the moment from the thoughts that had possessed him, and tried to listen to the twins' racket.

"Basileus Eurytus left! At night! Without warning anyone!" --- Alcides shouted, hopping with impatience and bursting with news (or was it Iphicles? No, it looked like Alcides after all, although...).

"And his son stayed behind!" --- the other boy echoed, waving his arms desperately and, for some reason, squatting down every other moment (most likely Iphicles, nevertheless...).

"And Teacher Myrtilus has vanished somewhere too! And nobody knows where! Even Tiresias --- even he's silent!"

"And everyone's looking for him --- dad, and Castor with Autolycus, and everyone, everyone..."

"That's why they canceled lessons at the palaestra!"

"Isn't that something!"

"And we ran straight to you..."

"To you!"

"Only it would be better if they actually found Myrtilus," one of the boys suddenly said, two tones lower, sniffling. "Let there be lessons, so long as they find him..."

And Dummy knew at once that this was Iphicles.

"Of course, let them find him," Alcides at once lowered his voice too. "Listen, Dummy, you know everything around here, you go everywhere --- find Myrtilus, will you? Just think --- everyone's looking for him, run off their feet, crying, bringing offerings to Zeus or Hermes or whoever, and then you and I bring him back, and everyone praises us, gives us presents, and Autolycus swears he'll never beat us with his staff again..."

And both boys stared hopefully at their friend.

"I'll try," Hermeias nodded slowly, sensing that none of this was mere chance: his morning brooding over things forgotten or forbidden, the sudden departure of the basileus of Oechalia, more like a flight than a departure, Iphitus left behind in Thebes, the search for the vanished Myrtilus, yesterday's contests... Hermeias didn't like this, didn't like it one bit --- something lay hidden behind these outwardly harmless events, and the not-knowing irritated the Trickster all the more.

"I'll try," the youth repeated, going slack in some elusive instant --- all at once, like a wild animal --- and closing his eyes.

Dummy's face settled into an expression of placid absence.

"So where do we go to look for him?" Alcides asked, understanding nothing.

"And when? Right now?" Iphicles added hastily, having understood no more than his brother.

"We're not going anywhere," Hermeias said, barely audible and flat, and the brothers exchanged glances: it seemed to them that Dummy's lips hadn't moved at all.

"Sit down beside me and wait. Quietly."

"Dummy, are you a... rascal?" Alcides stared at him, astonished, then instantly corrected himself. "I meant to say --- an oracle! Like Tiresias, right?"

Iphicles jabbed his brother painfully in the ribs with a sharp elbow and, when Alcides turned, pressed a finger to his lips: they'd been asked to keep quiet, hadn't they!

Alcides, catching himself, nodded, and the two of them hurried to sit down on the grass and stared at Dummy, exchanging meaningful glances now and then.
