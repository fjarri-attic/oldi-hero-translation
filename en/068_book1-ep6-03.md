#### 3

...Alkmene knew that in this life she had nothing left but her grandsons.

She had known it for almost five years now---since the very day Thebes threw open its gates to the victors come home. Many, no doubt, condemned her then, the wife of the fallen lawagetas, who had not once wept over her husband's body. Everyone grieved for Amphitryon: the living, and the kin of the living---giving thanks for that mercy; those whose houses had been emptied---seeing in the lawagetas their own kin, left behind in the accursed valley; basileus Kreon shed a sparing tear befitting the state, his wife Nausikaa burst into sobs, the mad dwarf-woman Galinthias howled by the funeral pyre, kissing in a frenzy the golden disk once given her by the dead man; sorrowful Autolykos, craftiest of the Hellenes, turned away from the crowd, hiding the telltale glint of his eyes...

Alkmene did not weep.

Not a tear, not a sigh, not a sob.

"Small wonder---after Zeus!" the Thebans whispered.

And when her sons came up to Alkmene together with Kreon of Thebes, a chill passed through all three of them---such was the look the departed man's wife turned on them.

His widow.

But a whole city can't look into one pair of eyes.

"I will give my daughters in marriage to the heroes: Megara to Alkeides, Asteropeia to Iphikles," Kreon mumbled, losing every word of his prepared speech in a single instant.

Alkmene said nothing.

"I... I will raise such a tholos for great Amphitryon as no basileus was ever buried in," Kreon said, not knowing what to say next.

Alkmene said nothing.

"We will raise a temple in Thebes, mom," said the twins. "A temple to the Father."

"Yes, that's right," Kreon said approvingly. "A temple to Zeus the Victor, father of gods and mortals."

"A temple to the Father," the brothers answered as one, as though they had not even heard the basileus, and Kreon thought it wisest to hold his tongue.

Alkmene walked up to the ashes and took a handful of embers and cinders.

And it seemed as though precious stones were throwing off sparks in the palm of the Thunderer's last woman.

"Lay this in the foundation," Alkmene said quietly.

And she poured the glowing ash into her sons' outstretched hands: half a handful to each.

Even Kreon, a man of state, understood then: the flesh feels nothing when the heart is burning.

"Ha-a-ai, tempest over waters," Alkmene half-sang under her breath, walking away, "ha-a-ai, hundred-headed Typhon..."

And the brothers, who had been worried that their mother had lost her mind, suddenly seemed to hear the rumble of a chariot racing along the edge of a cliff between sea and sky.

As clearly as if they had heard it once before.

But the living live on, and time cannot be caught by the tail, however hard one tries.

Amphitryon the Exile came to rest in his grand tholos; within two years a temple had grown up which everyone called, aloud, the temple of Zeus, though the Thunderer's statue had its right biceps strangely chipped, as though it had knit badly after a sword blow; tireless Megara, Kreon's daughter, bore Alkeides gray-eyed Therimachos, then sturdy Deikoon, and finally squalling Kreontiades; Asteropeia, too, gave Iphikles two sons and a daughter, and was gentle with her husband's firstborn, delicate Iolaos...

Still, everyone---the daughters-in-law, the sons, the household---knew that Iolaos had been, was, and would remain his grandmother's favorite.

Because he was the first.

Because he was sickly.

Because he had grown up in Alkmene's arms, calling his grandmother "mama" until he was four.

Because his face bore an elusive resemblance... just don't ask Alkmene---to whom?

She won't answer.

So now too, when six-year-old Iolaos had come to himself after a fever that had lasted nearly two weeks, Alkmene was afraid to answer even herself: why had her beloved grandson changed so, and what was it she kept seeing in the little boy's eyes?

And in any case, she did not know the answer.

Never before has little Iolaos avoided the adored grandmother who replaced the mother the boy never knew---but now he won't let himself be petted, twists out of every embrace, keeps trying to slip past her; you kiss him almost by force, and he looks right past you, mortal anguish in his eyes, as though he weren't being kissed at all but cut to pieces. The boiled goat's milk he has always drunk twice a day---he poured it out onto the ground. The nurse tried coaxing first, then force---and he turned on her so savagely that the poor woman had nightmares for ten whole days afterward; and he demanded meat instead. Roasted, with the blood still in it. And then, he's never been much for playing with children his own age, but now he's stopped altogether. He hangs about the grown-ups. And he's silent. An unwholesome silence, an old man's silence. Telemos the Nasal, a former watchman, died the other day---and the moment Iolaos heard of it, he demanded to be taken to the funeral. Said he was going either way. What is old Telemos to him?! Less than nothing. And yet there he stood by the pyre, muttering something, biting his lip, and in his eyes...

Gods, what are you punishing me for?!

Who can she complain to? Nausikaa?---that one's daughters have bred her so many grandchildren of her own that she has no time to spare for someone else's Iolaos...

So now, too---Alkeides has gone off to Troizen again, on trading business, Iphikles is getting ready to leave for the palaestra, to his pupils, and Iolaos comes up to his father with a rolling, unhurried gait.

"Dad," he says...
