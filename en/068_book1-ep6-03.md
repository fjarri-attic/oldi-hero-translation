#### 3

...Alcmene knew that in this life she had nothing left but her grandsons.

She had known it for almost five years now --- since the very day Thebes threw open its gates to the victors come home. Many, no doubt, condemned her then, the wife of the fallen lawagetas, who had not once wept over her husband's body. Everyone grieved for Amphitryon: the living, and the kin of the living --- giving thanks for that mercy; those whose houses had been emptied --- seeing in the lawagetas their own kin, left behind in the accursed valley; basileus Creon shed a stingy, official tear, his wife Nausicaa burst into sobs, the mad little dwarf-woman Galinthias howled by the funeral pyre, kissing in a frenzy the golden disk once given her by the dead man; sorrowful Autolycus, craftiest of the Hellenes, turned away from people, hiding the telltale glint of his eyes...

Alcmene did not weep.

Not a tear, not a sigh, not a sob.

"Well, of course --- after Zeus, no less!" the Thebans whispered.

And when her sons came up to Alcmene together with Creon of Thebes, a chill passed through all three of them --- in such a way did the departed man's wife look into their faces.

His widow.

But an entire city could not look into one pair of eyes.

"I will give my daughters in marriage to the heroes: Megara to Alcides, Asteropeia to Iphicles," Creon mumbled, losing every word of his prepared speech in a single instant.

Alcmene said nothing.

"I... I will raise such a tholos for great Amphitryon as no basileus was ever buried in," Creon said, not knowing what to say next.

Alcmene said nothing.

"We will raise a temple in Thebes, mother," said the twins. "A temple to the Father."

"Yes, that's right," Creon approved. "A temple to Zeus the Victor, father of gods and mortals."

"A temple to the Father," the brothers answered as one voice, as though not even hearing the basileus, and Creon thought it wisest to hold his tongue.

Alcmene walked up to the ashes and took a handful of embers and cinders.

And it seemed: precious stones were striking sparks from the palm of the Thunderer's last woman.

"Lay this in the foundation," Alcmene said quietly.

And she poured the glowing ash into her sons' outstretched hands: half a handful to each.

Even Creon, a man of state, understood then: the flesh feels nothing when the heart is burning.

"Ha-a-ai, tempest over waters," Alcmene half-sang under her breath, walking away, "ha-a-ai, hundred-headed Typhon..."

And to the brothers, worried that their mother's mind had come unhinged, there suddenly seemed to come the rumble of a chariot racing along the edge of a cliff between sea and sky.

So clearly, as though they had already heard it once before.

* * *

But the living live on, and time cannot be caught by the tail, however hard one tries.

Amphitryon the Exile came to rest in his grand tholos; within two years a temple had grown up which everyone called, aloud, the temple of Zeus, though the Thunderer's statue had its right bicep strangely chipped, as if poorly healed after a sword blow; tireless Megara, Creon's daughter, bore Alcides grey-eyed Therimachus, then sturdy Deicoon, and finally squalling Creontiades; Asteropeia, too, gave Iphicles two sons and a daughter, and was gentle with her husband's firstborn, delicate Iolaus...

Though everyone --- the daughters-in-law, the sons, the household --- knew that Iolaus had been, was, and would remain grandmother's favorite.

Because he was the first.

Because he was sickly.

Because he had grown up in Alcmene's arms, calling his grandmother "mama" until he was four.

Because his face bore an elusive resemblance... just don't ask Alcmene --- to whom?

She won't answer.

So now too, when six-year-old Iolaus had come to himself after a fever that had lasted nearly two weeks, Alcmene was afraid to answer even herself: why had her beloved grandson changed so, and what was it she kept seeing in the little boy's eyes?

And she did not know the answer, besides.

Never before had little Iolaus avoided the adored grandmother who had replaced the mother the boy had never known --- but now he would not let himself be petted, twisted out of every embrace, tried at every turn to slip past her; you would kiss him almost by force, and he would look right through you, mortal anguish in his eyes, as though he were not being kissed at all but cut into pieces. The boiled goat's milk he had always drunk twice a day --- he poured out onto the ground. The nurse tried coaxing first, then compulsion --- and he turned on the nurse so savagely that the poor woman had nightmares for ten days afterward; and he demanded meat instead. Roasted, bloody. Again, he had not much cared for playing with children his own age even before, but now he had stopped altogether. He hung about the grown-ups. And he was silent. An unwholesome silence, an old man's silence. Thelem the Nasal, a former watchman, had died the other day --- and the moment Iolaus heard of it, he ordered himself taken to the funeral. Said he'd go, come what may. What was old Thelem to him?! Less than nothing. And yet there he stood by the fire, muttering something, biting his lip, and in his eyes...

Gods, what are you punishing me for?!

Whom could she complain to? Nausicaa? --- that one's daughters had bred her so many grandchildren of her own that she had no time to spare for someone else's Iolaus...

So now, too --- Alcides had gone off to Troezen again, on trading business, Iphicles had set out that morning for the palaestra, to his pupils, and Iolaus comes up to his father with a rolling, unhurried gait.

"Dad," he says...
