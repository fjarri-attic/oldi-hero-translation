#### 9

Beyond another bend in the road appeared a handful of mud-brick huts with reed roofs --- a village. There was nothing remarkable about it; in his campaigns Amphitryon had seen a great many such settlements --- some that had greeted the victor with jubilation, some that had stood in sullen silence, and some black and burned, with corpses on the thresholds of what had once been homes.

And villages like this one too --- peaceful, busy, as alike as twins in their daily cares.

Another time Amphitryon would have driven past without stopping, but now his attention was caught by a crowd of people at the edge of the village, gathered on three sides around something --- evidently the local altar, since a thick, sooty smoke was rising from it into the sky.

Amphitryon touched his charioteer's shoulder, and the man obediently reined in the horses. Amphitryon climbed down from the chariot and made his way toward the crowd, stretching his stiff legs as he went.

No one paid him much attention. So some wealthy traveler had stopped --- wanted to honor the gods along with everyone else, or simply decided to gawk --- what of it?

"...I bring these heavy ears of grain, and the fruit of our trees, and the oil of fragrant olives --- to you, young half-god, Nameless Hero, Slayer of Monsters, son of mighty Zeus and fair Alcmene, your last mortal woman, O Dias the Cloud-Gatherer..."

Amphitryon flinched in surprise, but no one noticed, and the lawagetas at once collected himself out of long habit, continuing to listen to the gray, wizened priest whose face resembled a mask carved from wood.

"...accept our offering, infant hero, accept what we bring you with a pure heart, and may your spirit grow strong, and your strength increase tenfold..."

The priest went on intoning something more, but Amphitryon was no longer listening.

These people knew! Here, in a remote and forsaken Argolid village, people knew that his wife had borne a son by Zeus; his, Amphitryon's, disgrace was turning, for them, into hope for a future hero, a Slayer of Monsters, and these downtrodden peasants were already making sacrifices to the child, seeing in him a deliverer to come.

They had no need of Amphitryon's son.

They had no need of one just like themselves.

They needed a half-god hero.

The downtrodden villagers and dread Zeus --- they needed one and the same thing.

"And they shall have him," Amphitryon thought bitterly, climbing into the chariot and slapping his charioteer on the back. "They forget that a half-god is, at the same time, a half-man... They shall have their hero!..."

The priest went on droning his litany, the people silently mouthed their prayers --- while the charioteer pricked the horses with his goad, and the team lunged forward at a gallop, as if sensing their master's mood, and Amphitryon's chariot (followed by the other two) vanished in a cloud of dust around the bend in the road.

"They will have their hero," Amphitryon muttered, clenching his heavy fists, "they will... O Zeus the rival, is this truly your answer?!"

The sky remained silent, and slowly darkened.

By the time it had scowled over completely, and the chariots had rushed far away from the Argolid village, four figures emerged from the murky shadows and made their way toward the village altar, standing alone amid the night's silence.

One of the newcomers, wrapped in a woolen pharos, walked unhurriedly in front; two others, in short chitons belted with plain rope, led the last of them by the arms --- a completely naked man of middle years, whose body seemed to have been rubbed with oil, for the skin of the one being led gleamed, reflecting the ghostly light of the rising moon.

The naked man breathed fast and heavy, the whites of his eyes, wide with terror, all but glowing in the surrounding darkness, yet he walked without resisting, moving his stiff legs as if in a trance.

And his was the face of a slave.

The one walking in front stopped at the altar, looked unhurriedly around, raked together the remains of some brushwood at the western side of the altar, tucked a wisp of hay beneath it, and struck his flint several times. Sparks flew, a timid flame caught --- and the fire began to grow.

The two holding the naked man, as if obeying some unheard command, began a low howling moan, but through their half-bestial wailing an inner rhythm distinctly emerged --- a rhythm that pulled you in, that stripped away the will; soon they were swaying from side to side like the possessed --- only the one standing at the altar remained motionless.

And his strong, deep voice wove into the howling chant, overlapping it, rising above it:

"Take, O Deliverer, infant, take, O Nameless Hero, take it --- not willed by your father, but willed by those elder than Thunderer..."

"Tartarus! I hear Tartarus!" two voices joined in at once. "We hear you, our fathers!... not long now... not long to wait..."

"By the will of the Fallen we make you this night sacrifice, silent tribute, after the custom of our forefathers --- not those enthroned on Olympus, naming themselves as gods, but those who were cast down before their season..."

"Tartarus! I hear Tartarus!..."

The priest in the mantle went on shouting words --- unintelligible, inhuman --- while his two helpers had already thrown the victim onto his back on the altar. The naked man's hair crackled from the heat, his spine seemed about to snap, but he stayed silent, only his eyes widening further still in unspeakable terror.

"Blood of our offering, pour forth as a river, a river seething, flowing down into Tartarus; a river from the Fallen unto the Hero, river from mouth to source, from the days now past to the days that are with us... O bronze-necked ones, O serpent-legged ones, O you who yielded to the treachery
worked by your rivals --- worked by your own begotten children..."

"Oh-h-h... our fathers... not long left to wait!"

A flint knife, dully catching the glints of the fire, went with a crunch into the victim's left side; the body jerked, but not a sound escaped his tightly clenched lips. The blade moved evenly upward at a slant, breaking through the ribs, then seemed to meet some obstruction... an elusive, circling motion --- and the victim's body went slack, and the priest triumphantly raised aloft a lump, black in the moonlight, still pulsing.

A heart.

Blood flooded the altar; some of it spilled into the fire and hissed indignantly, rising in a sweetish stench.

"I hear the Fallen," the priest's voice changed abruptly, rumbling like a lion's roar. "They are pleased. They bless the Nameless One. And the day is not far off when the Deliverer himself will make the Fallen abundant sacrifices."

* * *

At that same hour, in far-off Thebes, little Alcides thrashed about on his kline with a bestial snarl, tearing the coverlet to shreds and foaming at the mouth, while weeping Alcmene, together with two nurses, could not manage to calm him or even hold him down, until bitterly sobbing Iphicles clung to his brother.

"Alci-i-ides! They're bad! Don't answer them! Mama-a! They're bad! They're calling Alcides! Don't give him to them, mama-a-a!..."

But no one paid heed to the strange words that little Iphicles was shouting through his sobs.

In the morning the boys could not for the life of them recall what had happened in the night. Most likely both had simply had a bad dream. Then again, neither Alcmene nor the nurses much wanted to remember that nightmare either.

But neither could they forget it.

* * *

By then the fire in the Argolid village had already died down, the embers barely glowing, filming over with gray ash. The three had gone, dissolved into the night, carrying with them the secret of the terrible sacrifice --- and no one noticed as a youthful figure in a tattered, hooded chlamys detached itself from a wild walnut tree growing nearby.

"So that's how it is..." the youth said thoughtfully, addressing no one but himself. "A river of blood from the Fallen to the Hero? No one in the Family could have foreseen this --- not father, not uncle... not I."

Suddenly, as though he had come to some decision, the youth clapped his hands, leapt lightly --- and vanished. Had he flown off? Slipped away among the trees? Or had he never been there at all? Only imagined? But by whom, if there had been no one nearby?

Who can say?
