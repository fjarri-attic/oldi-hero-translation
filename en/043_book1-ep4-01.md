### Fourth epeisodion

#### 1

The boy dashed into the inner courtyard and stopped, breathing hard, glancing back over his shoulder.

Belated remorse was stirring in him, and for a moment the boy hesitated, wondering whether he oughtn't go back to the palaestra and honestly take the thrashing he'd earned. He knew it was wrong to talk back to a teacher, especially when the caustic Autolykos was his father's friend and the finest wrestling instructor in all of Hellas; besides, Autolykos was exceedingly vindictive, and therefore hardly numbered among those one could defy with impunity.

Go back, or not?!

The boy's name was Alkeides. A stranger would confidently have put him at sixteen, calling him more a youth than a boy, if not a young man outright---and would have been wrong by a bit more than three years. Yet it would never have occurred to any observer to compare Alkeides to a statue of young Apollo, or, say, Hermes the Athlete, those embodiments of youthful beauty and grace. The boy was not handsome---not, at any rate, by any commonly accepted standard. Adolescents---true to the very word, which comes from growing---shoot up fast, their movements impulsive, forever straining upward, toward the sky, as though Mother Earth were letting her impatient children go for a moment before drawing them back...

Alkeides, though, had stood firmly upon Mother Gaia almost since the day he was born. His teacher, Linos the kitharode, own brother to the divine Orpheus, remarked more than once that he stood a little too firmly. And he would curse when the boy yanked too hard on the kithara's strings---Alkeides's fingers suffered from no excess of tenderness, though few indeed could tear themselves free of their grip.

The boy resembled a grown man. And his stubbornness, too, was a man's stubbornness. Sullen and relentless it was, the stubbornness of the boy Alkeides, whom no one had ever yet called Herakles.

Go back, or not?!

It was simply that the boy still believed he was right, and not the teacher Autolykos---and never mind that Autolykos had seen the deaths of more than one of his enemies, while Alkeides had only ever fought other students in the palaestra! But why, why hadn't the teacher wanted to hear him out, to try to understand, instead of mocking him at once---viciously, cuttingly, humiliatingly, as only Autolykos knew how! It was so simple, after all---a duck under the arm and a lock on the neck, but not in a single motion, the way Autolykos taught it, but only after a short elbow-strike to the stomach, and, the instant the opponent went slack, to jerk his chin up with the shoulder of the same arm, press the back of his head down with the other palm, and... It's so simple---admit it, teacher!

The boy didn't even notice that his hands were already moving---slowly at first, with pensive unhurriedness, then faster, faster still, and by the very end, swift as two snakes, dragging along the sturdy, tensed body...

"Alkeides!" came a call from the house. "Alci-i-ides!"

The boy started, as though waking, and froze. Without moving, he watched the megaron's door fly open and, vaulting over the balustrade, a tousled copy of himself came running toward him; ran, and stopped ten paces away.

Like two identical statues carved by the same sculptor's chisel, they stood in the courtyard; except that the first boy was naked but for a light loincloth, while the second wore everyday clothes with a brown stripe along the hem, and held in his hand a bone plectrum from a kithara.

"Is Autolykos in there?" asked the second boy, flinging the plectrum to the ground and pointing a finger toward the fence.

Alkeides nodded.

"Excellent!" the second boy bellowed at the top of his lungs, tearing off like a whirlwind, and a moment later Alkeides was alone in the courtyard again.

His solitude, however, proved short-lived.

The door banged again, and a lean man of about forty appeared on the megaron's terrace, dressed out of season in a woolen pharos mantle over a long chiton. Anyone would have called the newcomer's face refined and attractive, but at the moment it was twisted into a grimace of fury and looked more like the mask of a Gorgon.

The man's dry, nervous fingers, betraying the musician in him, gripped tightly around a teacher's staff, forked at the end.

"Iphikles!" he shouted, running down the steps, his sandals clattering loudly. "So there you are, you wretch!... Shed your chiton, did you---thought I wouldn't recognize you..."

"I didn't---" the boy began, but the teacher's staff had already come down on his shoulders, its fork scraping his cheek till it bled.

Two more blows followed, one after another. Linos the kitharode was no warrior, though he struck hard and mercilessly, lost in his own irritation---otherwise, raising the staff for another blow, he would have noticed how the boy's eyes blazed with a mad fire, and how, from a bitten lower lip, an almost invisible drop of blood fell to the ground; fell like an offering onto the altar of Ares Enyalios, the bloody son of Zeus, hateful to his own father.

No, teacher Linos was no warrior, and so he brought the staff down a fourth time, missing and doubling over from a sharp elbow-strike to the stomach, and Alkeides's right arm closed round his neck like a merciless ring, forcing his chin---smooth-shaven in the Mycenaean fashion---up at an absurd angle, while the boy's left palm settled on the back of Linos's head, almost tenderly; Alkeides pivoted, dropping to one knee, teacher Linos's legs kicked, leaving the ground, and there came a faint crack, the kind a dry branch makes when it snaps...

Autolykos had been wrong.

The boy knelt beside teacher Linos's body, and only one thought throbbed in his mind, like an inflamed wound: [Autolykos is wrong. Wrong. Wrong...]{.thoughts}

And in Alkeides's dry eyes, a dark flame was slowly dying down.

"You did it on purpose!" came a cry from the gate. "You made Autolykos angry on purpose! And he punished me for it! You did it on purpose, that wasn't what we agreed!"

"Yes, Iphikles," Alkeides whispered, barely audible. "That wasn't what we agreed..."

Iphikles suddenly fell silent, looking like a runner who'd slammed at full speed into a stone wall that had appeared out of nowhere, and stared wide-eyed at his brother and at Linos's corpse, its neck twisted at an unnatural angle.

Beside the teacher's head lay the bone plectrum, half-buried in the dust.

"It's my fault," Iphikles said quietly, subdued in an instant, coming closer. "I broke a string and ran off. And he..."

Alkeides said nothing, looking now at his own hands, now at the dead Linos.

"You didn't have a fit," Iphikles said flatly. "I know for certain you didn't."

Alkeides nodded slowly.

"You'll be tried." Iphikles frowned, and his face at once looked years older. Had their mother seen Iphikles at that moment, she would have flinched and looked away---Alkmene had already seen that face once before: the face of Amphitryon, when he accidentally killed Elektryon, ruler of Mycenae and Alkmene's father.

"You'll be tried. Even if I say it was my fault---it's you they'll try."

"He beat me unjustly," Alkeides said, hunching his shoulders, glancing about him hunted, like a fox ringed by hounds. "It's not my fault."

"Yes. Linos beat you unjustly, and Autolykos beat me unjustly. Only Autolykos is alive, and Linos isn't, and nothing else matters."

"I'll be tried." Alkeides, it seemed, had only just understood this.

"Yes. And at best, Father will have to exile you. To Phokis, or to Kithairon. For a long time. Maybe forever."

Alkeides got to his feet and, with an effort, tore his eyes from Linos's body.

"We'll go together," Iphikles touched his brother's welted shoulder, straightening his torn clothes with his other hand. "To Hyperborea, to Tartaros, wherever---I'll go with you. I don't know how yet, but they'll have to exile me too."

The twins looked at each other and smiled crookedly.

"A hero stands alone?" Alkeides asked, joyless.

"Yes," Iphikles answered, very seriously. "A hero stands alone. It's not our fault there are two of us... and that everyone wants something from us."

Already at the entrance to the megaron, Iphikles held his brother back for a moment.

"With the elbow?" he asked. "Elbow to the stomach? And down on the knee after the lock?"

Alkeides nodded.

"I thought so," Iphikles said sadly. "I thought so... only I never got the chance to show anyone. Not even Autolykos---he kicked me down at once. And they all laughed---the beanpole Polyktor, and Lykomedes, and Pausanias the discobolus, and... You should've thrown Autolykos instead---his neck's tougher!"
