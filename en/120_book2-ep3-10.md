#### 10

This festival, with its faint aftertaste of hysteria, which had seized three of the six regions of the Peloponnese, bore little resemblance to ordinary celebrations.

It was more like a challenge.

And the festival's name was---the rout of Heracles.

Basileus Augeas forgot his greed and remembered that he was the son of either Helios or Poseidon (certainly not of the rebellious blasphemer Phorbas the Lapith!), and in a trice became a lavish host. The farmers in the valleys of the rivers Alpheus and Peneus, relieved of a good third of their taxes, sang the praises of the wise Eleian basileus in chorus. The herdsmen from the grass-rich lowlands wiped mouths smeared with the sweet fat of gifted calves and lambs, and loudly extolled generous Augeas. The priests of the sacred grove of Olympia, which lay within Eleia's borders, could not fill their cellars fast enough with the pious basileus's gifts, and interpreted the silence of the gods in the required fashion.

Even the beggars who flooded carousing Eleia in those days diligently chewed flatbread with goat cheese and, grown kindly on a full stomach, whispered to one another:

"You know, friend, that son of a bitch Augeas maybe isn't such a bastard after all!.."

Then the beggars would sling their holey bags over their shoulders and set off north, to Messenia.

To the domain of Neleus of Pylos.

Where the alms were no less abundant.

O Messenia!.. O golden fields of barley and wheat, O orchard boughs sagging under the weight of fruit, O heady offspring of the grapevine, and thick oil---gift of the Messenian olives!

O wisest of the wise, wanax Neleus of Pylos!

How right you were, long ago, to refuse cleansing to the mad Heracles!

And the sweet-voiced rhapsodes sang in the streets and squares of Pylos, not neglecting, for that matter, Neleus's palace either:

The rhapsodes sang with feeling, recalling bygone days, running their fingers over the strings of their kitharas---and wanax Neleus threw open his spacious storerooms, ordered fires kindled in the outer courtyard and bulls and sheep slaughtered, drenching the ground with steaming blood.

Make merry, Messenia, in honor of the victory over Heracles!

Smoke rose to the sky from the Pylian altars, smoke tickled the nostrils of the immortals---true, even before this Pylos had passed over Zeus the Cloud-Gatherer in its prayers, preferring his brothers, the rebellious Poseidon the Earth-Shaker and gloomy Hades, as well as Hera, keeper of the hearth, and violent Ares the Killer.

But now they avoided glorifying Zeus doubly: it's unseemly to pray to the father after routing the son!

But to the other gods, who stood for Pylos against Heracles himself---we sacrifice with all our hearts!..

Smoke drifted over Messenia, smoke crept east, toward the high mountain ridge of Taygetus, where it mingled with other sacrificial smoke flowing from the far side of Taygetus, from the Spartan altars.

Sparta and all of Laconia rejoiced no less than their allies.

But for the violent Spartan, the fearsome basileus Hippocoon, a peaceful festival was not enough. Feed the beggars---by all means; lower the taxes---as much as you like; give the gods their due---for gods' sake, go ahead, all the more so since, as they say, whoever brings up the past---well, you know the rest...

But where is the blood?!

Where is the blood that gladdens a warrior's eye?!

And blood was found.

A dog---one of the pack of wolfhounds of the fierce Molossian breed that guarded Hippocoon's palace---attacked a youth who happened to be passing by the palace. The youth proved no pushover: he picked up a heavy stone from the ground and dispatched the rabid cur to its own doggy Hades---but just then basileus Hippocoon ran out of the palace together with his sons.

Quite a pack themselves, worse than any Molossian hounds...

It turned out that the insolent youth, who had not let himself be devoured, was named Oeonus, son of Licymnius.

Licymnius?

The one who's the half-brother of Alcmene, Heracles's mother?!

The youth named Oeonus, guilty of kinship with Heracles, died long and in torment.

Within a week the whole Peloponnese knew what had happened.

And Hippocoon, having slaked his thirst for blood and taken a human life for a dog's, not only did not seek cleansing from gods or men, but flaunted his deed in every way he could.

The rhapsodes, though, for some reason declined to extol the Spartan basileus's feat.

Happiness overflowed three of the six regions of the Island of Pelops[^82]; the fourth region, coastal Achaea, kept neutral; Arcadia and Argolis maintained a sullen silence.

[^82]: The Island of Pelops is the Peloponnese.

In gold-rich Mycenae, too, they kept silent at first, and then, to everyone's surprise, the Mycenaean wanax Eurystheus sent messengers to the triumphant rulers with his congratulations on the victory over Heracles.

The former master could not forgive his former servant.

Besides, it did not suit Mycenae to see Tiryns, already insolent beyond all measure, grow stronger.

Perhaps in those days there was no name more famous than the Molionids---the conjoined freak twins, sons of Augeas's sister Molione by who knows whom; the fighters who had made the invincible son of Zeus falter and retreat; the heroes who had slain Heracles's shadow, the one named Iphicles.

All the Achaeans knew for certain that it was Iphicles Amphitryad who lay buried in the grave on the hill within the bounds of the little town of Pheneus.

Who else could it be?!

The Molionids were pleasing to the gods---how could it be otherwise? The Molionids were great warriors---had they not proved it in deed? The Molionids were the mightiest of mortals---having defeated the mightiest before them!

The Molionids were the wedge that Ananke the Inevitability had driven into the myth of Heracles, splitting the labors of Heracles away from the living man, a man capable of losing a battle and losing a brother.

Pity only that the glorious Molionid brothers rarely left the confines of Augeas's palace, denying the grateful Eleians a chance to express the feelings that overflowed them. As it was---the finest garments were presented to the twins grown together into one (the sons of Molione reminded one of someone---but of whom?!), the finest food went to their table, the finest girls lay down beneath the tireless Molionids, their deformity notwithstanding...

Only the word "conjoined" somewhat puzzled those who saw the Molionids for the first time---no visible membrane joined the brothers, although...

Had anyone managed to peer at night into the chambers of both sons of Molione at once, this imaginary observer would surely have been struck by the complete synchrony of the Molionids' actions. When one bent over a waiting concubine---the other did the same; when one entered a moaning woman---the other did the same, in the same position; and a violent orgasm seized them both and left them both in one and the same second.

Once, a concubine of one of the brothers, late for the appointed hour, was running down the corridor and, as she ran, pressed her eye to the crack of a door leading to another's chambers, feasting on the spectacle of another's passion. Without watching to the end, she dashed on, burst into her master's room---and froze on the threshold.

The creature on the kline was making the same movements as its copy, only without a woman; and the late concubine thought, with a shudder: was it not a single being, made up of two enormous bodies, that was now making love in different chambers?

At dawn the strangled concubine was quietly buried at the foot of the hill behind the palace.

But the peak of the Molionids' dazzling fame, which had flared up like lightning, came a little later---when the time came for the sacred contests, the third Isthmian Games.

The grateful people of Eleia put forward their saviors as theoroi, sacred envoys---and watched, deeply moved, as the two young giants made the necessary sacrifices, put on white garments, took branches of laurel and magnolia in their hands...

And ran east, toward the Isthmos.

Neither god nor man would have dared raise a hand against theoroi cleansed of every miasma---otherwise, whoever he might be, the wrath of the Olympians would inevitably have come crashing down on the impious wretch.

Robbers gave way to the theoroi, herdsmen and farmers considered it their duty to warm and feed them, wild beasts did not touch the envoys...

But in Cleonae, halfway along the road, death barred the way of the running Molionids.

Death in a shabby lionskin.
