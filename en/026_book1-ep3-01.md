### Third epeisodion

#### 1

"Stifling," Autolycus said with some irritation, wiping his face with a cloth he'd thought to bring along. "There'll be a storm, I'd wager..."

"I'll say," Amphitryon answered in the same vein, eyeing the naked wrestler with envy. "Helios has gone mad, it seems... Easy for you, Autolycus, but you could wring my clothes out! And I can hardly go home naked..."

Both men sat on the edge of the curb bordering the jumping pit, casting longing glances at a distant ridge of leaden clouds; a third man, Castor Dioscurus, lithe as a mountain lion, tinkered a little way off with a practice spear --- whether weighting the head or simply killing time.

A muffled yell carried from the covered gymnasium.

"They've caught Polyctor," Autolycus reported with satisfaction, flaring his nostrils. "Hear that, Castor? They're thrashing your little favorite! Told you yesterday --- keep him away from Alcides, don't let him feed him sand... Well, he's fed himself into it, the lout! Now the little Amphitryads will treat him to something worse than sand, while Polyctor's retinue splashes about in the washroom..."

An unkind smirk twisted Castor's fine features. Anyone seeing Dioscurus for the first time risked underestimating the fair-haired pretty boy, but after a smirk like that, no one would have chosen to count Castor among his enemies. Quick-tempered, proud, forever striving to yield nothing to his brother Polydeuces in anything --- though their mother, Leda from Aetolia, had named the brothers "Dioscuri," that is, "youths of the Thunderer," common gossip held that only Polydeuces was Zeus's son --- Castor suffered from a wounded vanity and a complete absence of humor.

"A generation of heroes," Amphitryon suddenly recalled. Yes, blind Tiresias had said as much, more than once, whenever another fit of talkativeness came over the old man. "All of us," Tiresias would say, "are a generation of heroes; warriors, seers, fratricides, sages, madmen, slayers of monsters and of men --- heroes... It isn't valor, or a badge of honor, or a privilege --- it's more of a sickness; or not even that --- it's some inborn trait, like the color of one's hair or the shape of one's nose."

Heroes.

Freaks?

A bridge between men and gods?

Orpheus the singer, who in his whole life had never stained his hands with another's blood; the murderous Pelopids Atreus and Thyestes; righteous Aeacus, mad Bellerophon, lying Tantalus, Perseus the Gorgon-Slayer; unpredictable Autolycus, stubborn Castor; he himself, Amphitryon Perseid, and his children, Alcides and Iphicles, at this very moment at war with the beanpole Polyctor, whose mother trumpets her descent from Bacchus-Dionysus on every street corner, and to prove it has taken to running with the maenads...

Heroes?

All of them?

Half-gods, gods by a quarter, a third, a fifth... fathers, sons, grandsons, great-grandsons?

What are we for?

What are we all for?!

* * *

...Over the past three years, since the founding of the new palaestra, Amphitryon had often found time for reflection. Life flowed measured and calm, unburdened by mishaps; the gods had forgotten about seven-gated Thebes, ceasing to indulge it with their perilous attention; Amphitryon was rich, well-born, renowned, favored by fate, happy in his wife and children, loved by his friends...

All of this was true.

Just as it was true that he still had not learned to forgive old grievances, or to take pride in favors granted from someone else's shoulder --- whoever that shoulder might belong to.

Amphitryon had simply learned to live beneath the point of an invisible sword.

And he had also learned of Alcides's fits of madness --- which came regularly, two or three times a year.

Six months after the lessons had begun, Alcides was seized right there in the palaestra; fortunately, it happened almost at sunset, when no one was about except Amphitryon and his sons, who had lingered by a broken chariot. Fortunately --- because when he tried to restrain the raging Alcides, Amphitryon was flung aside, struck the back of his head against the corner of the chariot's base, and lost consciousness for a moment.

Coming to, he saw Iphicles bent over his exhausted brother --- and did not ask who had stopped Alcides, or the demon that dwelt within him.

From that day on, Amphitryon, like Alcmene, never again confused the boys, telling them apart instantly and unerringly; and from that day on, he stopped bringing offerings to Hera.

"A generation of heroes," Amphitryon thought again.

A generation of the damned?
