### Third epeisodion

#### 1

"Stifling," Autolykos said with some irritation, wiping his face with a cloth he had had the foresight to bring. "There'll be a storm, I'd wager..."

"I'll say," Amphitryon answered in the same vein, eyeing the naked wrestler with envy. "Helios has gone mad, it seems... Easy for you, Autolykos, but my clothes are wringing wet! And I can hardly go home naked..."

Both men sat on the stone edging of the jumping pit, casting longing glances at a distant ridge of leaden clouds; a third man, Kastor Dioskouros, lithe as a mountain lion, tinkered a little way off with a practice spear---either adding weight to the spearhead or simply killing time.

A muffled yell carried from the covered gymnasium.

"They've caught Polyktor," Autolykos reported with satisfaction, flaring his nostrils. "Hear that, Kastor? They're thrashing your pet! Told you yesterday---keep him away from Alkeides, don't let him feed Alkeides sand... Well, he's fed him once too often, the lout! Now the little Amphitryads will treat him to something worse than sand, while Polyktor's retinue splashes about in the bathing room..."

An unkind smirk twisted Kastor's fine features. Anyone seeing Dioskouros for the first time risked underestimating the fair-haired pretty boy, but after seeing a smirk like that, no one would want Kastor for an enemy. Quick-tempered, proud, forever striving not to give his brother Polydeukes an inch in anything---though their mother, Leda the Aitolian, had named the brothers "Dioskouroi," that is, "youths of the Thunderer," common gossip held that only Polydeukes was Zeus's son---Kastor was marked by prickly vanity and a complete absence of humor.

[A generation of heroes,]{.thoughts} Amphitryon suddenly recalled. Yes, blind Teiresias had said as much, more than once, whenever another fit of talkativeness came over the old man. "All of us," Teiresias would say, "are a generation of heroes; warriors, seers, fratricides, sages, madmen, slayers of monsters and of men---heroes... It isn't valor, or a badge of honor, or a privilege---it's more of a sickness; or not even that---it's some inborn trait, like the color of one's hair or the shape of one's nose."

Heroes.

Freaks?

A bridge between men and gods?

Orpheus the singer, who in his whole life had never stained his hands with another's blood; the murderous Pelopids Atreus and Thyestes; righteous Aiakos, mad Bellerophon, lying Tantalos, Perseus the Gorgon-Slayer; unpredictable Autolykos, stubborn Kastor; he himself, Amphitryon Perseid, and his children, Alkeides and Iphikles, who are at this very moment fighting with the beanpole Polyktor, whose mother trumpets her descent from Bakchos-Dionysos on every street corner and, to prove it, has taken to tagging along with the maenads...

Heroes?

All of them?

Half-gods, gods by a quarter, a third, a fifth... fathers, sons, grandsons, great-grandsons?

What are we for?

What are we all for?!

...Over the past three years, since the founding of the new palaestra, Amphitryon had often found time for reflection. Life flowed measured and calm, untroubled by incident; the gods had forgotten about seven-gated Thebes, no longer favoring it with their perilous attention; Amphitryon was rich, well-born, renowned, favored by fate, happy in his wife and children, loved by his friends...

All of this was true.

Just as it was true that he still had not learned to forgive insults and to take pride in hand-me-down favors---whoever had worn them first.

Amphitryon had simply learned to live beneath the point of an invisible sword.

And he had also found out about Alkeides's fits of madness---which came regularly, two or three times a year.

Six months after the lessons had begun, a fit took Alkeides right there in the palaestra; fortunately, it happened almost at sunset, when no one was about except Amphitryon and his sons, who had lingered by a broken chariot. Fortunately---because when he tried to restrain the raging Alkeides, Amphitryon was flung aside, hit the back of his head on the corner of the chariot's bed, and lost consciousness for a moment.

Coming to, he saw Iphikles bent over his exhausted brother---and did not ask who had stopped Alkeides or the demon that dwelt in Alkeides.

From that day on, Amphitryon, like Alkmene, never again confused the boys, telling them apart instantly and unerringly; and from that day on, he stopped making offerings to Hera.

[A generation of heroes,]{.thoughts} Amphitryon thought again.

A generation of the damned?
