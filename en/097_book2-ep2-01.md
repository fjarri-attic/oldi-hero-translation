### Second epeisodion

#### 1

Alcides lay on the hot sand, arms flung freely behind his head, and gazed without much enthusiasm at the Trickster's shapely leg, the leg of a runner and a rogue, swinging back and forth right in front of Alcides's nose. The little wings on the backs of Hermeias's sandals fluttered feebly, as though the Trickster were still speeding off somewhere rather than sitting close by, on the bald crown of a boulder grown into the desert's flesh, one leg tucked beneath him, defying common sense with his foolish pose.

"Hello there, little sister," Hermeias giggled.

Alcides bent his knees, which made the woman's dress Omphale, queen of Maeonia, had dressed him in ride up almost to his waist; and the Trickster giggled again, eyeing the bared thighs thick with coarse black hair.

"It suits you," Hermeias said approvingly, sticking out his thumb and tilting his head, crowned with a Phrygian cap with drooping donkey ears.

Alcides rolled onto his side and closed his eyes.

"I swear by dad, it suits you!" the Trickster wouldn't let up. "Not planning on getting married?"

"I am," Alcides answered calmly.

"To whom?"

"To you."

"You can't marry me," Hermeias declared, dead serious, as though bent on confirming assorted indecent rumors in which the Trickster wasn't always cast in the most honorable role. "You can't marry me, little brother. You and I are close kin through dad. Our children would turn out badly. Worse than the Chimera. With your brains and my temper. Offspring like that belong only in the fire --- and not just any fire, either..."

"Guess I'll stay a bachelor, then," Alcides concluded, and spat --- without opening his eyes, and without deliberately aiming --- at the Trickster's sandal, and very nearly hit it.

"Come lie down with me, sweetheart," Hermeias purred, eyes narrowed, to the tune of a fashionable Tirynthian ditty, "you're a god, and I'm a god, and we'll tread the meadow sod."

Then he thought it over and corrected himself:

"I'm a god, you're not a god."

"You're a rogue and a clod," Alcides grunted into his beard. "May Hephaestus sear your..."

The wings on Hermeias's Talaria beat fiercely, after which he tucked his other leg beneath him too.

"Dad's worried," the Trickster said flatly, in an entirely different voice. "He says: the Gigantes are stirring at Phlegrae. He says: they'll likely come pushing this way soon."

"Let them come," Alcides shrugged. "What's it to me?"

Hermeias jabbed an accusing finger at him.

"To you --- plenty. To you it's very much something indeed. Understand?"

"No. You sold me into slavery. To a Libyan echidna. As a weaving-girl. For a whole three talents. And for us weaving-girls, your squabbles don't graze our hems... So off you fly, little dove! Flap your wings and don't keep me from resting after a hard day's work."

Without changing his pose, Hermeias floated smoothly up above the boulder and wriggled, settling himself more comfortably in midair. The Trickster's little cap had slid down over one ear, his pharos billowed in waves on the wind... a jaunty smirk, fluffy girlish lashes, a barely restrained restlessness in every limb --- and the only thing spoiling the whole carefree, pleasant picture was his eyes.

The eyes of a snake in the underbrush.

"Shall I tell our dad just that?" Hermeias asked, without the faintest hint of menace; and anyone who knew the Trickster well would have sensed danger at once.

"Tell him just that."

"No, actually," the Trickster muttered with suspicious languor, "I don't think I'll tell him anything at all. I'll fly off to the girls instead. Or the boys. I'll tell dad I was guiding shades down to Hades, strained myself on the job, and so flew off to southern Aetolia to watch the Calydonian Hunt instead..."

The Trickster fell silent, staring intently at the steadily breathing Alcides --- as though waiting for something.

Nothing came.

"A boar's turned up at Calydon," Hermeias began again, "worse than your Erymanthian one! A horror with tusks! You're not around, so they've gathered all of Hellas to hunt it... there's the sanctimonious Peleus Aeacid, and Meleager the Invulnerable, and Amphiaraus the Seer from Argos, and that fop Pirithous, and Atalanta the Virgin; and the old boys Argonauts, every last one of them! Heroes galore!"

Alcides scratched his sinewy shin; thought about it, and scratched harder.

"That's for certain," he agreed placidly. "They'll start elbowing each other, then blunder in blindly in a mob, skewer one another full of javelins, wait for the boar to die laughing, and then start arguing over who gets the stinking hide."

"So maybe I should give them some advice? On behalf of the slave Heracles?"

"Go ahead. Tell them not to get drunk before the drive."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

And when the offended Hermeias sped off toward the bend of the murky Cayster, Alcides didn't even raise himself up to watch the Trickster go.

Hermeias looked back three times before he was convinced of it.

* * *

Iphicles was waiting for the Trickster by the vineyards.

"Well?" he asked impatiently, stepping toward Hermeias.

Before answering, the youth-god caught his breath, plucked a juicy grape from the cluster in Iphicles's hand, and popped it into his mouth.

"Astonishing!" Hermeias finally breathed out. "I kept waiting for him to grab me by the leg and crack the back of my head against the boulder! I tried everything: needled him about the women's dress, dragged in the Gigantes, brought up our shared dad --- three years ago that would have set him off for certain! --- and the Calydonian Hunt... This way and that, I trampled on his pride, tried to scare him, smeared his self-respect with mud --- nothing! Not so much as a blink. And by the end he'd actually fallen asleep. How did you manage that, Iphicles?"

"Me?" Iphicles said, surprised. "What do I have to do with it?!"

He was in no mood whatsoever to let anyone --- even Hermeias --- in on the details of Heracles's three years of servitude. Fortunately, Omphale of Lydia, who had taken the throne of Maeonia after her late husband Tmolus, had proven a highly practical and far from foolish woman. And, it must be said, she scrupulously carried out her obligations under their agreement, which boiled down, in essence, to one thing: tormenting Heracles by every possible and impossible means (short of maiming him). Sometimes it seemed to Iphicles that Omphale's former husband, Tmolus Tantalid, had died happily --- so inexhaustible was the queen's talent in that particular area. Through the whole first year of the servitude he'd had to watch Alcides with the closest attention, dreading that his brother wouldn't hold out and would snap, doing something terrible. By the middle of the second year, Omphale confessed to him one night (Iphicles had to work off Heracles's debts not only by subduing the local bandit brotherhood) that it was growing harder and harder for her to think up insults capable of driving Alcides out of his mind: he'd sit for weeks at the weaver's shuttle, laughing, as if he'd set himself the goal of weaving a perfectly smooth cloth; he listened with interest to the vilest inventions about his labors, from the first to the twelfth and back again; he discussed the composition of scented salves and face creams with the other slave-women with real enthusiasm, and looked on with utter indifference when Omphale dressed herself up in his famous lion's skin.

At the start of the third year, Iphicles nearly fainted when Alcides met him in the courtyard and said calmly, the way one speaks of something habitual, like an aching callus: "Around midnight I had an fit." What mattered wasn't who had made a human sacrifice to Heracles, or where; what mattered was that Iphicles himself had felt nothing at all. He hadn't even had nightmares.

"You know," Alcides went on, frowning thoughtfully, "I wasn't even afraid. Not at all. As if it didn't concern me; as if I --- Omphale of Lydia's slave-woman weaver --- were watching from outside... no, not even watching, just listening to someone tell the story of the mad Heracles, son of Zeus. For the first time I understood where I was; for the first time I could tell visions from reality; for the first time I felt that all I had to do was wait it out --- not run, not be afraid, not fight, just wait, doing nothing at all, because it's like a woman's pregnancy, which sooner or later ends by itself --- and everything falls back into place. For the first time I wasn't a hero; and I wasn't a madman."

Alcides fell silent and walked on, off about some business of his own, while Iphicles stood there watching his brother go, feeling a rainbow shimmer in his own tear-swollen eyes.

It was then that Iphicles swore to himself that he would raise a temple to Asclepius --- no matter where, in Pergamon, Epidaurus, or on Kos[^60].

[^60]: A little later, Pergamon, Epidaurus, and the island of Kos would become centers of the cult of Asclepius.

For it was Asclepius, the great healer whom everyone but himself considered a god, who had given the advice that led Heracles into servitude to Omphale of Lydia. "There's a door in each of us that leads into Tartarus," Asclepius had said, when he and Iphicles sat on the terrace of his small, cozy house in Athens, "not in Heracles alone. And that door only opens from the other side. It's madness to fight Tartarus; it's madness to be afraid of it or flee from it, for the Tartarus that has entered me is already 'I'. Let Heracles master himself; let him understand that the madness is himself too; and then Tartarus will come and go, and Heracles will remain."

They had talked for a long time that day, Iphicles Amphitryad and Asclepius, son of Apollo; they drank herb-scented wine brought by quiet, dark-skinned Epione, the healer's wife, who had borne him sons Machaon and Podalirius, and two more daughters, Hygieia and Panacea; they parted only at sunset... and Iphicles still couldn't believe that almost three years had passed since that day.

It had been yesterday; or no --- today.

If that meeting still lives in me, then it is today. And nothing else matters, just as it doesn't matter that a twenty-oared galley already waits in the harbor of Phocis, and that on the other side of the Aegean, from Thessaly to Aetolia, relay chariots stand ready so that two more men can turn up among the participants of the Calydonian Hunt in time: Iphicles, son of Amphitryon, and Iolaus, son of Iphicles.

This had been arranged with Alcides a week before.

Iphicles unconsciously clenched his hand into a fist, and the juice bursting from the grape cluster splashed his chiton.

"You know, Hermeias," he said, apropos of nothing, looking at the laughing Trickster, "what day is it today?"

"What day?" the young god raised his eyebrows, uncomprehending. "A sunny one?"

"No. Today I turned exactly forty years old. Both I and Alcides. Do you understand, god, what that means --- forty years?..."

The grape juice ran down Iphicles's arm in drops of blood.
