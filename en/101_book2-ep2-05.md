#### 5

Troy was taken.

It had already been taken back when nine fifty-oared ships quietly put out from Iolcus, leaving a disappointed Peleus and Theseus the Athenian behind on the shore --- of the well-known heroes, only the brawler Telamon set out along with Iolaus and Iphicles; all the others, whose disappearance would certainly have started a wave of rumors capable of reaching Troy before the Achaean ships did, Iolaus had made go back home.

It had already been taken back when three hundred Argives and Tirynthian veterans, in a suicidally brazen raid, drew the Trojans' attention onto themselves and managed to hold the narrow strip of land between the ships and the rampart (the very one they themselves had raised seven years before) for exactly as long as was needed.

It had already been taken back when six ships, having rounded Lesbos, put in at the southern tip of Cape Lectum, and Iphicles, taking more than five hundred warriors with him, managed to steal along the wooded slopes of Ida almost to the walls of Troy itself --- so as afterward to strike Laomedon's soldiers openly and unexpectedly in the back.

It had already been taken back when Alcides --- without any loud proclamation that his servitude to Omphale was over --- called to mind Chiron's teaching and rode on horseback from Maeonia to Cape Lectum, driving before him a herd of ten horses; there he took up Telamon, Lichas (not because the boy was a true-born Thracian, but because, having gone three years without a sight of his idol, he had simply lost his mind with joy) and seven others who, during the campaigns against Diomedes the Biston and the Amazons, had learned the horseman's art --- in short, ten false Thracians in all --- slipped into a crowd of refugees and, with excellent timing, made his way into Troy, lingering inside by the Scaean Gates.

Or it may be that Troy had fallen much earlier still --- when its king Laomedon joined the Brotherhood of Salmoneus, when he forced his own son Podarces off to Phlegrae like a bull driven into a herd; when he gave up Hesione as a sacrifice and then drove Heracles the savior away, vaunting the impregnability of his walls and the title of "living god."

It might all be true.

...Panic gripped the city.

No one so much as thought of resistance; the surviving soldiers obediently threw down their weapons, even when no one had asked them to; the townsfolk passed the fearsome name of Heracles from mouth to mouth and cursed arrogant Laomedon --- secretly hoping that the great son of Zeus would slake his thirst for vengeance without razing or too zealously plundering unfortunate Troy.

Terror and hope; fire in a thicket of human reeds.

Only in the agora, by the central altar, Laomedon of Troy stood frozen like an impassive statue, holding up a son who could barely stand --- a youth in dented, hacked armor, bareheaded, his hair matted with blood.

And it was empty all around the king, who gripped a knife in his hand.

That was how he was remembered by the twins who stepped out of the alley: Alcides bare to the waist, and Iphicles in the lion skin over his cuirass.

Around the brothers, the excited voices slowly fell silent --- Telamon broke off; Lichas lost his thread mid-sentence; one warrior fell silent, then another, a third... a fifth...

"I have just, by the right of a king, reconsecrated this altar," Laomedon said dryly, shifting his gaze from Alcides to Iphicles. "It is now an altar to Heracles the Victor."

A bewildered murmur among the Achaeans interrupted the Trojan.

"It is an altar to Heracles the Victor," Laomedon repeated with emphasis, addressing only the brothers. "You will at once order your men to leave the bounds of my city, but you yourselves --- all of you I see here --- will remain until my terms are met. Otherwise... otherwise I will sacrifice this man to Heracles. My son. Well?"

"And after this people still claim Heracles is the maddest man in Hellas?" Alcides took a step forward, not noticing the warriors behind him exchanging glances and shrugging, or Telamon bending down to pick up a stone the size of a child's head. "I don't even pity you, Possessed one: in your wish to become a god, you have already ceased to be a man. And yet I will tell you..."

Before Alcides could finish, Telamon impulsively shoved aside the Achaean blocking his way and, with a short swing, hurled the stone.

Two bodies, convulsing, fell onto the altar of Heracles the Victor: the youth in hacked armor, whose throat his father had managed to cut an instant before the flying rock caved in Laomedon's temple, and the Trojan king, a Possessed.

The father's blood mingled with the son's.

"Ha!" Telamon roared joyfully. "Whom the gods would destroy, they first make mad! The sacrifice is made, heroes --- glory to Heracles the Victor! Glory!..."

No one took up his cry.

Everyone slowly backed away from Alcides, as from a lightning bolt driven into the ground, as though Heracles were radiating a heat that grew with every passing moment, as though the Achaeans were afraid to look him in the face --- in the blind, statue's mask, beaded with salt dew, turned toward a place where there is no altar with two bodies, no Troy, no Telamon fallen silent mid-word, nothing at all, and yet something...

Only Iphicles, biting his lower lip, did not budge an inch --- shoulder to shoulder, he stood beside his brother, listening to the something breaking through out of nothing.

"This too is 'me,'" Alcides at last muttered, stirring, "this too is 'me'... Tartarus came and went --- and Heracles remained. And Heracles does not blame you, Telamon: you did not know what you were doing. Time... we always live in an unfortunate time, because there is no such thing as fortunate times. Because my time, too, is 'me'... no, you are not to blame, Telamon, but a hero's madness will someday kill, if not you, then your son.[^65]"

[^65]: Telamon's son, Ajax --- who will take part in the future Trojan War, and be the strongest of the Greeks --- will lose to Odysseus the contest for the slain Achilles's armor and, in a fit of madness, slaughter a herd of cattle, believing he is killing his dishonest comrades-in-arms; then fall on his own sword.

Iphicles walked up to the stunned Telamon and, without any windup, struck him in the jaw with the heel of his palm. The strongman was lifted off his feet and flung aside; he groaned, shook his head, and began heavily hauling himself back up, spitting red saliva.

"Do you know what that was for?" Iphicles asked coldly.

"I do," Telamon rasped through his bloodied mouth. "No one offers Heracles human sacrifices."

Iphicles nodded.
