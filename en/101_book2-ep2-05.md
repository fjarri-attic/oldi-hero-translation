#### 5

Troy was taken.

It had already been taken when nine fifty-oared ships quietly put out from Iolkos, leaving Peleus and Theseus the Athenian disappointed on the shore---of the well-known heroes, only the brawler Telamon set out along with Iolaos and Iphikles; all the others, whose disappearance would certainly have started a wave of rumors capable of reaching Troy before the Achaian ships did, Iolaos had sent back home.

It had already been taken when three hundred Argives and Tirynthian veterans, in a suicidally brazen raid, drew the Trojans' attention to themselves and managed to hold the narrow strip of land between the ships and the rampart (the very one they themselves had raised seven years before) for exactly as long as was needed.

It had already been taken when six ships, having rounded Lesbos, put in at the southern tip of Cape Lekton, and Iphikles, taking more than five hundred warriors with him, managed to steal along the wooded slopes of Ida almost to the walls of Troy itself---to then strike Laomedon's soldiers openly and unexpectedly in the back.

It had already been taken when Alkeides---without any loud proclamation that his servitude to Omphale was over---called to mind Cheiron's teaching and rode on horseback from Maionia to Cape Lekton, driving before him a herd of ten horses; there he picked up Telamon, Lichas (not because the boy was a true-born Thracian, but because, having gone three years without seeing his idol, he was simply beside himself with joy) and seven others who, during the campaigns against Diomedes the Biston and the Amazons, had learned the horseman's art---in short, a band of ten false Thracians---and, slipping into a crowd of refugees, got into Troy at just the right moment and lingered inside by the Skaian Gates.

Or it is possible that Troy had fallen much earlier still---when its king Laomedon joined the Brotherhood of Salmoneus, when he forced his own son Podarkes off to Phlegra like a bull driven into a herd; when he gave up Hesione as a sacrifice and then drove Herakles the savior away, vaunting the impregnability of his walls and the title of "living god."

Anything is possible.

* * *

...Panic gripped the city.

No one so much as thought of resistance; the surviving soldiers obediently threw down their weapons, even when no one had asked them to; the townsfolk passed the fearsome name of Herakles from mouth to mouth and cursed arrogant Laomedon---secretly hoping that the great son of Zeus would slake his thirst for vengeance without razing or too zealously plundering unfortunate Troy.

Terror and hope; fire in a thicket of human reeds.

Only in the agora, by the central altar, stood Laomedon of Troy, frozen like an impassive statue, holding up his son, who could barely stay on his feet---a youth in dented, hacked armor, bareheaded, his hair matted with blood.

And there was no one around the king, who gripped a knife in his hand.

That was how he was remembered by the twins stepping out of a side street: Alkeides bare to the waist, and Iphikles in the lionskin over his cuirass.

Around the brothers, the excited voices slowly died away---Telamon broke off; Lichas lost his thread mid-sentence; one warrior fell silent, then another, a third... a fifth...

"I have just, by my right as king, reconsecrated this altar," Laomedon said dryly, shifting his gaze from Alkeides to Iphikles. "It is now an altar to Herakles the Victor."

A bewildered murmur among the Achaians interrupted the Trojan.

"It is an altar to Herakles the Victor," Laomedon repeated with emphasis, addressing only the brothers. "You will at once order your men to leave the bounds of my city, but you yourselves---all of you I see here---will remain until my terms are met. Otherwise... otherwise I will sacrifice this man to Herakles. My son. Well?"

"And after this people still claim Herakles is the maddest man in Hellas?" Alkeides took a step forward, not noticing the warriors behind him exchanging glances and shrugging, or Telamon bending down to pick up a stone the size of a child's head. "I don't even pity you, Possessed one: in your wish to become a god, you have already ceased to be a man. And yet I will tell you---"

Before Alkeides could finish, Telamon impulsively shoved aside the Achaian blocking his way and, with a short swing, hurled the stone.

Two bodies, convulsing, fell onto the altar to Herakles the Victor: the youth in hacked armor, whose throat his father had managed to cut an instant before the flying rock caved in Laomedon's temple, and the Trojan king, one of the Possessed.

The father's blood mingled with the son's.

"Ha!" Telamon roared joyfully. "Whom the gods would destroy, they first make mad! The sacrifice is made, heroes---glory to Herakles the Victor! Glory!..."

No one took up his cry.

Everyone slowly backed away from Alkeides, as from a lightning bolt driven into the ground, as though Herakles were radiating a heat that grew with every passing moment, as though the Achaians were afraid to look him in the face---in the blind mask of a statue, beaded with salt dew, turned toward a place where there is no altar with two bodies, no Troy, no Telamon fallen silent mid-word, nothing at all, and yet something...

Only Iphikles, biting his lower lip, did not budge an inch---shoulder to shoulder, he stood beside his brother, listening to the something breaking through out of nothing.

"This too is 'me,'" Alkeides at last muttered, stirring, "this too is 'me'... Tartaros came and went---and Herakles remained. And Herakles does not blame you, Telamon: you did not know what you were doing. Time... we always live at the wrong time, because there is no such thing as the right time. Because my time, too, is 'me'... no, you are not to blame, Telamon, but a hero's madness will someday kill, if not you, then your son[\*](#fn-65){.noteref}."

Iphikles walked up to the stunned Telamon and, without any windup, struck him in the jaw with the heel of his hand. The strongman was lifted off his feet and flung aside; he groaned, shook his head, and began to haul himself heavily back up, spitting red saliva.

"Do you know what that was for?" Iphikles asked coldly.

"I do," Telamon rasped through his bloodied mouth. "Herakles is not offered human sacrifice."

Iphikles nodded.
