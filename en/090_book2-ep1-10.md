#### 10

And somehow, imperceptibly and unexpectedly for everyone, the contests drew to a close. The bows fell silent, the arrows stopped singing, the lucky ones finished laughing, the clumsy ones finished cursing, and by the last day --- the day of the basileus's hunt --- only five real contenders for the hand of Iole the Oechalian remained: the twins, no less surprised by this turn of events than anyone else; Augeas's younger son, whose name nobody had bothered to remember; then a peer of the twins, the stern Spartan Pronous with a monstrously burned face (a distant kinsman of Hippocoon, it seemed); and a certain Leodes of Crete, a handsome, sly fellow.

And no one knew that nearly everything --- names, events, hopes and failures, words and deeds --- would later be garbled a hundred times over, mangled beyond recognition, and lost beyond recall; no one knew, nor, in truth, meant to spoil his digestion with such useless musings.

* * *

"There's Iole!" Iphicles observed, watching the litter disappear into the gaping mouth of the gates, in which sat, strangely motionless, a very young girl with straight fair hair gathered into a knot at the back of her head. "At least we'll finally see what we've been fighting for!"

"And who's that?" the sleep-deprived Alcides yawned hugely and jabbed a hairy hand, without ceremony, toward the second litter, where a black-haired girl the bride's own age was watching the assembled men with interest --- unlike the fragile Iole, more like a young, fully formed woman. "She reminds me of Mother, somehow..."

"Laodamia, daughter of Acastus of Iolcus," Iolaus tossed off lazily, trying not to show how much Alcides's last words had stung him. "The one who sailed on the Argo..."

"Acastus's daughter?" the brothers marveled in unison. "How would you know that?"

They had to speak loudly, shouting over the failed suitors who had neither gone home nor cared to come along as spectators to the hunt, but had stayed behind in the courtyard --- drinking themselves into grief-stricken oblivion at someone else's expense, singing the praises of Dionysus Lusios[^52].

[^52]: Lusios --- "the Loosener," "the Deliverer."

"I know," Iolaus said, and left it at that; already the night before he had decided he would tell the twins everything only once they were back in Tiryns. "A little robin told me. Right in my ear. Well, lads, shall we go?"

And they moved off toward the gates along with everyone else, cuffing Lichas about the ears for variety's sake as he got underfoot --- the boy had gotten hold of a short sword from somewhere, mercifully a rather blunt one, and was now beaming with happiness, clearly hoping to distinguish himself, though he had yet to work out how.

...The road ran downhill at first, then began to wind, now and again pretending to be a barely visible sheep track, leading the hunters away from the coast into the wooded heart of the island; sunbeams sprayed sparks in every direction, cutting themselves on spearheads and the bronze fittings at the tips of bows; the numerous spectators chattered noisily, weapons clanking, in anticipation of fine sport; the damate courtiers loudly praised the steady hand and godlike marksmanship of their basileus and his sons; ahead, two patches of color --- soft pink and azure --- glimmered from the girls' garments, and the slap of the litter-bearers' sandals on the worn stones carried back along the line.

Some of the general excitement rubbed off on Iolaus as well, while flush-faced Lichas had run off ahead entirely, worming his way into the very thick of the crowd, where he lied through his teeth about the arrows in Alcides's quiver: those very famous ones, he claimed, dipped in the black gall of the Lernaean Hydra --- dangerous even to stand next to.

They believed him.

Only as they approached a forest clearing, across from which stretched a wide meadow rustling with fragrant grasses nearly as tall as a man, did basileus Eurytus finally call for silence, and soon began arranging the archers in a semicircle, facing the far tree line, from which the beaters --- sent out well before dawn --- were to drive the game.

The litters --- rigged into a sort of low seat for each girl --- were set up in the center, behind the archers' backs; Iole the bride sat with the same absent expression on her face as before; if what was happening around her stirred anything in her, it showed in no way at all. The cool morning wind toyed with loose fair strands escaping her hair at the temples, tugged at the hem of her light cloak, brazenly pressed a chill kiss to her cheek --- in vain. Iole remained motionless.

Laodamia, by contrast, took a lively interest in everything going on; she kept asking the litter-bearers and the stout, clumsy damate standing nearby about one thing or another, and studied the tense contenders for her friend's hand with undisguised curiosity; for a moment Laodamia's gaze lingered on Iolaus (he and Lichas had stayed on the right flank next to the brothers, unwilling to withdraw behind the trees, since no power on earth could presently have dragged Lichas away from his beloved Heracles, nor Iolaus away from his beloved Lichas); the girl thoughtfully knit her downy brows, as though trying to recall something, then turned to the fat damate --- and Iolaus, as if sensing the interest directed at his person, jerked his head up sharply...

At that moment, from the forest standing like a dark-green wall on the far side of the meadow, came the approaching sound of shouting, crashing, the clatter of bronze tympana; the branches swayed opposite the hunters, who stood ready to shoot, and a large stag burst into the open with one graceful bound and froze for an instant at the sight of so many people.

A perfect target.

Everyone understood as much.

And eight arrows sang almost as one across the meadow. It seemed not a single hunter had missed --- and Laodamia clapped her hands over her ears when the stag, crying out shrilly, almost like a man, collapsed into the grass in a shapeless heap.

Nothing showed at all on Iole's thin, translucent face.

The hunt had begun.

Next came two white-tailed roe deer. Bursting from the forest, they immediately, without stopping, went racing headlong along the far edge of the meadow that had turned so deadly for them.

Silence.

Not only did the still-distant noise of the beaters fall quiet, for no clear reason --- even the birds and crickets seemed to freeze in anticipation.

A bowstring barked once. A second. A third...

The nearer roe deer tumbled head over heels at a full run and vanished into the swaying wild grass; her companion began turning back toward the thicket, and in the next instant four arrows caught up with the animal: two struck the neck, two the flank.

Leodes the Cretan and Augeas's nameless son had not managed to loose in time. Languid Leodes, a little girlish-looking, lowered his bow with wistful regret and, striking a small pose, smoothed down his wild curly hair; Augeas's son, for his part, made no secret of his irritation, his eyes searching for someone on whom he might vent his temper.

A "someone" turned up almost at once, to the delight of everyone gathered. It was an entire flock of ducks, bursting up from beyond the treetops with a deafening quacking.

"Where in the world did they come from?" Iolaus still had time to think, watching the birds tumble down again and again, run clean through by arrows. No doubt every hunter knew whether he had hit or missed --- but the final tally would only be known later, once the servants gathered up the dead game and it became clear whose arrows were whose; and there was no danger of confusing the suitors' arrows with one another, if only because each man's were fletched differently.

"Iolie, what's that?" whispered Lichas, dancing nervously in place beside him, pulling Iolaus out of his thoughts.

"Where?"

"Over there, in the grass... a snake, is it?"

Sure enough, some fifteen or twenty orgyiai away, the grass was swaying in a thoroughly inexplicable way, as though something were crawling through it --- say, a large snake...

The hunters had no attention to spare for earthly matters just then --- their eyes were riveted to the sky and the wildly darting ducks.

A faint, barely perceptible chill stirred inside Iolaus --- his sense of danger rarely failed him, and had saved his life more than once, the first or the second one --- and without stopping to think, he touched Alcides's elbow, just as he was taking aim at yet another duck, spoiling his shot.

With his other hand Iolaus beckoned to the fat damate, who blinked in alarm; the damate himself was the least of his interest, but slung across the courtier's back, on its strap, hung a heavy double-bladed labrys with a shortened haft.

Alcides turned around irritably, ready to curse; beside him, as though he too had heard a silent shout, Iphicles turned as well, having just brought down a duck. Iolaus silently repeated Lichas's gesture, pointing at the trail of stirring grass --- the twins' faces changed, and Iphicles began dragging another arrow from his quiver --- and then, out of the corner of his eye, Iolaus still had time to notice the damate with the axe coming up and starting to speak, and basileus Eurytus staring at them in bewilderment...

And it was just then, evidently, that the failed suitors left drinking back at the palace, raising their cups to the health of the departed hunters, committed some tactlessness toward vengeful Dionysus --- because events suddenly slipped their leash and came rushing on as swiftly as a leaping leopard, the merry god's sacred beast.

Two leopards.

A male and a female.

The grass tassels swayed, spraying pollen, and spat out two spotted bolts of lightning; the seasoned male leopard, with a low, guttural growl, knocked down handsome Leodes the Cretan, who had been standing ahead of the rest --- a gap now opened in the semicircle of hunters, and shooting at the leopard was possible only at the risk of hitting one of the men, while between the beasts and freedom stood only the litters with the girls, who had not yet had time to grasp what was happening.

The leopard lashed its flanks with its powerful, supple tail and sprang a second time.

Two arrows struck the beast at once --- from above and below. Basileus Eurytus shot coolly and cleanly, as if on the practice range, accounting for everything except what could not be accounted for --- the enraged beast, for some reason, sprang not at the girls but at the hesitating Spartan Pronous; and Eurytus's arrow, loosed from the height of the basileus's giant stature, went into the leopard not at the base of the skull, as intended, but into the shoulder.

Alcides, meanwhile, with a short push off both feet, threw himself flat onto his back, backward and slightly to the right, holding his bow horizontal across his chest --- and the feathered oak shaft of the arrow buried itself halfway under the male's lower jaw, its bronze head shattering the vertebrae at the base of the skull and coming out the other side.

It was a fortunate death.

Instant and easy.

The convulsions did not count.

The more prudent female, streaking low over the very ground, slipped past Iolaus --- and he, nearly wrenching the unfortunate damate's head off, tore the hanging axe free of the fat man and slashed diagonally at the dodging spotted brute. The wounded animal roared, dropping onto its haunches; there was no time for a second swing, and Iolaus flung the axe at the female's bared snout, seized the accursed cat by the scruff of the neck --- and, as if she were a wrestling opponent in the palaestra, hurled her over himself and away, off toward safety, away from Iole, who had not so much as flinched, and shrieking Laodamia, who had scrambled with both feet up onto the litter as though that could save her.

Throwing aside his useless bow, Iphicles caught the yowling female out of the air, slammed her to the ground --- bleeding claw-marks now ran from the Amphitryad's collarbone to his right shoulder --- and brought his fist down twice on the animal's narrow, almost serpentine muzzle. There was a crack; the female twitched once, twice --- and went still.

For a moment everything froze --- even Laodamia choked on her own scream --- and the silence was torn apart by a wild, triumphant cry, tearing up from Lichas's very depths:

"A sign! A sign! An omen from the gods! Great Heracles has slain the beast! Great Heracles has beaten basileus Eurytus! Heracles is the victor! Glory!..."

"Glory..." croaked Leodes the Cretan, unharmed, on all fours, having lost a good deal of his languor.

"Glory!" Iphitus the Oechalian threw his arms up, and it suddenly became clear that he was taller than his father, only he stooped, unlike the basileus.

"Heracles is the victor!" barked the Spartan Pronous hoarsely, in soldier's fashion, and his terrible, burned face suddenly lit up with childlike joy. "Glory to the son of Zeus!"

"An omen!..." the spectators, having recovered their wits, took up the cry without troubling over particulars, and individual shouts drowned in the general rapturous, welcoming din.

Iolaus finally wiped his palms more or less clean of the sticky animal blood --- the grass turned out to be surprisingly coarse, all too ready to slice the skin --- and straightened up.

"Glory!... Eurytus has lost to Heracles!..."

Iolaus felt himself going deaf. All around him mouths opened and shut soundlessly, and there, standing opposite, was the basileus of Oechalia, Eurytus the archer, Eurytus the Possessed, watching joyfully bellowing Lichas and mud-streaked Alcides getting up off the ground with eyes gone white with hatred.

That is how men look at mortal enemies.
