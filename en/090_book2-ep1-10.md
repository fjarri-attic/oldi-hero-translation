#### 10

And somehow, without anyone quite noticing and to everyone's surprise, the contests drew to a close. The bows fell silent, the arrows stopped singing, the lucky ones finished laughing, the clumsy ones finished cursing, and by the last day---the day of the basileus's hunt---only five real contenders for the hand of Iole the Oichalian remained: the twins, no less surprised by this turn of events than anyone else; Augeias's younger son, whose name nobody had bothered to remember; then a young man the twins' own age, the stern Spartan Pronoos with a monstrously burned face (a distant kinsman of Hippokoon, it seemed); and a certain Leiodes of Crete, a handsome, sly fellow.

And no one knew that nearly everything---names, events, hopes and failures, words and deeds---would later be garbled a hundred times over, mangled beyond recognition, and lost beyond recall; no one knew, and truth be told, no one cared to spoil his digestion with such idle musings.

* * *

"There's Iole!" Iphikles observed, watching a litter disappear into the gaping mouth of the gates; in it sat, strangely motionless, a very young girl with straight fair hair gathered into a knot at the back of her head. "At least we'll finally see what we've been fighting for!"

"And who's that?" Alkeides, short on sleep, yawned hugely and jabbed a hairy hand toward the second litter without ceremony. A black-haired girl of the bride's age sat there, eyeing the assembled men with interest; unlike the fragile Iole, she looked more like a young woman in full bloom. "She reminds me of mom, somehow..."

"Laodameia, daughter of Akastos of Iolkos," drawled Iolaos, trying not to show how much Alkeides's last words had stung him. "The one who sailed on the *Argo*..."

"Akastos's daughter?" the brothers marveled in unison. "How would you know that?"

They had to speak loudly, shouting over the failed suitors who had neither gone home nor cared to come along as spectators to the hunt, but had stayed behind in the courtyard---drowning their sorrows at their host's expense, singing the praises of Dionysos Lysios[\*](#fn-52){.noteref}.

"I know," Iolaos said, and left it at that; he had made up his mind during the night to tell the twins everything only once they were back in Tiryns. "A little robin told me. Right in my ear. Well, boys, shall we go?"

And they moved off toward the gates along with everyone else, cuffing Lichas about the ears for variety's sake as he got underfoot---the boy had gotten hold of a short sword from somewhere, mercifully a rather blunt one, and was now beaming, clearly hoping to distinguish himself, though he had yet to work out how.

...The road ran downhill at first, then began to wind, now and again pretending to be a barely visible sheep track, leading the hunters away from the coast into the wooded heart of the island; sunbeams sprayed sparks in every direction, cutting themselves on spearheads and the bronze fittings at the tips of bows; the numerous spectators chattered noisily, weapons clanking, in anticipation of fine sport; the damate courtiers loudly praised the steady hand and godlike marksmanship of their basileus and his sons; ahead, the girls' garments showed as two patches of color---soft pink and azure---and the slap of the litter-bearers' sandals on the worn stones carried back along the line.

Some of the general excitement rubbed off on Iolaos as well, while Lichas, flushed red, dashed off ahead altogether, worming his way into the very thick of the crowd, where he lied through his teeth about the arrows in Alkeides's quiver: the very ones, he claimed, the famous ones dipped in the black gall of the Lernaian Hydra---dangerous even to stand next to.

They believed him.

Only as they approached a forest clearing, across from which stretched a wide meadow rustling with fragrant grasses nearly as tall as a man, did basileus Eurytos call for silence; soon after, he began arranging the archers in a semicircle, facing the far tree line, from which the beaters---sent out well before dawn---were to drive the game.

The litters---rigged into a sort of low seat for each girl---were set up in the center, behind the archers; Iole the bride sat with the same absent expression on her face as before; if what was happening around her stirred anything in her, she gave no sign of it. The cool morning wind toyed with loose fair strands escaping her hair at the temples, tugged at the hem of her light cloak, brazenly pressed a chill kiss to her cheek---in vain. Iole remained motionless.

Laodameia, by contrast, took a lively interest in everything going on; she kept asking the litter-bearers and the stout, clumsy damate standing nearby about one thing or another, and studied the tense contenders for her friend's hand with undisguised curiosity; for a moment Laodameia's gaze lingered on Iolaos (he and Lichas had stayed on the right flank next to the brothers, unwilling to withdraw behind the trees, since no power on earth could have dragged Lichas away from his beloved Herakles just then, nor Iolaos away from his beloved Lichas); the girl thoughtfully knit her downy brows, as though trying to recall something, then turned to the fat damate---and Iolaos, as if sensing he was the subject of interest, jerked his head up sharply...

At that moment, from the forest standing like a dark-green wall on the far side of the meadow, came the approaching sound of shouting, crashing, the clatter of bronze tympana; the branches swayed opposite the hunters, who stood ready to shoot, and a large stag burst into the open with one graceful bound and froze for an instant at the sight of so many people.

A perfect target.

Everyone understood as much.

And eight arrows sang almost as one across the meadow. It seemed not a single hunter had missed---and Laodameia clapped her hands over her ears when the stag, crying out shrilly, almost like a man, collapsed into the grass in a shapeless heap.

Nothing showed at all on Iole's thin, translucent face.

The hunt had begun.

Next came two white-tailed roe deer. Bursting from the forest, they streaked headlong, without a pause, along the far edge of the meadow that had turned so deadly for them.

Silence.

Not only had the still-distant noise of the beaters died away, for no clear reason, but even the birds and crickets seemed to freeze in anticipation.

A bowstring barked. A second. A third...

The nearer roe deer tumbled head over heels at a full run and vanished into the swaying wild grass; her companion began turning back toward the thicket, and in the next instant four arrows caught up with the animal: two struck the neck, two the flank.

Leiodes the Cretan and Augeias's nameless son had not managed to loose in time. Languid Leiodes, a little girlish-looking, lowered his bow with wistful regret and, with a touch of affectation, smoothed down his wild curly hair; Augeias's son, for his part, made no secret of his irritation, his eyes searching for someone on whom he might vent his temper.

A "someone" turned up almost at once, to the delight of everyone gathered. It was an entire flock of ducks, bursting up from beyond the treetops with a deafening quacking.

[Where in the world did they come from?]{.thoughts} Iolaos found a moment to wonder, watching the birds tumble down again and again, run clean through by arrows. No doubt every hunter knew whether he had hit or missed---but the final tally would only be known later, once the servants gathered up the dead game and it became clear whose arrows were whose; and there was no danger of confusing the suitors' arrows with one another, if only because each man's were fletched differently.

"Iolie, what's that?" whispered Lichas, dancing nervously in place beside him, pulling Iolaos out of his thoughts.

"Where?"

"Over there, in the grass... a snake or something?"

Sure enough, some fifteen or twenty orgyiai away, the grass was swaying in a thoroughly inexplicable way, as though something were crawling through it---say, a large snake...

The hunters had no attention to spare for earthly matters just then---their eyes were riveted to the sky and the wildly darting ducks.

A faint, barely perceptible chill stirred inside Iolaos---his sense of danger rarely failed him, and had saved his life more than once, whether his first life or his second---and without stopping to think, he touched the elbow of Alkeides, who was taking aim at yet another mallard, spoiling his shot.

With his other hand Iolaos beckoned to the fat damate, who blinked in alarm; the damate himself interested him least of all, but across the courtier's back, on a strap, hung a heavy double-bladed labrys with a shortened haft.

Alkeides turned around irritably, ready to curse; beside him, as though he too had heard a silent shout, Iphikles turned as well, having just brought down a duck. Iolaos silently repeated Lichas's gesture, pointing at the trail of stirring grass---the twins' faces changed, and Iphikles began dragging another arrow from his quiver---and then, out of the corner of his eye, Iolaos managed to catch the damate with the axe approaching, about to speak, and basileus Eurytos staring at them in bewilderment...

And it was just then, evidently, that the failed suitors left drinking back at the palace, raising their cups to the health of the departed hunters, committed some impropriety toward vengeful Dionysos---because events suddenly slipped their leash and came rushing on as swiftly as a leaping leopard, the merry god's sacred beast.

Two leopards.

A male and a female.

The grass tassels swayed, spraying pollen, and spat out two spotted bolts of lightning; the seasoned male leopard, with a low, guttural growl, knocked down handsome Leiodes the Cretan, who had been standing ahead of the rest---a gap had now opened in the semicircle of hunters, and shooting at the leopard was possible only at the risk of hitting one of the men, while between the beasts and freedom stood only the litters with the girls, who had not yet had time to grasp what was happening.

The leopard lashed its flanks with its powerful, supple tail and sprang a second time.

Two arrows struck the beast at once---from above and below. Basileus Eurytos shot coolly and cleanly, as if on the practice range, accounting for everything except what could not be accounted for---the enraged beast, for some reason, sprang not at the girls but at the hesitating Spartan Pronoos; and Eurytos's arrow, loosed from the height of the basileus's giant stature, struck the leopard not in the back of the head, as intended, but in the shoulder.

Alkeides, meanwhile, with a short push off both feet, threw himself flat onto his back, backward and slightly to the right, holding his bow horizontal across his chest---and the feathered oak shaft of the arrow buried itself halfway under the male's lower jaw, its bronze head shattering the vertebrae at the base of the skull and coming out the other side.

It was a fortunate death.

Instant and easy.

Not counting the convulsions.

The more prudent female, hugging the ground, slipped past Iolaos---and he, nearly wrenching the unfortunate damate's head off, tore the hanging axe free of the fat man and slashed diagonally at the dodging spotted brute. The wounded animal roared, dropping onto its haunches; there was no time for a second swing, and Iolaos flung the axe at the female's bared snout, seized the accursed cat by the scruff of the neck---and, as if she were a wrestling opponent in the palaestra, hurled her over his head and away from Iole, who had not so much as stirred, and from the shrieking Laodameia, who had pulled her feet up onto the litter as though that could save her.

Throwing aside his useless bow, Iphikles caught the yowling female in midair, slammed her to the ground---bleeding claw-marks now ran from the Amphitryad's collarbone to his right shoulder---and brought his fist down twice on the animal's narrow, almost serpentine muzzle. There was a crack; the female twitched once, twice---and went still.

For a moment everything froze---even Laodameia choked on her own scream---and the silence was torn by a wild, triumphant cry that burst from the very depths of Lichas's being:

"A sign! A sign! An omen from the gods! Great Herakles has slain the beast! Great Herakles has beaten basileus Eurytos! Herakles is the victor! Glory!..."

"Glory..." croaked Leiodes the Cretan from his hands and knees, unhurt and much less languid than before.

"Glory!" Iphitos the Oichalian threw his arms up, and it suddenly became clear that he was taller than his father; he simply stooped, unlike the basileus.

"Herakles is the victor!" barked the Spartan Pronoos hoarsely, in soldierly fashion, and his terrible, burned face suddenly lit up with childlike joy. "Glory to the son of Zeus!"

"An omen!..." the spectators, having recovered their wits, took up the cry without troubling over particulars, and individual shouts were drowned out by the general din of rapture and acclaim.

Iolaos finally wiped his palms more or less clean of the sticky animal blood---the grass turned out to be surprisingly coarse, all too ready to slice the skin---and straightened up.

"Glory!... Eurytos has lost to Herakles!..."

Iolaos felt himself going deaf. All around him mouths opened and shut soundlessly, and there, standing opposite, was the basileus of Oichalia, Eurytos the archer, Eurytos the Possessed, staring with eyes gone white with hatred at Lichas, who was whooping with joy, and at the mud-streaked Alkeides picking himself up off the ground.

That is how men look at mortal enemies.
