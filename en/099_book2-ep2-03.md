#### 3

"Pies! Barley pies with honey! Come and get 'em, prices down --- used to be a shekel[^61], now it's two!"

[^61]: A Hittite unit of currency, about ten grams of silver; one sixtieth of a silver mina.

"For you, and only between us! Five minutes ago a caravan arrived from Syria with cloth --- I'll put you together with Saaph the Syrian, and you pay me double the usual commission... Deal?"

"Oy, woman! Not even princess Hesione has anklets like these! What? Too dear?! Get away, bandy-legs, don't besmirch my good name!.."

"Planks! Who needs planks?! Cedar of Lebanon --- never rots, never cracks, a house to last generations! Planks!"

"Stop the thief!"

"Amulets! Amulets! Against the evil eye, against curses, against the pangs of love! Pay once --- give thanks for a lifetime!"

"Who's a stinking Sidonian?! Me --- a stinking Sidonian?! Where's my knife?! Who bought my knife?! Ah, right, you did..."

"Fish! Sun-dried fish!"

When the Achaeans told one another about the fair on the banks of the full-flowing Scamander, which ran past Troy rising on its hill, they would say: "There's everything there, even money!" And they were right: so strange a thing as money --- Phoenician ingots, or Hittite minas and shekels --- might well have been invented for no other reason than to justify a trip to bountiful Troy, proudly astride the crossroads of the land and sea routes running from the Aegean Sea into the Euxine Pontus. Caravans laden with timber, cloth, and metals; carts heaped high with grain and fruit; barefoot slaves and the servants of local craftsmen carrying their masters' wares to the market rows; potters, smiths, furriers, armorers, jewelers, harness-makers, beggars, wandering vagrants, petty thieves...

Here indeed was everything, even money; and those who had nothing, not even money, consoled themselves with the tired truth that money can't buy happiness, while keeping a sharp eye out on all sides for whatever might be lying around unguarded.

And amid the noise, jostling, and bustle of merchants, gawkers, and buyers, no one paid much attention at first to three ships that slipped out from behind the western island of Tenedos, visible on the horizon, and made full speed for the shore --- for the very spot where the god-equal Heracles himself had once come ashore with his companions, on his way home from the campaign against the Amazons.

At the spot where the heroes had landed, a long, deep trench with an earthen rampart still remained: behind this fortification Heracles had lain in wait for the sea monster that had come for its due --- the Trojan princess Hesione, sent to be devoured by her own father, Laomedon.

The ships' prows, carved with statues, all drove into the sand together; the raised oars fell still, leaving the blessed rays of Helios to dry the salt tears of the Nereids glistening on the blades; and three hundred armed men leapt onto the shore and ran toward the hushed merchants in a brisk, silent column --- and having reached them, the uninvited guests set about loading whatever goods caught their fancy onto the carts standing nearby, just as silently and briskly as before. Only when the first carts began rolling away from the market rows toward the raiders' ships did the famous fair realize it was being robbed.

And robbed so brazenly, so openly, that one might as well have thrown up one's hands to the sky and asked, rhetorically:

"How can this be?!"

"Just like this," one of the robbers called back on the run, and having tossed a dully clinking sack onto the next cart, dashed off after another.

The fair exchanged glances, shrugged, and, wailing and keening, rushed for the Scaean Gates --- the western entrance to Troy --- hoping less for the protection of the sturdy walls than for the timely help of Laomedon's soldiers.

And indeed --- warned by the tower sentries, who had spotted the pirate ships before anyone else --- the chariots of Laomedon and his sons were already rolling out through the gates, followed by foot soldiers clattering with weapons and armor, about a thousand strong in all.

"Out of the way!" the Trojan ruler strained his voice, cursing the insolent pirates and the thick-headed crowd blocking the road, first in his thoughts and then at the top of his lungs. Precious minutes ticked by one after another; the soldiers bogged down in the press of bodies, someone got trampled, someone else was about to be, and he objected at a shriek; in short, by the time the keepers of Trojan order --- unused to such upheavals (after Heracles's campaigns into Thrace and against the Amazons, raids on the outskirts of Troy had all but stopped) --- broke free onto open ground and raced at full tilt for the seashore, the pirates were already finishing the loading of their plunder onto the ships.

Laomedon, riding out in front, had only a moment to feel glad of the sea-raiders' greed --- apparently unwilling to leave any of the rich haul behind, the pirates were prepared to give battle, so as to let part of their band finish stowing the stolen goods on the ships' plank decks. This, however, was very nearly the only cause for gladness (aside from a threefold advantage in numbers), because the pirates had chosen an ideal spot for their defense: the trench and the rampart stood between them and the Trojans, and atop the rampart the emptied carts had already been piled.

The result was something like a fortress wall, curving in a semicircle with both ends anchored on the shore; so the Trojan soldiers found themselves forced, like it or not, to storm fortifications standing on their own home ground.

Dismounting, Laomedon paused for a moment, working out the best way to cross the trench and storm the rampart, bristling as it was with the shafts of overturned carts.

"Ugh, that cursed legacy!" he muttered, meaning Heracles's fortifications, and wondering why he had never yet ordered the trench filled in and the rampart leveled.

From among the carts in the middle, a young, round-faced warrior in an expensive plate cuirass leaned out to the waist.

Laomedon, just in case, took a step toward his armor-bearer, who carried a large oval shield emblazoned with a snarling lion, but the warrior held neither javelin nor bow and arrow.

"I am Oicles of Argos, son of Amphiaraus the Soothsayer," the round-faced man introduced himself, as calmly as if he were a guest and not a raider. "I trust the gods are gracious to you and your city, honored Laomedon?"

"Gracious? To him?!" Beside Oicles, who was grinning from ear to ear, another pirate appeared; the man's gruff voice struck Laomedon as suspiciously familiar. "To this beardless scoundrel? Now there's a thing to say, Oicles..."

The pirate pulled off a bronze horned helmet that had covered almost his whole face, and the words caught in Laomedon's throat: before him stood Iolaus, the driver of Heracles's chariot --- the very man to whom the Trojan ruler, together with the rest of Salmoneus's brethren, had offered the Mycenaean throne no more than three years before.

Laomedon's furious rasp and the almost convulsive wave of his hand were, fortunately, read correctly by the Trojan soldiers, who charged forward with shouts.

Their numerical advantage came to nothing at first: at best only half the force could attack the rampart at once, while the other half pressed up from below, gradually filling the trench and seeing nothing past the backs of their countrymen; the pirate villains, for their part, instantly stopped loading and rained a hail of stones and javelins down on the Trojans --- and the especially spiteful (or long-armed) among them used ship's poles, half again as tall as a man, to knock Laomedon's soldiers down onto the heads of those climbing up behind them.

Neither side had anywhere to retreat: behind the raiders' backs the sea lapped at the shore, and there was no longer time to board the ships and cast off; while the Trojans fighting hand to hand at the carts were themselves pressed from behind by a mass of their brothers-in-arms, eager to add their own bloody share to Ares's sacrifice. In two places the pirates' defense was breached, some of the carts thrown down or dragged aside, and at the points of breakthrough the plate cuirass of Oicles the Argolidian and the horned helmet of Iolaus the driver instantly flickered into view, the fighting boiling around them tenfold. Sooner or later --- Laomedon, watching the battle from the side, saw this clearly --- the pirates would be driven back to the sea and cut down; but the Trojan losses, and the fact that some of the raiders were quite capable of getting one ship away and leaving the rest to their fate, drove Laomedon into a fury.

And so he silently thanked fate --- little disposed to favor Troy that day --- when he saw an imposing detachment of reinforcements running toward the fray from the city walls, from the direction of the hill of Batieia.

* * *

But fate, Ananke the Inevitability, equally indifferent to gratitude and to curses alike, had its own plans for Laomedon of Troy, head of the Brotherhood of Salmoneus since the death of Eurytus, basileus of Oechalia.
