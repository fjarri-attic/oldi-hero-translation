#### 11

Having nearly reached the cave itself --- Hermeias remembered perfectly well where it was, for it was at Chiron's cave that the Family had sworn by the Styx never to cross the border of Pelion without the centaur's leave --- the Trickster suddenly swerved aside and made for the place among the pines where a stream, running along the bottom of a ravine, laughed almost inaudibly.

Later.

He would come to the cave later, once he had regained his former shape; he did not want to appear before Chiron like this, bewildered and unsettled. It ill befits a god to go about with his feelings in disarray, and besides... it was shameful. Shameful, and that was that! He had guided so many souls down to Hades, and it never troubled him, yet now his own soul would not sit still...

Strange, though --- the moment Hermeias admitted to himself that he was ashamed, and not so much before Chiron as before the boys, before the eight-year-old mortal scamps, he felt lighter at once. The pines stopped swaying their branches reproachfully, the bushes stopped poking him with their twigs, the dry needles underfoot rustled far more amiably, and a pretty dryad's little face peeked out of a hollow, winked at Hermeias, and, growing bashful, vanished with a light laugh.

"That's right," the Trickster added, pouring oil on the fire, "a laughingstock is exactly what I am! Take Apollo and Artemis, who shot down a dozen of gossipy Niobe's children, or maybe a full two dozen (who keeps count of the departed?!), and afterward they beamed like the noonday sun, boasting that not one arrow had been wasted! Tantalus, or, say, Procne, Tereus's wife --- those didn't even spare their own children, let alone anyone else's; they slaughtered them like cattle and even cooked a meal out of them afterward... There isn't, there's simply no such goddess as Conscience! There's Ate the Deception, there's Lyssa the Rage, there's even Dike the Justice, if you like --- but no Conscience! Why does everyone else do without one, and I alone have to have it?! What kind of luckless god am I?! Tell anyone that the Trickster's conscience has been tormenting him, and they'll laugh in your face! I stole --- it never troubled me; I lied --- like water off a duck's back; I set one against another --- without batting an eye... I should go bow at Ares's feet: teach me, brother, how to kill!.. Oh, he'll teach me... some tough guy! Always boasting that Hera bore him with his helmet already on! Must be the helmet that pinched his head shut on the way out..."

By the time he came down to the stream, Hermeias had not at once noticed that his fits of self-flagellation had somehow drifted, quite mechanically, into mental flogging of his eternal foe Ares; nor did he notice at once that someone was already sitting by the stream, cloven hooves dangling in the water, thoughtfully scratching the tip of a sharp, hairy ear.

"Hello there, Trickster!" The right hoof churned up water that had been still until then. "What brings you here?"

"Hello," Hermeias replied, sitting down beside him.

The old satyr Silenus, tutor and eternal companion of curly-haired Dionysus, glanced sidelong at the upset god and gave an indeterminate grunt.

The Trickster winced --- Silenus reeked unmistakably of stale wine.

"Now, when I was teaching young Dionysus the difficult art of wine-drinking..." Silenus trailed off without finishing; then, without looking, he reached out and dragged a bulky wineskin out of a pile of dry brushwood.

The wineskin gurgled ominously.

"Give it here," Hermeias ordered, surprising even himself. "Come on, come on, don't be stingy..."

"Don't be stingy, goat-legs," Silenus corrected him instructively, waggling his ears comically. "When you gods are in trouble, you're supposed to be rude and unpleasant."

He didn't hand over the wineskin, though --- just shook it, listening to it gurgle, baring his gap-toothed mouth in a grin.

"What troubles could gods have?" Hermeias shrugged. "But you, goat-legs, are about to have some of your own. On account of your greed. And no Dionysus will help you then. Understand?"

"Understood," the satyr nodded indifferently. "How could I not... Drink, you extortionist! Maybe you'll cheer up --- it's downright unpleasant to look at you like this! You lot raised such a racket over all of Pelion --- Chiron stamping his hooves, that flying fellow waving his caduceus about, the hazel trees crackling, the very stones all but wrenching themselves out of the ground on their own... How could one not drink, after such righteous labors?"

Hermeias, who by then had managed to snatch the wineskin from Silenus and fasten his lips to it greedily, choked and coughed, his face turning crimson.

"Care for a little water to wash it down?" the satyr inquired solicitously. "Wouldn't want you getting sick..."

"You were spying, weren't you?" Hermeias, for whom witnesses were just what his day needed, blurted out.

"No, just a drunken vision, I'm sure! Pelion has been a quiet place since time immemorial, I come here to recuperate" ("From drinking!" Hermeias put in venomously, but it went in one of the satyr's shaggy ears and out the other), "and next thing you know, the mountain nearly gets uprooted! The deaf won't hear it, the blind won't see it... and the foolish won't get it --- if Chiron's calling on Tartarus, then someone's tail got singed. And certainly not Chiron's. Now go on and hand over the wineskin, my throat's gone dry all of a sudden..."

Silenus moistened his dried-out throat long and diligently, smacking his lips and snorting with satisfaction, while Hermeias watched the old satyr and felt the ember of irritation inside him dying down, curling over with grey ash, felt how the wine from Silenus's wineskin fogged his head --- but that was good, because a head like that reasoned far better, and even if it only seemed so to Hermeias, it didn't matter, never mind it at all... and, besides, it would be just the right time to pull off his sandals and dip his bare feet into the cold stream.

"Good sitting here," Silenus said, giving the noticeably lighter wineskin a regretful shake before magnanimously holding it out to the Trickster.

"Good..."

The combination of water cooling his feet and wine warming his heart filled Hermeias with tolerance; and even Silenus's wrinkled face, with its everted lips and cunning, close-set eyes, looked, in that moment, quite... quite passable.

Except that it brought to mind a past that Hermeias did not like disturbing.

"You didn't happen to pass by Dryope, did you?" Hermeias asked Silenus, as if in passing. "How is she --- still blooming?"

The satyr shook his shaggy head no. Maybe he hadn't passed by, or maybe she wasn't blooming... spring had gone, so what was there to bloom for?

* * *

...Ah, how divinely, how godlessly lovely black-eyed Dryope had been, daughter of the Dryopian tribe, a wood-nymph through her mother! How foolish was her mortal husband Andraemon, who failed to prize the treasure entrusted to him! Foolish and cruel was Andraemon; he beat poor Dryope viciously, marring the nymph's lovely face with bruises! Hermeias could not bear it, captivated as he was by the fragile charm of the wretched creature --- the beast Andraemon came to a bad end, caught beneath the blow of the invisible rod-caduceus, and the innocent nymph laughed at her husband's funeral, already carrying Hermeias's child.

By him --- a tireless lover, a great god, the best in the world, the one and only --- Dryope proved inexhaustible, both in love and in singing his praises!

Only upon seeing his newborn son, whom black-eyed Dryope had abandoned indifferently in the forest, without even troubling to wash the infant or wrap him in a blanket --- only then did the Trickster understand why the late Andraemon had beaten his wife the way Zeus once beat the rebelling Titans. He understood, and went down to Hades, found Andraemon's shade there, and generously plied it with the blood of a sacrificial cow, restoring the dead man's memory; and afterward the slain and his slayer, Andraemon and Hermeias the Trickster, Hermeias the Simpleton, Hermeias the Cuckold, talked for a long while...

The son amorous Dryope had borne her husband Andraemon, young Amphissus, took after neither mother nor father (strange that this hadn't interested the Trickster before!), but the son the white-breasted nymph had borne him, Hermeias... that was simply beyond all bounds!

The infant squirmed and whimpered, while the Trickster stared dumbly at his son --- horned, goat-legged, covered in dark, thick fur, sharp-eared, swarthy...

For a year, or two, or more, the wild satyrs hid themselves from the Trickster after that, until Hermeias cooled down and grew calm again, and in the nymph Dryope's place there rose in the forest a tree that bloomed once a year with snow-white flowers, yet bore no fruit --- they say the gods punished insolent Dryope for tearing petals from the sacred lotus... only nobody knows exactly which gods did the punishing, or what the lotus had to do with any of it!

Nothing at all.

And Hermeias carried the infant to show the Family. He stood there as if spat upon, listening to the laughter, enduring the jokes, teeth clenched, and did not fly at Ares when the latter proposed naming the newborn Pan, that is (as Ares pointedly put it) "everybody's."

"Universal," Apollo corrected with a smirk, never fond of Ares's soldierly manners. "The one who pleases everyone... like that mommy of his."

Hermeias swallowed that too (though, true enough, Ares's favorite helmet went missing afterward; they searched and found nothing, and when it did turn up, it was filled to the brim with... "a helmet of plenty," as Momus[^29] the mocker put it); the Trickster held his tongue, waited until father Zeus gave a benevolent nod --- meaning, let the grandsonny live, the Family had no objection --- waited, and carried Pan off to Dionysus to be raised.

[^29]: Momus --- god of mockery and slander, whose wise counsels proved ruinous to whoever heeded them; his epithet was "truthful through lies."

And so little Pan settled into Dionysus's retinue. He grew used to it, grew up, ran about among the satyrs and the wildly drunken maenads; he took shepherds and hunters under his protection, just as Hermeias himself did, and considered every forest his own... goat-legged, goat-horned, shaggy, a merry idler, calling Hermeias "dad" and Bacchus-Dionysus "uncle."

One thing was strange: let Pan frown unexpectedly, let him give so much as a glowering look from under his brows, or start whistling and hooting... and whether you were a man, a god, or a monster, you'd go running --- blindly rushing, choosing no path, not understanding what drove you on, like gadflies driving a horse...

In a word, panic.

Otherwise he turned out fine --- a good god, a proper one, except that he hadn't come out much to look at, but beauty is only skin deep; and not all nymphs are choosy.

To some, a miracle or a monster --- makes no difference.

* * *

...Tossing aside the wineskin, long since empty by now, Hermeias got abruptly to his feet and, swaying, trudged off along the bottom of the ravine. He remembered that he was supposed to tell Chiron something, only he couldn't remember what exactly, and hoped to work it out once he got there.

He had already forgotten about Silenus.

The old satyr, dangling his hooves in the water, watched him go for a long while.

"You're playing with fire, Trickster!" Silenus muttered, barely audible. "With that fire burning inside you... and it burns worse than any of Dias's thunderbolts. There's a mixture in you of the earthly, the divine, the infernal; a stranger on Olympus, not at home in Hades, a guest on Pelion, a spy in Thebes! No wonder now one thing flares up in you, now another!.. But killing children --- that you never could do. Zeus could, Apollo could, Athena, even gentle Aphrodite could --- you alone never learned how. Which means you never will. Well then, I'll tell Pan just that --- it's not for nothing Pan calls you father, not for nothing he nearly tore out Dionysus's throat when he said something ill of Hermeias! He's right, Pan is --- fathers like that are hard to come by..."

The satyr lay back and closed his eyes.
