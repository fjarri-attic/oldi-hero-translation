#### 11

Having nearly reached the cave itself---Hermeias remembered perfectly well where it was, for it was at Cheiron's cave that the Family had sworn by the Styx never to cross the border of Pelion without the centaur's leave---the Trickster suddenly swerved aside and made for the place among the pines where a stream, running along the bottom of a ravine, laughed almost inaudibly.

Later.

He will come to the cave later, once he has regained his composure; he does not want to appear before Cheiron like this, bewildered and unsettled. It ill befits a god to go about with his feelings in disarray, and besides... it's shameful. Shameful, and that's that! He has guided so many souls down to Hades, and it never troubled him, yet now his own soul is restless...

Strange, though---the moment Hermeias admitted to himself that he was ashamed, and ashamed to face not so much Cheiron as the boys, the eight-year-old mortal scamps, he felt lighter at once. The pines stopped swaying their branches reproachfully, the bushes stopped poking him with their twigs, the dry needles underfoot rustled far more amiably, and a pretty dryad's little face peeked out of a hollow, winked at Hermeias, and, growing bashful, vanished with a light laugh.

[That's right,]{.thoughts} the Trickster thought, adding fuel to the fire, [a laughingstock is exactly what I am! Take Apollo and Artemis, who shot down a dozen children of loose-tongued Niobe, or maybe two dozen (who keeps count of the departed?!), and afterward they beamed like the noonday sun, boasting that not one arrow had been wasted! Tantalos, or, say, Prokne, Tereus's wife---those didn't even spare their own children, let alone anyone else's; they slaughtered them like cattle and even cooked a meal out of them afterward... There isn't, there's simply no such goddess as Conscience! There's Ate the Deception, there's Lyssa the Rage, there's even Dike the Justice, if you like---but no Conscience! Why does everyone else do without one, and I alone have to have it?! What kind of luckless god am I?! Tell anyone that the Trickster's conscience has been tormenting him, and they'll laugh in your face! I stole---it never troubled me; I lied---like water off a duck's back; I set people at each other's throats---without batting an eye... I should go bow at Ares's feet: teach me, brother, how to kill!... Oh, he'll teach me... the great warrior! Always boasting that Hera bore him with his helmet already on! Must be the helmet that squashed his little head on the way out...]{.thoughts}

Having come down to the stream, Hermeias did not at once notice that his fits of self-flagellation had somehow drifted, quite mechanically, into a mental flogging of his eternal foe Ares; nor did he notice at once that someone was already sitting by the stream, cloven hooves dangling in the water, thoughtfully scratching the tip of a sharp, hairy ear.

"Hello there, Trickster!" The right hoof churned up water that had been still until then. "What brings you here?"

"Hello," Hermeias replied, sitting down beside him.

The old satyr Silenos, teacher and eternal companion of curly-haired Dionysos, glanced sidelong at the upset god and gave a noncommittal grunt.

The Trickster winced---Silenos reeked unmistakably of stale wine.

"Now, when I was teaching young Dionysos the difficult art of wine-drinking..." Silenos trailed off; then, without looking, he reached out and dragged a bulky wineskin out of a pile of dry brushwood.

The wineskin gurgled ominously.

"Give it here," Hermeias ordered, surprising even himself. "Come on, come on, don't be stingy..."

"Don't be stingy, goat-legs," Silenos corrected him instructively, waggling his ears comically. "When you gods are in trouble, you're supposed to be rude and unpleasant."

He didn't hand over the wineskin, though---just shook it, listening to it gurgle, baring his gap-toothed mouth in a grin.

"What troubles could gods have?" Hermeias shrugged. "But you, goat-legs, are about to have some of your own. On account of your greed. And no Dionysos will help you then. Got it?"

"Got it." The satyr nodded indifferently. "How could I not... Drink, you extortionist! Maybe you'll cheer up---it's downright unpleasant to look at you like this! You lot raised such a racket all over Pelion---Cheiron stamping his hooves, that flying fellow waving his caduceus about, the hazel trees crackling, the very stones all but wrenching themselves out of the ground on their own... How could one not drink, after all that hard work?"

Hermeias, who by then had managed to snatch the wineskin from Silenos and fasten his lips to it greedily, choked and coughed, his face turning crimson.

"Care for a little water to wash it down?" the satyr inquired solicitously. "Wouldn't want you getting sick..."

"You were spying, weren't you?" blurted out Hermeias, for whom witnesses were just what his day needed.

"No, just a drunken vision, I'm sure! Pelion has been a quiet place since time immemorial, I come here to recuperate" ("From drinking!" Hermeias put in venomously, but it went in one of the satyr's shaggy ears and out the other), "and next thing you know, the mountain nearly gets uprooted! Only a deaf man wouldn't hear it, only a blind man wouldn't see it... and only a fool wouldn't get it---if Cheiron's calling on Tartaros, then someone's tail got singed. And certainly not Cheiron's. Now go on and hand over the wineskin, my throat's gone dry all of a sudden..."

Silenos spent a long time diligently wetting his parched throat, smacking his lips and snorting with satisfaction, while Hermeias watched the old satyr and felt the ember of irritation inside him dying down, filming over with gray ash, felt how the wine from Silenos's wineskin fogged his head---but that was good, because a head like that reasoned far better, and even if it only seemed so to Hermeias, it didn't matter, and who cared anyway... and, besides, it would be just the right time to pull off his sandals and dip his bare feet into the cold stream.

"This is the life," Silenos said, giving the noticeably lighter wineskin a regretful shake before magnanimously holding it out to the Trickster.

"That it is..."

The combination of water cooling his feet and wine warming his heart filled Hermeias with tolerance; and even Silenos's wrinkled face, with its blubbery lips and cunning, close-set eyes, looked, in that moment, quite... quite passable.

Except that it brought to mind a past that Hermeias did not like disturbing.

"You didn't happen to pass by Dryope, did you?" Hermeias asked Silenos casually. "How is she---still blooming?"

The satyr shook his shaggy head no. Maybe he hadn't passed by, or maybe she wasn't blooming... spring had gone, so what was there to bloom for?

...Ah, how divinely, how godlessly lovely black-eyed Dryope had been, daughter of the Dryopian tribe, a wood-nymph through her mother! How foolish was her mortal husband Andraimon, who failed to prize the treasure entrusted to him! Foolish and cruel was Andraimon; he beat poor Dryope viciously, marring the nymph's lovely face with bruises! Hermeias could not bear it, captivated as he was by the fragile charm of the wretched creature---the beast Andraimon came to a bad end, getting in the way of a blow from the invisible caduceus rod, and the innocent nymph laughed at her husband's funeral, already carrying Hermeias's child.

By him, a tireless lover, a great god, the best in the world, the one and only---indeed Dryope proved inexhaustible, both in love and in singing his praises!

Only upon seeing his newborn son, whom black-eyed Dryope had abandoned indifferently in the forest, without even troubling to wash the infant or wrap him in a blanket---only then did the Trickster understand why the late Andraimon had beaten his wife the way Zeus once beat the rebelling Titans. He understood, and went down to Hades, found Andraimon's shade there, and generously plied it with the blood of a sacrificial cow, giving the dead man his memory back; and afterward the slain and his slayer, Andraimon and Hermeias the Trickster, Hermeias the Simpleton, Hermeias the Cuckold, talked for a long while...

The son amorous Dryope had borne her husband Andraimon, young Amphissos, took after neither mother nor father (strange that this hadn't interested the Trickster before!), but the son the white-breasted nymph had borne him, Hermeias... that was simply beyond all bounds!

The infant squirmed and whimpered, while the Trickster stared dumbly at his son---horned, goat-legged, covered in dark, thick fur, sharp-eared, swarthy...

For a year, or two, or more, the wild satyrs hid themselves from the Trickster after that, until Hermeias cooled down and grew calm again, and in the nymph Dryope's place there rose in the forest a tree that bloomed once a year with snow-white flowers, yet bore no fruit---they say the gods punished insolent Dryope for tearing petals from the sacred lotus... only nobody knows exactly which gods did the punishing, or what the lotus had to do with any of it!

Nothing at all.

And Hermeias carried the infant to show the Family. He stood there like a whipped cur, listening to the laughter, enduring the jokes, teeth clenched, and did not fly at Ares when the latter proposed naming the newborn Pan, that is (as Ares pointedly put it) "everybody's."

"Universal," Apollo, who had never been fond of Ares's soldierly manners, corrected with a smirk. "The one who pleases everyone... like that mommy of his."

Hermeias swallowed that too (though, true enough, Ares's favorite helmet went missing afterward; they searched and found nothing, and when it did turn up, it was filled to the brim with... "a helmet of plenty," as Momos[\*](#fn-29){.noteref} the mocker put it); the Trickster held his tongue, waited until Zeus the father gave a benevolent nod---meaning, let the little grandson live, the Family has no objection---waited, and carried Pan off to Dionysos to be raised.

And so little Pan settled into Dionysos's retinue. He grew used to it, grew up, ran about among the satyrs and the wildly drunken maenads; he took shepherds and hunters under his protection, just as Hermeias himself did, and considered every forest his own... goat-legged, goat-horned, shaggy, a merry idler, calling Hermeias "dad" and Bakchos-Dionysos "uncle."

One strange thing, though: let Pan frown unexpectedly, let him give so much as a glowering look from under his brows, or start whistling and hooting... and whether you are a man, a god, or a monster, off you run---blindly rushing, heedless of the road, not understanding what drives you on, the way gadflies drive a horse...

In a word, panic.

Otherwise he turned out fine---a good god, a proper one, except that he wasn't much to look at, but beauty is only skin deep; and not all nymphs are choosy.

To some, a marvel or a monster---makes no difference.

...Tossing aside the wineskin, long since empty, Hermeias got abruptly to his feet and, swaying, trudged off along the bottom of the ravine. He remembered that he was supposed to tell Cheiron something, only he couldn't remember what exactly, and hoped to work it out once he got there.

He had already forgotten about Silenos.

The old satyr, dangling his hooves in the water, watched him go for a long while.

"You're playing with fire, Trickster!" Silenos muttered, barely audibly. "With that fire burning inside you... and it burns worse than any of Dias's thunderbolts. There's a mixture in you of the earthly, the divine, the infernal; a stranger on Olympos, not at home in Hades, a guest on Pelion, a spy in Thebes! No wonder first one thing flares up in you, then another!... But killing children---that you never could do. Zeus could, Apollo could, Athena, even gentle Aphrodite could---you alone never learned how. Which means you never will. Well then, I'll tell Pan just that---it's not for nothing Pan calls you father, not for nothing he nearly tore out Dionysos's throat when Dionysos said something ill of Hermeias! Good on you, Pan---fathers like that are hard to come by..."

The satyr lay back and closed his eyes.
